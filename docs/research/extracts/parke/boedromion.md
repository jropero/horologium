### Eleusinian Mysteries (Mysteria)
**Day**: Multi-day festival; content here covers days 5/procession through day 8. "The sixth and final day of the festival took place entirely in Eleusis itself." Day 8 is the Plemochoai ceremony. Festival conclusion with dedicating garments follows.
**Deity**: Demeter (goddess of corn); the Twin Goddesses (Demeter and Persephone); other deities receive sacrifices also.

**Ritual** (from the point the text begins — the procession to Eleusis is already underway):
- The priests and Mystai and Mystagogoi were enveloped in an armed escort and led "with dignity and without disturbance to Eleusis and back." [Context from p. 69 top; appears to describe an earlier procession day]
- Day 6, at Eleusis: sacrifices were made to the Twin Goddesses and other deities
- A vast offering of meal to Demeter was made, known as the *Pelanos*; on one occasion 21 *medimnoi* less three *choinices* of corn were used (attested by inscription)
- The Eumolpidai were to expound the sacred law determining what offerings were to be made from the *Pelanos*
- The spiritual preparation for the rite of initiation included physical fasting; the Mystai imitated the mourning Demeter fasting at the loss of Persephone
- The Mystai drank the *kykeon* (a mixture of water, flour, and the herb pennyroyal) to break the fast; this gesture of acceptance by Demeter established the *kykeon* as the proper drink for initiates before the ceremony (precedent: the Homeric Hymn describes Metaneira offering wine, refused by mourning Demeter, who commanded the *kykeon* mixture instead, drinking it "for the sake of the rite")
- The main ceremony took place in the Telesterion (Hall of Initiation) at Eleusis: a building approximately 150 feet square, intended for congregational worship under cover; in its final form it may have held ten thousand worshippers; it grew through earlier stages, each time enlarged
- At the centre of the Telesterion was the Anaktoron, a walled-off holy of holies which only the Hierophantes ("The revealer of the Holy Things") might enter
- After a period of darkness the Hierophantes appeared "in a brilliant light" and revealed the *Hiera* (Holy Things)
- The ultimate point of the Mysteries was "the sharing of a visual exhibition which as a result of all that the initiates had undergone was high of emotional content"
- The highest degree of initiation was the *epoptai* ("those who gazed at something")
- Day 7–8 night: rite of initiation lasting through the night
- Day 8 — *Plemochoai*: named after a vessel shaped like a spinning top with a firm base; initiates took a pair of such vessels, filled them with water, then tipped one toward the east and the other toward the west, uttering a mystic formula; "the words are not recorded"
- Post-festival: initiates dedicated to the Two Goddesses the clothing worn at the time of initiation; garments were stored in a room at Eleusis; alternatively, garments were taken home and cut to make swaddling clothes for infants (the notion: consecrated cloth could protect the child at the dangerous period immediately after birth)

**Aition**: The precedent for the *kykeon* is given by the Homeric Hymn to Demeter: Metaneira (queen of Eleusis) offered Demeter a cup of wine, which she refused in her mourning, commanding instead the mixture of water, flour, and pennyroyal, thus setting the precedent for the prohibition of wine in her cult and establishing the *kykeon* as the proper drink for initiates.

**Participants**:
- Mystai (initiates) and Mystagogoi (guides/sponsors of initiates)
- Priests
- Hierophantes (sole person permitted to enter the Anaktoron)
- Eumolpidai (priestly clan who expound the sacred law on offerings)
- Open to Athenians and others ("approached by all manner of persons, Athenian and other"); the Mysteries "survived after the official adoption of Christianity"; Alaric the Goth devastated the site in AD 395

**Sources**:
- Inscription (Pelanos offering: 21 *medimnoi* less three *choinices*); Mylonas cited in interpretation
- Homeric Hymn to Demeter (kykeon precedent)
- Hippolytus: "the Athenians, when they initiate in the *Eleusinia* exhibit in silence to the *epoptai* the mighty and marvellous and most epoptic mystery, an ear of cut-wheat"
- Aphorism attributed to Aristotle: the initiates "rather had something done to them than had received instruction"
- Plutarch: compared the progress of the initiate and the development of the philosopher
- Aristophanes (joke about initiates' clothes being used as swaddling)
- Ancient commentary on Aristophanes (alternative: clothes taken home for swaddling)
- Inscription at Eleusis: a room for storing clothing of initiates
- Plates 27 (the Anaktoron, referenced in text)

**Notes**:
- "Our ancient authorities become vague as we approach the secret part of the ceremonies"
- "The nature of the initiation remains ultimately a secret. The Christian Fathers, who are the only writers claiming to lay bare the mystery, were doing so to discredit it"
- The traditional categories — "things said," "things done," "things revealed" — are not illuminated by sources: "Nothing suggests that the 'things said' were in any real sense the exposition of an esoteric doctrine or the delivery of moral sermons"
- The charge of indecency raised by Christian Fathers is "an obvious charge against everything kept hidden"; Parke considers it unconvincing
- Hippolytus's statement about an ear of wheat is assessed as "not likely that it was such an isolated anti-climax as Hippolytus suggests"
- *Plemochoai* ceremony: "So there is little clue as to the meaning of the ceremony. Taken by itself it perhaps suggests a magic rite meant to encourage rain, and such may have been the original purpose in the ceremonies of the goddess of corn"
- The *epoptai* either "had been allowed to see what the other initiates had not yet seen or had been allowed to have a closer look at the 'Holy Things'"
- Mylonas is cited on the clothing practice but Parke notes the evidence is an indirect joke in Aristophanes; the ancient commentary gives an alternative that Parke presents without adjudicating
- Festival survived more than a thousand years until AD 395

### Boedromia
**Day**: 7 Boedromion
**Deity**: Apollo Boedromios
**Ritual**: A general thanksgiving to Apollo as the god who rescued Athens in war; there is only one reference in ancient literature indicating the form of the celebration, and it mentions a procession. Demosthenes alludes sarcastically to politicians who induce the populace 'to eat out of their hands' by sharing festival funds or mounting a procession at the Boedromia — suggesting popular display but nothing very grand.
**Aition**: The name "Boedromios" is correctly derived from a word meaning "to run to help in response to a shout." The local Attic legend held that the Athenians were at war with the Eleusinians in the reign of Erechtheus; their enemy Eumoipus was winning when Ion came hastily to help the Athenians and brought about victory. In gratitude Ion was made king after Erechtheus, who had fallen in battle. Ion in legend was closely associated with Apollo, sometimes even made his son. So the victory was celebrated as a feast of Apollo.
**Participants**: General public; popular procession implied.
**Sources**: Demosthenes (sarcastic allusion to the procession). Parke notes the legend is "a typical story invented to explain a cult, but it is unnecessary."
**Notes**: Parke considers the festival may have been fixed at this date because September represented the close of the campaigning season, making a thanksgiving for military rescue appropriate. The legend connecting it to Ion or Theseus is secondary and explanatory.

**Parke**: printed p. 53 / PDF p. 51

### Genesia
**Day**: 5 Boedromion
**Deity**: The Athenian dead (public/state commemoration, not private family cult)
**Ritual**: A state celebration in honour of the dead. Jacoby may be right that originally it belonged to the aristocratic clans who may have separately reverenced their dead in private burial grounds on this day. Solon may have converted it into a national celebration. As established by Solon it may have been a kind of Remembrance Day on which the state recognized its debt to the dead, which in early times would be mainly those who fell in battle. The ceremony (best illustrated by Thucydides' account of the setting for Pericles' funeral speech) involved: two days before the ceremony the bones of the fallen are brought out and put in a tent; people make whatever offerings they wish to their own dead; then a funeral procession in which coffins of cedarwood are carried on wagons — one coffin per tribe containing the bones of members of that tribe; one empty bier for the missing; everyone who wishes, citizens and foreigners, can join; the women who are related to the dead make their laments at the tomb; the bones are laid in the public burial place (the most beautiful quarter outside the city walls); a man chosen by the city for his intellectual gifts makes a speech in praise of the dead.
**Aition**: None given explicitly; the festival's origin is in state care for the war dead.
**Participants**: Citizens and foreigners alike; women related to the dead.
**Sources**: Thucydides (account of Pericles' funeral speech as the paradigm example). Philochoros cited by Jacoby (via Parke's note that "its name implies it was a family festival"). Parke, p. 54.
**Notes**: The ceremony described by Thucydides took place at the end of a major war and is not safe to equate exactly with the annual peacetime Genesia, which "will not have had the same material for a public funeral." But "it does express beautifully the spirit of fifth-century Athens in such ceremonial with its balanced association of dignified public mourning and restrained private participation." Apart from the Genesia there is no evidence of a public festival of the Athenian dead — private families went to tombs on anniversaries, but the state was not otherwise concerned except for the cult of heroized dead such as Harmodius and Aristogeiton or the dead at Marathon.

### Artemis Agrotera — Marathon Vow Sacrifice
**Day**: 6 Boedromion
**Deity**: Artemis Agrotera (the Huntress)
**Ritual**: 500 she-goats sacrificed annually, conducted by the Polemarchos (the official responsible for military affairs). The young soldiers in training (Epheboi) paraded under arms and marched in procession to the temple of Artemis. They probably had a share in the ensuing feast.
**Aition**: The battle of Marathon became associated with the 6th of Boedromion, and the anniversary became an important festival. Apollo's birthday and festival day was the 7th; his twin sister Artemis was evidently thought of as being born a day earlier and usually had her festivals on the 6th. Before the battle the Athenians made a vow to the goddess to sacrifice a she-goat for every Persian they killed. Artemis was not normally a goddess of war in Athens, so the vow was probably made because of some connection in date between the campaign and Artemis' traditional festival. Actually in later times it was asserted that the battle itself had taken place on the 6th of Boedromion, but Parke considers this impossible given the Spartans' refusal to march before the full moon of the Carneia had passed — the 6th was more likely the date on which the resolution was passed by the Athenian army to set out from Athens, and the vow was made in anticipation of the battle which did not actually take place till ten days later.
**Participants**: Polemarchos (officiant); Epheboi (young soldiers in training); general public for the feast.
**Sources**: Herodotus (192 Greeks fell; ~6,400 Persians). Xenophon (cited on the difficulty of finding sufficient she-goats). The compromise vow: 500 yearly, still performed in Xenophon's day when the original total of Persian dead had long been passed.
**Notes**: "The goat population of Attica would have been wrecked" if the original vow (one goat per Persian killed) had been fulfilled literally. So 500 yearly was the compromise, converted into a commemorative festival for the victory of Marathon — which also led to the popular but mistaken belief that the 6th was the actual anniversary of the battle. Also there is no evidence the she-goats were burnt as a holocaust; they must have meant a feast of goat flesh for some thousands. The festival was made a military occasion in later times. (Plate 20.)

**Parke**: printed pp. 54–55 / PDF pp. 52–53

### Eleusinian Mysteries — Background and Priesthoods
**Day**: Multi-day festival beginning 15 Boedromion (with preparatory movement of 'Holy Things' on the 13th–14th)
**Deity**: Demeter (the Two Goddesses: Demeter and Persephone); Iacchos (processional personification)

**Background and origins**:
- The Eleusinian Mysteries were the most famous Greek religious festival; they differed from, e.g., the Panathenaia in that participation required initiation and promised spiritual benefit.
- The origin of the cult was probably quite simple and local to Eleusis itself: Demeter was the goddess of the corn crop and was linked at Eleusis with the original royal palace in the manner of other Mycenaean goddesses.
- The legend associated with her told how when her daughter Persephone was carried off to Hades by the King of the Underworld she wandered the earth in grief seeking her and came disguised as an old woman to the town of Eleusis. She was received in a kindly way by the royal family and appointed nurse of the boy prince. Later her divinity was revealed and she commanded the building of a temple in her honour and instituted the Mysteries as an expiatory ceremony. This myth is enshrined in the *Homeric Hymn to Demeter*, probably to be dated to the latter half of the seventh century BC. At the time it was composed Eleusis was still a community independent of Athens with a little port in the Bay of Salamis.
- When Eleusis fell under the control of Athens (late seventh century), Athens took over the festival. The form in the classical period was a combination of the cults of Eleusis and Athens. The organization of the festival was controlled by the King Archon.
- In the classical period, the Great Mysteries of Eleusis were not the first stage in initiation. This was properly begun at the Lesser Mysteries held at Agrai some seven months previously in Anthesterion. Those who wished to be initiated at Eleusis should first have received initiation at Agrai. Also there was a further stage of higher initiation at Eleusis itself — those who had taken part in the rites in one September could return next year or later and be advanced to the final stage (*epoptai*).

**Priesthoods**:
- **King Archon**: organized the festival.
- **Hierophantes** ("Revealer of the Holy Things"): chief priest; appointed from the Eumolpidai; in the Roman period his personal name was subject to a taboo and must not be spoken or written in his lifetime. He wore elaborate robes; priests at festivals wore elaborate robes compared to the dignified costume Aeschylus invented for his kings in tragedy. He was drugged at the time of the Mysteries to ensure his bodily chastity.
- **Daiduchos** ("Torch-holder"): second rank; hereditary in the family of the Kerykes (family names of Callias and Hipponicus); they seem to have been an Athenian not an Eleusinian clan. The Daiduchos wore a beard and long hair confined in a special headband, marking out the effect of royalty. Story: on the field of Marathon a Persian seeking mercy mistook Callias the Daiduchos for a king because of his long hair and headband; Callias showed him a pit full of gold, then slew him to conceal the evidence — not necessarily to be believed but illustrates the Daiduchos's garb.
- **Sacred Herald**: drawn from the clan of the Kerykes, charged in later times with the formal proclamation.
- **Eumolpidai**: claimed descent from the kings of Eleusis; controlled the hereditary Hierophantes post.
- **Kerykes**: alternated the family names of Callias and Hipponicus; supplied the Daiduchos.
- **Iacchagogos**: special priest of Iacchos who escorted the Iacchos image in the procession (Day 5).
- **Phaidryntes** (Cleaner): minor official at the Eleusinion.

**Sources**: Aeschylus (said to have been prosecuted for profanation of the Mysteries); inscriptions; Andocides (trial for impiety); Parke pp. 56–58.

**Notes**: The Athenian state won a place for itself in the pattern of the Eleusinian Mysteries by establishing a local cult of Demeter (called simply 'The Mother') at Agrai on the Ilissos — this had its own Mysteries which included the worship of Persephone, held in the springtime in Anthesterion. When Eleusis was incorporated in Attica the relation between these two cult centres was worked out on the basis that the Mysteries at Agrai were the Lesser Mysteries where initiation must be sought before applying to the Greater Mysteries of Eleusis. (Plates 21, 28.)

### Eleusinian Mysteries — Day 0 Preparations: Transfer of the Holy Things (13–14 Boedromion)
**Day**: 13 Boedromion (departure from Eleusis); 14 Boedromion (escort to Athens)
**Ritual**:
- Two days before the 15th (i.e., on the 13th), young men on military training (Epheboi) went out to Eleusis to escort the 'Holy Things' to Athens.
- The 'Holy Things' (*Hiera*) had to be shielded from the sight of the uninitiated and so were brought out in round boxes (*kistai*) tied with purple ribbons. (In 322 BC when Athens had to submit for the first time to a Macedonian garrison, it was recognized as an omen of this disaster that the dye on the ribbons had turned out a sickly hue.)
- The 'Holy Things' themselves must have been quite small as the boxes containing them could be carried by the priestesses. Mylonas has conjectured that they were the appurtenances of a Mycenaean palace shrine which had survived in Eleusis through the Dark Ages.
- The priestesses and the *kistai* were conveyed in a wagon paid for by the state. About half-way to Athens they had to cross the streams known as Rheitoi. About 421 BC the Athenians built a bridge over them, using the stones left over from the temples at Eleusis demolished by the Persians, but the decree authorizing the work specified that the bridge was to be only 5 feet wide and was not for carts, so that the priestesses could better carry the 'Holy Things' over the bridge. They must have dismounted from the wagon, which went round by the ford to pick them up again on the other side of the streams.
- On arrival at the Eleusinion in Athens, the minor official known as the Cleaner (*Phaidryntes*) of the Two Goddesses went to the Acropolis and reported to the priestess of Athena that the 'Holy Things' and their escort had arrived. By this piece of etiquette the senior importance of the city's goddess over all other divinities was maintained in dignity.
- The conveying of the 'Holy Things' to Athens was a high state occasion but did not yet involve the participation of those about to be initiated.

**Sources**: Decree on the bridge over Rheitoi (421 BC, cited); inscriptions; Mylonas (on the kistai and Mycenaean origin). Parke, p. 59.

**Notes**: The purple ribbons on the kistai were a recognized omen-vehicle; their colour change in 322 BC was widely noted.

**Parke**: printed p. 59 / PDF p. 57

### Eleusinian Mysteries — Day 1: Agyrmos / The Gathering (15 Boedromion)
**Day**: 15 Boedromion
**Ritual**:
- The first day of the Mysteries fell on the 15th of Boedromion. The full moon in this lunar month would make an auspicious beginning.
- The day was known as *Agyrmos* ("the Gathering"). It was so named because those seeking initiation were required to assemble in the Agora at the Painted Portico (*Stoa Poikile*), where in the presence of the Hierophantes and the Daiduchos a formal proclamation was delivered.
- In later times there was a special official, the Sacred Herald drawn from the clan of the Kerykes, charged with this duty.
- The proclamation invited candidates for initiation to declare themselves and also contained prohibitions against those who could not take part without the guilt of sacrilege. The postulants were to be of pure hands, particularly in respect of blood-guilt; but also the Herald specified more spiritual fitness: the would-be initiate must have 'a soul conscious of no evil and have lived well and justly.'
- There was no distinction of age or sex; free men and slaves were in theory equally entitled to admission. The only racial exclusion was directed against barbarians (i.e. non-Greeks), but in later times Romans were accepted.
- Registration followed: it is not clear how far there was formal registration of candidates, but it is probable that this was the point where they paid their fees, and if so, some sort of roster was probably prepared.
- Cost of initiation in the late fourth century: a total of 15 drachmas each (estimated as some ten days' wages). This sum was made up of contributions paid to each of the officials responsible. Per inscription (fragmentary, a century and a half earlier, recording the restored regulations after the Persians destroyed the sanctuary): 'the Hieropoios is to receive a half obol a day from each initiate: the priest of Demeter at the Great Mysteries is to receive an obol a day from each initiate.' Other officials listed above also received fees. A footnote added later in a different, less careful hand recorded that 'the priest at the altar and the cleaner of the Two Goddesses and all-holy priest each receive a half obol from each initiate consecrated to the Two Goddesses' — as if overlooked initially and belatedly added. Total received for the Two Goddesses must exceed 15,000 drachmas; from this sum the priestess pays customary expenses, and the priesthood divides the balance.
- Foreign initiates came from all over the Greek world. Already in the fifth century heralds were sent out in advance to all the cities that recognized the festival inviting them to declare a truce of fifty-five days to allow safety of travel to and from Eleusis for the pilgrims.
- Those foreigners who might never have seen Athens before, and had likewise never taken part in an elaborate programme of ritual in which a mistake might cause a serious offence, obviously had to be guided through the routine. For this purpose there were *Mystagogoi* — guides to the initiate who accompanied their charges through the ceremonies. The clans of the Eumolpidai and the Kerykes supplied from their lay members the chief *cicerones*. Regulations specify that the Kerykes and the Eumolpidai are to initiate the new Mystai individually, and if they deal with them in a body they are liable to a fine of 100 drachmas.

**Sources**: Inscriptions (fees and regulations); Andocides (trial for impiety — he belonged to the Kerykes and can tell the jury that in three years after he returned from Cyprus he had 'initiated a Delphian and other guest-friends of mine'). Parke, pp. 60–62.

**Notes**: "As the stress seems to have been placed not so much on foreign birth as on 'intelligible speech', the underlying motive may have been less to exclude the alien than to ensure that those taking part in the Mysteries could understand what was said and, if necessary, respond correctly." The *Agyrmos* may also have made sure that the numbers coming forward for admission would not exceed the space in the Hall of Initiation. Each pilgrim had personal attention throughout; the Mystagogos is likely to have been more in the nature of a father confessor who may for instance have recommended spiritual exercises for the remainder of the day after the meeting in the Agora.

**Parke**: printed pp. 60–62 / PDF pp. 58–60

### Eleusinian Mysteries — Day 2: Halade Mystai / Sea-Bath (16 Boedromion)
**Day**: 16 Boedromion
**Ritual**:
- After the registration came purification.
- The second day of the festival was devoted to this purpose. It was known as 'Seaward, Initiates!' (*Halade Mystai*) — a name which was the command given on the occasion and perhaps also a watchword shouted by those who took part.
- The purification was performed by two methods: a sea-bathe and a sprinkling with pig's blood. The salt sea was a traditional purifier; also traditionally sacrificial pigs were used for atonement. The animal was specially appropriate on this occasion as it was the conventional victim offered to Demeter, perhaps as a natural emblem of fecundity.
- The Mystai set out in carriages and carts to drive the eight miles to the port of the Piraeus or the beach of Phaleron.
- Though they may not have gone in a formal procession, the cavalcade was to some extent organized, for they left the city by one gate; in the third century BC at least there were superintendents arranging the proceedings who were thanked by the state for their services.
- When they reached the coast, they had to walk out into the water carrying a piglet. This animal had been supplied by the authorities as part of the return for the fees paid, but it was the responsibility of the would-be initiate to convey the victim to the sea and purify it as well as himself by immersion in salt water.
- Then when the party had returned to Athens each Mystes sacrificed his pig and was sprinkled with the blood. Not told whether the pig had to be made a holocaust — so it is to be supposed that the evening of the 'Seaward, Initiates!' day was spent in eating a feast of suckling pig.
- Mylonas has suggested that the trains of carriages making for the coast would be more like a holiday outing in modern Athens than a solemn religious rite.
- Incident: In 339 BC a Mystes at one of the creeks in the Peiraeus rashly cut the throat of his piglet while in the water. The blood attracted a shark which attacked the Mystes with fatal results. At the time this was taken as an omen for the destruction of the state.

**Sources**: Parke, pp. 62–63. (Plate 25.)

**Notes**: "Actually it could have its alarming incidents" — the shark story. The whole procedure "even if done for a serious purpose, sounds as if it must have had its more flippant and comical side."

**Parke**: printed pp. 62–63 / PDF pp. 60–61

### Eleusinian Mysteries — Day 3: Hither the Victims (17 Boedromion)
**Day**: 17 Boedromion
**Ritual**:
- The third day is much less noticed in our sources. It was probably called 'Hither the victims' (*Deuro hai hiereia*).
- If so, this was the date of the main state sacrifice in Athens for the Two Goddesses. (It was usual as part of the secrecy connected with the festival to treat the names of Demeter and Persephone as too sacred to utter and to substitute for them this paraphrase.)
- Also presumably the private persons who could afford to show their piety in that manner would make their own personal offerings.

**Sources**: Parke, p. 63 (the name 'Hither the victims' is tentative/probable, not certain).

**Notes**: Very little ancient evidence survives for this day.

### Eleusinian Mysteries — Day 4: Indoor Day / Epidauria (18 Boedromion)
**Day**: 18 Boedromion
**Ritual**:
- The fourth day, following on these rather festive occasions, was evidently intended to bring the Mystai back to the proper solemn attitude, in which they should approach their initiation.
- They were expected to spend the day indoors; an even more unusual restriction than it would appear in modern times. For in ancient Athens much of the male citizen's life was spent outdoors. This quiet time will then have been a suitable preparation for the spiritual experience of the two final days.
- The vacuum created by the retirement of the Mystai from public life was curiously filled by another religious festival in honour of Asclepius, the god of healing — the *Epidauria*.
- The cult of Asclepius had been introduced to Athens from Epidaurus, its chief centre in southern Greece, in 420/19 BC. Until that date healing in Athens seems to have been mainly the concern of local hero shrines. The year 420/19 was particularly appropriate as during the interruption of the Peloponnesian War caused by the Peace of Nicias, Epidaurus was open to friendly relations with Athens.
- A fragmentary chronicle inscribed in the early fourth century recorded that the god had 'come at the time of the Great Mysteries to the Eleusinion [in Athens] and sent for his snake from home and brought it thither on a chariot when they were met by Telemachus' (the Athenian who was responsible for establishing the cult in Attica). 'Hygieia [Asclepius' daughter, the goddess of Health] came with him and thus there was founded all this sanctuary in the archonship of Astyphilos' (420/19 BC).
- The worship of Asclepius must have been introduced with the approval and active co-operation of the priesthood of the Eleusinian Mysteries. Otherwise the god would not have put up on arrival at the sanctuary of the Eleusinian Demeter in Athens. The sacred snake often shown with him in art was one of the features frequently mentioned in references to Epidaurus; one of the tame specimens from the sanctuary there must have been sent over.
- The advent of Asclepius was worked up into a myth which told that in his own lifetime on earth Asclepius had come to Athens to seek initiation but had arrived too late for the preliminary ceremonies, so that they had all to be rushed through again for his benefit on this, the fourth day. His privilege provided a mythical justification for any other would-be initiates who had started belatedly on the ceremonies.
- The occasion was known as the *Epidauria* and continued to be celebrated in the middle of the older festival of the Eleusinian Mysteries.
- The usual women carrying offerings and a banquet was spread for the god with a couch on which he was supposed to recline.

**Sources**: Fragmentary chronicle (early fourth century); inscription (archonship of Astyphilos = 420/19 BC). Parke, pp. 63–65. (Plate 29.)

**Notes**: Asclepius had a second annual festival, the *Asclepieia*, which was closely linked in date to another important older festival, the City Dionysia. They were neatly spaced at six-monthly intervals. "The whole arrangement suggests the conscious planning of a careful priesthood working in harmony with the authorities of Athens."

**Parke**: printed pp. 63–65 / PDF pp. 61–63

### Eleusinian Mysteries — Day 5: The Great Procession to Eleusis / Iacchos (19 Boedromion)
**Day**: 19 Boedromion
**Ritual**:
- The fifth day of the Eleusinian festival reached the climax. The 'Holy Things' of Demeter were escorted back to Eleusis by the procession of the Mystai going to receive initiation.
- The 'Holy Things' had been stored in the Eleusinion, on the northern slope under the Acropolis, and for the return to their home they were accompanied by another sacred object — the image of Iacchos. He was represented as a young man bearing a torch. In origin he seems to have been simply a personification of the triumphant shout (*iacche*) uttered by those in the procession, just as Hymenaios appears to be a personification of the wedding song. He gradually developed into a young male attendant on the Two Goddesses and became more and more assimilated to Dionysus.
- The procession probably formed up near the Dipylon Gate as the shrine of Iacchos was somewhere in the humbler quarter of Athens near there.
- Young military escort of Epheboi accompanied the march and all the officials of the Eleusinian cult will have had their places. Iacchos was escorted by a special priest of his own called the *Iacchagogos* (the Leader of Iacchos). (Plates 24, 30.)
- The would-be initiates and their Mystagogoi formed the core of the procession.
- Those taking part wore garlands of myrtle and also often carried objects connected with the rite, known as *bacchoi* — branches of myrtle tied together with strands of wool, frequently shown in artistic representation of the Mysteries. They also often carried a stick from which a bundle of provisions hung. This equivalent of the medieval pilgrim's scrip was no doubt needed in the next two days when the Eleusinians must have been quite incapable of catering for the vast influx of worshippers.
- Besides the priests and the band of would-be initiates with their companions, many other Athenians who had been initiated in the past joined the pilgrimage.
- The distance from Athens to Eleusis was 14 miles, and most of the pilgrims traversed it on foot with the help of donkeys or mules to carry their luggage or give them a ride at intervals.
- The procession was no doubt interspersed with bands of flute and harp players and choirs of singers, and the mixed company will have joined in the choruses of hymns or in shouts of '*iacche*'.
- The sacred route was traditional and passed a number of religious sites, with halts for ritual observances and refreshment. Leaving Athens at the Dipylon Gate they passed the Gymnasium of Academus; then as they climbed the pass over Mount Aegaleus they reached the sanctuary of the Pythian Apollo where now stands the famous Byzantine church of Daphni; and as they descended to the shore of the Bay of Eleusis there was a sanctuary of Aphrodite.
- Beyond this they passed over the bridge at the streams of Rheitoi. They had reached at this point the original territory of Eleusis. The Krokidai family (claiming descent from the legendary Krokos) bound the right hand and the left leg of each Mystes with a yellow woollen thread. The rite presumably took place at this point. The real meaning of the custom is buried in the myth, for the alleged ancestor Krokos derives his name from the yellow woollen thread (*kroke*); it suggests some magic purpose in protecting the initiates.
- Further out on the Eleusinian plains the Mystai crossed another bridge, this time over the local river named Kephisos. It was the scene of a traditional custom (the *gephyrismos*) which may have been religious in origin but obviously gave scope for human entertainment. Men with heads covered to conceal their identity sat on the bridge and hurled ribald insults at distinguished persons in the procession — impossible to decide how far this had a magic intention to forestall any ill-luck, or again how far it was just a traditional opportunity for popular licence.
- At last after dark the procession reached Eleusis itself, the final stages of the pilgrimage being completed by torch-light.

**Sources**: Parke, pp. 65–66. (Plates 24, 30.)

**Notes**: Iacchos "could not be truly said that Dionysus penetrated into the cult of Demeter and Persephone at Eleusis, but the great procession from Athens became as much a triumphant march of Iacchos as a return of the Goddesses' 'Holy Things'." The *gephyrismos* at the Kephisos bridge is impossible to evaluate: "It is impossible to decide how far this had a magic intention to forestall any ill-luck, or again how far it was just a traditional opportunity for popular licence of this sort."
