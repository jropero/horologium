### Hephaisteia

**Day**: Uncertain (no month or day assigned; treated as "Other Festival")
**Deity**: Hephaestus (god of fire used in handicrafts)
**Ritual**:
- Torch-race run on the same lines as the Prometheia
- 200 Athenians chosen from the Epheboi (youths beginning military service), arranged in teams each assigned in turn to one bull
- Extraordinary practice of "lifting" the bulls (also attested at the Herakleia and the Proerosía)
- Three bulls assigned to the resident aliens (Metoikoi), who receive the flesh raw; citizens receive a much larger number (Parke conjectures a minimum of ten bullocks with teams of twenty each)
- Large and popular celebration

**Aition**: None discussed explicitly.
**Participants**: 200 Epheboi; Metoikoi (three bulls assigned to them); citizens (majority of victims). Non-citizens were a prominent fraction of worshippers (consistent with Hephaestus as deity of handicrafts, where non-citizens dominated).
**Sources**: Inscription dated to 421/20 BC (fragmentary); general knowledge of the Prometheia for comparison. Mommsen cited for the point that citizens received the larger share.
**Notes**:
- The inscription has sometimes been interpreted as ordering the institution of the festival, but Parke regards it as more plausibly establishing a major celebration every fourth year.
- The inscription does not state the total number of victims.
- Content on printed p. 172 begins mid-sentence (continuation from printed p. 171 = PDF p. 169, not read); the Hephaisteia section begins on a page not included in this extraction.

**Parke**: printed p. 172 (tail only) / PDF p. 170

### Pan — torch-race and sanctuary

**Day**: Not recorded. Parke conjectures the 9th of the lunar month, probably Boedromion, as the anniversary of the god's epiphany.
**Deity**: Pan
**Ritual**:
- Sanctuary established in a cave under the Acropolis
- Annual sacrifices at the sanctuary
- Annual torch-race, finishing (Parke surmises) at the altar of Pan under the Acropolis cliff

**Aition**: Parke explicitly discusses the aition. Herodotus records that Philippides (the long-distance runner carrying news of the Persian landing at Marathon to Sparta) heard Pan calling him by name while crossing Mount Parthenion in the Peloponnese. Pan told the Athenians had neglected him despite his past goodwill and his future support. Philippides reported this; after the war the Athenians established the sanctuary and annual rites. Parke notes the story is probably founded on contemporary sources; Philippides' experience (a hallucination or supernatural companion under severe physical stress) is treated as sincere.
**Participants**: Not specified beyond "the Athenians" in general.
**Sources**: Herodotus (origin of the sanctuary and torch-race).
**Notes**:
- Up to 490 BC, Pan was an Arcadian god of goatherds; the Athenian cult was a new fifth-century introduction.
- Date chosen for the festival is not recorded; Parke's suggestion of 9th of Boedromion is conjectural.
- The torch-race course is not recorded.

**Parke**: printed p. 172–173 / PDF p. 170–171

### Anakia (Festival of the Dioscuri)

**Day**: Unknown; no date preserved.
**Deity**: Dioscuri (Castor and Polydeuces), addressed under the title **Anakes** ("kings") — a title not used for them elsewhere.
**Ritual**:
- Sacrifice described as a **trittya** — a triple offering meaning three different species of victim offered on the one occasion.
- No other ritual details known.

**Aition**: None discussed.
**Participants**: Community-level worship rather than individuals, though Nilsson notes they were less an object of worship by the community than by individuals needing their help. By the late 5th century their function as twin stars to protect seafarers was prominent (fragmentary inscription on the Anakia refers to nautical subjects).
**Sources**: A late source (unnamed) recording the trittya. A fragmentary inscription from the late fifth century on the subject of the Anakia with nautical references. Nilsson cited for the point about individual versus communal worship.
**Notes**:
- The Athenian myths were not particularly favourable to the Dioscuri: Theseus had carried off the youthful Helen before her marriage to Menelaus and hidden her at Aphidnai; her brothers came on an expedition to rescue her.
- A late commentator tried to explain the title Anakes as meaning "prudent" in relation to their behaviour when invading Attica — Parke regards this as a rationalisation; the cult had no original connection with this myth.
- No date, month, or further ritual details are known.

**Parke**: printed p. 173 / PDF p. 171

### Epikleidia

**Day**: Unknown.
**Deity**: Demeter
**Ritual**: Unknown; only a lexicographical reference survives.

**Aition**: None discussed.
**Participants**: Unknown.
**Sources**: A lexicographical reference (the feast is described as "a feast of Demeter at Athens").
**Notes**:
- The name may have a philological connection with the word for "key" (κλείς); keys were traditionally associated with the Mysteries.
- Nothing more can be said about it.

**Parke**: printed p. 173 / PDF p. 171

### Galaxia

**Day**: Unknown; Deubner conjectured Elaphebolion, but Parke rejects this.
**Deity**: Meter (Mother of the Gods / Cybele)
**Ritual**:
- A feast at which they boil the *galaxia*: a porridge made from barley cooked in milk.

**Aition**: None discussed.
**Participants**: Unspecified.
**Sources**: A lexicon (quoted verbatim by Parke: "a feast at Athens, held in honour of the mother of the Gods, on which they boil the galaxia: it is a porridge made from barley cooked in milk").
**Notes**:
- Deubner conjectured the feast took place in Elaphebolion, arguing from the coincidence that in Delos a month named Galaxion fell at that point in the calendar.
- Parke explicitly dismisses this: "Such synchronisms cannot be regarded as anything more than supposition."

**Parke**: printed p. 173–174 / PDF p. 171–172

### Theoinia

**Day**: Unknown ("when they were held is not recorded").
**Deity**: Uncertain. Possibly connected with Dionysus (Aeschylus addressed Dionysus as "Father Theoinos, God of wine, leader of the team of maenads" in a lost play; this title may be related). Parke says the festival seems only to have been known to ancient commentators through a legal dispute.
**Ritual**:
- Basileus and his wife, together with the Gerarai, took part in the ceremonies.
- No further ritual details preserved.

**Aition**: None discussed.
**Participants**: Gerarai (the group of women associated with the Anthesteria); Basileus and his wife; women devotees appear to have been prominent.
**Sources**: The oath sworn by the Gerarai at the Anthesteria, which included a pledge to celebrate "the Theoinia and the Iobaccheia in the ancestral manner and at the appropriate times". A legal dispute in the 4th century BC between two related clans at Eleusis (the Krokonidai and the Koironidai) — this is how the festival comes to be known. Aeschylus (lost play, quoted indirectly through later commentators). One reference to a "Theoinion" (sanctuary).
**Notes**:
- Parke treats the Iobaccheia (attested as a formal religious guild, the Iobacchoi, from the 2nd century AD) as a related but distinct observance.
- The Theoinia was probably in origin a festival at Eleusis managed by local families (Krokonidai and Koironidai) that was later taken over to some extent by the Athenian state along with the major cults of Eleusis.
- The Iobacchoi inscription (recording rites at what Dörpfeld identified as the former temple of Dionysus in the Marshes) is an example of a religious guild of late Roman times and casts no direct light on the classical festival.
- Parke explicitly flags uncertainty: "What relation they bore to the classical festival of the Iobaccheia is quite uncertain, and their rites and ceremonies are an example of the working of a religious guild of late Roman times."

**Parke**: printed p. 174 / PDF p. 172

### 3rd Skiraphorion — Erchia deme (Greater Demarchia)

**Day**: 3rd of Skiraphorion
**Deities**: Athena; Zeus; Poseidon; Aglauros and Erse (joint); Kourotrophos ("Nurturer of the Young")
**Ritual**:
- Ewe described as "in place of a cow" offered for Athena
- Ram each for Zeus and Poseidon
- Probably a ewe for Aglauros and Erse
- Pig for Kourotrophos
- Parke identifies this as the patronal festival of the deme ("what one could describe as the patronal festival of the *deme*")
- The pattern reproduces the worship on the Acropolis at Athens and must have been modelled on it

**Aition**: None.
**Participants**: Men of the deme of Erchia (demotai).
**Sources**: The Greater Demarchia inscription (marble stele of ~60 lines, discovered in the 1940s at the village of Spata, ~20 km east of Athens; described as the principal sacrificial calendar of the deme under the *demarchos*).
**Notes**:
- Parke flags that the Erchia calendar is impressive and comprehensive but by no means complete.
- The association of Kourotrophos with this complex of deities (Athena, Zeus, Poseidon) is consistent with the Acropolis worship pattern.
- The deme festivals were scheduled to avoid clashing with the great City festivals.

**Parke**: printed p. 179 / PDF p. 177

### Skiraphorion (before the Skira) — Marathon Tetrapolis

**Day**: Sometime in Skiraphorion before the Skira; exact day not recorded.
**Deities**: Hythenion; Kourotrophos; Tritopatreis; Untiring Heroes (Akamantes)
**Ritual**:
- To Hythenion: "the seasonal offering" (presumably ripe corn) + a sheep at twelve drachmas
- To Kourotrophos: a pig at three drachmas
- To the Tritopatreis: a sheep
- To the Untiring Heroes (Akamantes): a sheep at twelve drachmas
- Priests allowed a fee of two drachmas on each of the last three sacrifices

**Aition**: None.
**Participants**: Demarch of Marathon (organising the sacrifices); priests of the relevant cults.
**Sources**: Calendar of the Marathon Tetrapolis (a league of four demes: Marathon and three neighbouring communities). Only parts of the calendar survive in fragmentary form, set up in the first half of the 4th century.
**Notes**:
- Hythenion is described as a "seasonal offering" (presumably ripe corn) — Parke's gloss.
- These are described as offerings to "various minor deities" made before the Skira, apparently as a preliminary service acknowledging the importance of the city festival without requiring direct community participation in it.
- Parke notes the men of the Tetrapolis were presumably free to join as individuals in the Skira at Athens after performing these preliminary rites.

**Parke**: printed p. 181 / PDF p. 179
