### Dipolieia

**Day**: 14th of Skiraphorion
**Deity**: Zeus Polieus (Zeus "as god of the city")
**Ritual**:
- Ceremony takes place on the Acropolis
- An altar of Zeus Polieus is laden with barley mixed with wheat, left unguarded
- An ox, kept prepared for the sacrifice, goes to the altar and touches/eats the grain
- A priest called the Bull-slayer (Bouphonos) kills the ox with a pole-axe
- The Bull-slayer throws down the pole-axe and runs away in flight — "this is their traditional practice"
- Others, acting as though they do not know who did the deed, bring the pole-axe to trial at the Prytaneion
- The trial of the axe takes place every year (attested in a speech of Aeschines)
- By the time of Aristophanes the festival was regarded as utterly antiquated and out of touch with contemporary thought

**Aition**: Pausanias records the traditional explanation: the first ox had been offered in sacrifice under King Erechtheus, and the historic Bouphonia was a repetition of that event. A separate myth relates that in the contest between Athena and Poseidon for the lordship of Athens, Athena appealed to Zeus to vote for her and promised in return that the first victim would be offered at his altar as Polieus. Parke notes neither aition has strong historical credibility; the ritual's blood-guilt motif was probably worked up in the 7th century BCE when Dracontic concepts of blood-guilt won fuller acceptance.
**Participants**: A hereditary official priest (the Bull-slayer, possibly hereditarily from the aristocratic Thaulonidai family per Porphyry's account); others present at the sacrifice; officials at the Prytaneion for the trial of the axe. Deubner's view is that the killing was not done by the regular priest but by heralds selected from those present.
**Sources**: Pausanias (description of the Acropolis and the Prytaneion); Aristophanes, *The Clouds* (archaic quality of the festival); Aeschines (speech mentioning the annual trial); Porphyry (fuller account including the *Kentriadai* — the Prickers — who drove oxen to the bronze table with sacred cakes; the ox that tasted the cakes was slaughtered; the hide was sewn up and stuffed and yoked to a plough); Theophrastus (probable source for Porphyry's account, from a work used by Theophrastus, *On Piety*); Deubner (argued Zeus on Acropolis is very ancient and Athena was his predecessor as Mycenaean palace goddess)
**Notes**:
- The sub-ceremony called the **Bouphonia** is included in the list of out-of-date practices cited by Aristophanes; Parke treats it as the particular recorded ceremony of the Dipolieia
- The name *Bouphonia* "ominously meant the slaying of an ox and applied the same word for killing the animal as would ordinarily have been used for the killing of a man" — the act carried moral overtones, hence the elaborate guilt-transfer
- Pausanias gives only a simplified account; Porphyry (via Theophrastus) gives a longer, more elaborate version with discrepancies: (a) in Pausanias the human agents flee; in Porphyry they stand their ground and pass on responsibility one to another until the knife, not the pole-axe, is finally convicted; (b) Porphyry adds the restoration of the ox — hide stuffed, yoked to a plough — which Parke notes is inconsistent with the rest of the Bouphonia (the pole-axe trial would be pointless if the ox recovered)
- Parke discusses but cannot resolve whether Pausanias or Porphyry is more correct
- Deubner's theory: the bull was a yoke-ox, not an ordinary herd animal; the *Bouphonia* fell at harvest-time, marking the end of the ox's agricultural labours; killing a yoke-ox required ritual justification, supplied by the animal's own act of eating sacred grain
- Anthropological theories reviewed and largely rejected: totemism (no evidence the bull was treated as totemic in Attica); analogy with hunters placating prey spirits (does not apply to a domestic animal); Stengel's theory (bull as substitute for human victim, legal transfer of guilt surviving from human sacrifice era) — Parke calls this "an arbitrary guess"
- The discrepancies between Porphyry's and Pausanias' narratives have "given rise to much debate" (Parke's phrase)
- Porphyry's source also names the first ox-slayer as a resident alien named Sopatros who fled to Crete

### Diisoteria

**Day**: Uncertain (towards the end of Skiraphorion; Parke says "towards the end of the same month" after the Dipolieia on the 14th)
**Deity**: Zeus Soter / Zeus the Saviour; Athena the Saviour associated in the same cult
**Ritual**:
- Procession through the streets of the Piraeus, with a maiden carrying a basket
- Sacrifices to Zeus and Athena
- Sacrifices also to other deities worshipped in the Piraeus, including Asclepius
- A couch and a table furnished for the gods to banquet (*theoxenia*)
- Regatta: Epheboi held a regatta and raced round the cape to the harbour of Munichia (parallel to the local festival of Artemis)
- Victims were bulls, sacrificed in large numbers; fourth-century accounts attest this scale
- The state reimbursed those who undertook the expense of erecting and equipping the altar of Zeus Soter annually (documented in a story about Demosthenes)

**Aition**: Parke does not explicitly discuss an aition, but notes the possibility that the original foundation of the Piraeus sanctuary was after the Persian Wars, on the recovery of the port from the enemy. The title "Saviour" as usually applied to Greek gods carries a specific military-rescue meaning, which supports this conjecture.
**Participants**: The Piraeus community generally; Epheboi in military training (at least in the Hellenistic period); multiple deities' cults represented. A maiden carried the basket in procession.
**Sources**: Hellenistic inscriptions (frequently record the festival; does not appear much in classical literature); Demosthenes story (fourth-century context, large-scale sacrifice); Parke notes it was "under the management of the archon" as a more recently founded cult
**Notes**:
- Parke calls it the probable "biggest annual occasion in the Peiraeus"
- Not found in classical literature; frequently recorded in Hellenistic inscriptions — suggests it may not have been older than the foundation of the Piraeus (492 BCE) and its temple with surrounding colonnades
- Athena the Saviour associated with Zeus the Saviour "in worship there" at the Piraeus

**Parke**: printed p. 167–168 / PDF p. 165–166

### Year-end sacrifice to Zeus the Saviour

**Day**: Last day of Skiraphorion (last day of the Attic year)
**Deity**: Zeus Soter / Zeus the Saviour
**Ritual**:
- Sacrifice performed in Athens (not the Piraeus), probably at the statue of Zeus in the Agora in front of the colonnade named after him (as Deubner suggests)
- City magistrates and the council took part
- Led by the King Archon
- Served as an introductory service to the new year (when a fresh set of magistrates and a fresh council took office)
- Also a thanksgiving for preservation of the state through the past year and a supplication for protection in the year to come
- Law-courts closed for the day (but Parke says this was because all presiding magistrates would otherwise be engaged at the sacrifice, not because it was a general public holiday)

**Participants**: City magistrates; the council; King Archon leading; not a festival in which citizens generally took part ("the Greeks generally did not take much notice of the passing of the year as a personal event" — Parke)
**Sources**: Lysias, speech dated to 382 BCE (earliest reference; the orator calls it an "ancestral sacrifice"); Parke notes it need not have been established before the founding of the Cleisthenic council (end of the 6th century) or perhaps even later
**Notes**:
- Parke emphasises this was "the only Athenian festival to mark the end of the old year and the beginning of the new" but that it naturally concerned officials due to leave or take up office more than ordinary citizens
- The festival was an institutional rather than popular occasion

**Parke**: printed p. 168–169 / PDF p. 166–167

### Thargelia

**Day**: 6th and 7th Thargelion (two-day combined festival)
**Deity**: Apollo (main deity honoured at Athens); Artemis co-honoured (6th was her day by Delian tradition)
**Ritual**:
- Day 6 (purification day): Two *Pharmakoi* were chosen each year — ugly, poor men fed at state expense for some time beforehand. One represented the men of Athens, one the women. On the festival day they were led out, one wearing a string of black figs around his neck, the other white figs. After a procession around the city they were beaten with branches of figs and squills and driven out. This was the scapegoat rite to cleanse the whole community.
- Day 6 also: a ram was offered on the Acropolis to Demeter Chloe ("goddess of the green shoots"), a rite Deubner sees as directed toward winning her blessing before the harvest.
- Day 7 (offerings day): A *thargelos* — a pot of all kinds of corn and vegetables boiled together — was offered as first-fruits to Apollo. This was the central act that gave the festival its name.
- Day 7 also: choral and musical *agones* (competitions). The archon organised choirs of men and boys (ten tribes grouped in pairs to form five choirs of men and five of boys, each fifty singers); victors received a tripod dedicated at the Pythion (Apollo's temple near the Ilissos).
- The *Phratriai* (religious family groupings) also recognised the Thargelia as a day for sacrifice and festival; fathers presented newly adopted or natural sons at the *Phratria* altar on this day.

**Aition**: The two-day structure follows the pattern in the *Iliad* (1.314): when the Greek camp was struck by Apollo's plague, the remedy was to clean themselves, throw off-scourings into the sea, and then offer hecatombs — purification on day 1, offerings on day 2. The Thargelia was arranged on exactly this pattern. The Delians held that Artemis was born on the 6th and Apollo on the 7th Thargelion.
**Participants**: Two selected *Pharmakoi* (ugly, poor men); the archon (responsible for organising the choral competition); the ten tribes (paired for the competitions); the *Phratriai*; priestesses associated with Demeter Chloe.
**Sources**: *Iliad* 1.314 (plague-purification pattern); Hipponax (6th-century satiric poet, attests the Pharmakos rite at Ephesus); Plato's *Republic* / Isaeus (Phratria custom of presenting sons); ancient lexicon sources on dates. Note 187–194 in Parke.
**Notes**:
- Parke notes the *thargelos* pot resembles the ceremony at the *Pyanepsia* seven months earlier — both involve cooking all kinds of vegetables for a god, with Apollo as god of fertility of vegetation (not just flocks or athletics as in literary tradition).
- Deubner is cited (with approval) that the *Eiresione* branch is NOT part of the Thargelia proper; the *eiresione* belongs to the Pyanepsia.
- The *Pharmakoi* custom was widespread across Ionian states, not an exclusively Attic development. By the classical period the custom had become a term of comic abuse and was regarded as vulgar by the more serious-minded.
- Apollo is described as a relative newcomer to Athens (probably arriving from Asia via Delos in the 8th century BCE), taking over fertility functions previously belonging to Demeter. The Thargelia was already practised in Ionia; Athens adapted it.

### Bendideia

**Day**: 19th Thargelion (and night following)
**Deity**: Bendis, Thracian goddess identified with Artemis
**Ritual**:
- Two processions converged on the sanctuary of Bendis at Munichia (hill near the Artemis temple, same road): one Athenian procession starting from the Prytaneion in Athens; one separately organised Thracian procession.
- Devotees who had marched over six miles from Athens were provided with sponges, basins, water, and garlands on arrival for a wash-up before the lunch served in the precinct.
- After the interval and lunch, an equestrian torch relay-race took place after dusk — a novelty for Greece (torch-races were normally run on foot; the Thracians introduced horse-back racing).
- An all-night festival followed the torch race.

**Aition**: The cult was officially founded in 429 BCE with Athenian state sanction. Thrace (homeland of Bendis) was strategically important to Athens; Sitalces, king of the Thracian tribes, was treated as a friend and ally. There was a Thracian resident community in the Peiraeus (metoikoi). The Athenian state gave this foreign community the right to acquire land and found a sanctuary. Delphi was unavailable (Apollo hostile to Athens during the Peloponnesian War), so the oracle of Zeus at Dodona was consulted instead and gave a favourable response.
**Participants**: Thracian residents (*metoikoi*) in the Peiraeus; native Athenian worshippers; two guilds (one Thracian, one Athenian) organised the cult; equestrian teams (young men, naked) competed in the torch race; the Athenian presiding committee (the office of the presiding committee of the state) led the official procession.
**Sources**: Plato, *Republic* Book I opening (Socrates and Adeimantus attend the first performance; the torch race on horseback described in dialogue); a marble relief from the Peiraeus (mid-4th century BCE) showing the winning team presenting torches to the goddess; a regulation (extant) on provisions for the Athenian procession. Notes 195–198.
**Notes**:
- Parke identifies the sanctuary's location on the hill of Munichia near the temple of Artemis as reflecting the Greek practice of identifying foreign deities with Hellenic counterparts (Bendis ≈ Artemis).
- The all-night festival which followed the race is never described in surviving sources. Cotyto, the other Thracian goddess, was notorious for wildly orgiastic nocturnal rites, but Parke explicitly notes Bendis was not accepted in Athens (though apparently in Corinth) and the rites of Bendis probably did not differ greatly from other Athenian all-night festivals.
- Modern scholars have suggested the choice of late May for the Bendideia was because it approximated the Attic harvest, and Bendis may have been looked to for general blessing and protection.
- The festival is dated exactly because the *Republic* was set on the first occasion it was held.

### Kallynteria

**Day**: Around 20th–25th Thargelion (exact date uncertain; one late lexicon says 19th, which is improbable as it coincides with the Bendideia; Parke places it between the 20th and 25th)
**Deity**: Athena Polias
**Ritual**:
- The name *Kallynteria* means "beautification by sweeping and polishing" — the occasion when the temple of Athena (the Erechtheion) received its special annual clean-up.
- Carried out under the direction of the priestesses by women who also had functions of cleansing to perform.
- Deubner suggests with some plausibility that it was also the day the famous ever-burning lamp of Athena Polias was refilled with olive oil and relit. Athena had always had a lamp burning in her shrine; in the late 5th century when the Erechtheion was rebuilt, Callimachus made a gold lamp that held enough oil to burn day and night for twelve months without refilling. The annual cleaning would be the sensible date to refill it.

**Aition**: None recorded.
**Participants**: Priestesses of Athena; women with cleansing duties.
**Sources**: Late lexicon sources (giving 19th as date, which Parke rejects); Deubner's inference about the lamp. Note 199.
**Notes**: The *Kallynteria* is scarcely mentioned in the ancient sources; the name is the clearest evidence for what it involved.

### Plynteria

**Day**: 25th Thargelion (penultimate day of the month; "better source" gives this date; another source says penultimate day — Parke accepts 25th)
**Deity**: Athena Polias
**Ritual**:
- The *Plynteria* ("Festival of Washing") was the occasion for cleaning the goddess herself, i.e., the ancient wooden cult statue of Athena.
- The **Praxiergidai** genos (a family traditionally entrusted with robing the image) performed the rite. Though the cleaning was a practical business, it was treated by the women as a secret ceremony of great solemnity.
- Procedure: first the statue was stripped of its robes and ornaments. Then it was wrapped up (for it had to be taken out of the temple for cleaning). This stripping was done early on the morning of the 25th with much traditional ceremonial.
- The statue was then carried in procession to the sea at Phaleron for a purificatory bath. Sponging in the temple was apparently not considered sufficiently cleansing; the Greeks recognised salt sea-water (not the nearly-dry Kephisos or Ilissos rivers in May) as the most purifying element.
- In the fourth century the goddess was escorted by the *Epheboi* (young men in military training) to whom it was a ceremonial parade — resembling the procession of the Mysteries in this respect.
- The procession was headed by a woman carrying a basket containing sweetmeats made out of figs (the mythological explanation being that figs were the first food cultivated by man; Deubner's alternative: an offering to Athena for refreshment on the picnic).
- The image was kept wrapped and carried in privacy; it must not be seen by the public (like sacred objects carried in the Mysteries).
- At the sea the image was unrobed and washed by two young women called the *Loutrides* (Bathers or Washers). Athena's *peplos* (robe) was also washed at the same time by a special priestess (the laundress).
- The procession returned by torchlight (torches held by the military escort). The image was unwrapped by the Praxiergidai, re-robed, re-decorated with jewellery, and restored to its place in the shrine.

**Dies nefastus**: The day of the *Plynteria* was treated as highly inauspicious. The fact that the goddess was pre-occupied (her image away at the sea) meant her sanctuaries were kept shut throughout; a barrier of rope was put up across the entrances to prevent intrusion. No Athenian would dare to touch any serious business on that day. Deubner compares the similar practice at the Festival of Pots (*Choes*) in Anthesterion when spirits of harm were about — here on the *Plynteria* the purification of the goddess implied the presence of foul powers being driven away.

**Aition**: The ritual goes back to primitive times when the cult statue was made of wood and was human-size or less; the ceremony evidently continued into the 5th century even after the colossal gold-and-ivory statue by Pheidias had become the chief cult object on the Acropolis. A sadly fragmentary inscription records that at some date in the mid-5th century the Delphic oracle was consulted on the matter: money was to be provided from state funds for robing the image, Apollo replied that the image should be robed, and preliminary sacrifices to the Fates, Zeus Leader of the Fates, and the Earth Goddess should be made. Parke suggests the Pythia had asked to approve some change in the ritual of the *Plynteria* and used the opportunity to recommend some deities of Delphic cult.

**Participants**: Praxiergidai genos; priestess of Athena; two *Loutrides* (young women, Bathers/Washers); a special priestess as laundress (*peplotheke*); the Epheboi (military escort, 4th century onwards); a woman who led the procession carrying the fig basket.
**Sources**: Xenophon (*Hellenica*) — records that in 408 BCE Alcibiades happened to arrive at the Peiraeus on the day of the *Plynteria*; some people took it as an omen of ill for him and the city. Fragmentary Delphic inscription (mid-5th century). Deubner's comparative analysis of rope barrier / dies nefastus. Notes 199–203.
**Notes**:
- The day was the most inauspicious day of the Attic year because the city's protective goddess was temporarily absent and her purification implied the active presence of miasma.
- Deubner's view (that the washing had become less a material act of hygiene than a spiritual act meant to reinforce the goddess's powers) is noted but Parke considers it "probably overstressing the uncanny and magical aspects of the ritual."
- The procession to Phaleron (and back by torchlight) was a considerable journey — Phaleron being the nearest part of the sea to Athens.
- The Xenophon anecdote about Alcibiades provides the clearest ancient attestation that the day was considered unlucky for serious business.
