### Apaturia
**Day**: No fixed calendar date. Each Phratria held it at its own convenience within Pyanepsion. Three consecutive days (Dorpia, Anarhysis, Koureotis) plus sometimes a fourth (Epibda).
**Deity**: Zeus Phratrios and Athena Phratria ("who presided in common over all the activities of the Phratriai"). Dionysus also had a footing in the festival (Wine God accepted into some Phratriai worship, perhaps mid-sixth century).
**Ritual**:
- Day 1 — *Dorpia* ("Supper Eve"): members of the Phratria foregather at a traditional locality for a communal meal together.
- Day 2 — *Anarhysis* ("Sacrificing"; named for "the action of drawing back the neck of the victim to cut its throat"): sacrifices offered.
- Day 3 — *Koureotis* ("Day of Youths", but derivation also implies "day of hair-cutting"): official occasion when new members were introduced to the Phratria. Male infants born since the previous Apaturia registered at this festival; at puberty the boy was introduced a second time for full membership. On each occasion the father provided a victim for sacrifice. Consecration of locks of hair to the gods for successful attainment of manhood also took place on this day. General annual meeting of all members who might otherwise be scattered in different parts of Attica or Athens.
- Day 4 — *Epibda* ("the Following"): day-after day; "notorious for the hang-over which it left." Sometimes counted as part of the festival. "The morning after" sense also implied reckoning/day of accounting.
- Tariff of victims (from inscription of Demotionidai of Decelea): for an infant, the sacrifice was "the Lesser" (*Meion*); priest received a thigh, side, and ear with a fee of half a drachma. For a full-grown son: a thigh, side, ear, flat cake made with a measure of flour, half a measure of wine, and a fee of a silver drachma. Father and family had the remainder for a feast with cakes and wine as the father could afford.
- The father was expected to advertise the next Dorpia five days in advance in writing on a whitened tablet not smaller than a span, so members could attend (per Demotionidai decree).
**Aition**: Myth of Melanthus vs. Xanthus: Athenians and Boeotians at war over a border district; the Athenian general Melanthus challenged the Boeotian king Xanthus to single combat. During the fight, Melanthus called out that it was unfair to come two against one, as he had seen a figure in a black goatskin behind Xanthus. Xanthus looked round; Melanthus stabbed him. The apparition was later explained as the god Dionysus, who thus acquired the epithet Melainaigis ("of the Black Goat's Skin"). The festival of the Apaturia was instituted to celebrate this deception-won victory. Parke notes: "As an explanation of the festival this myth is quite absurd" — the Apaturia was celebrated in all Ionian cities, not just Athens, making an Athenian local-victory aition untenable. The folk-tale motive (St George / Saracen pattern) is noted; 'Melanthus' means 'Dark Coloured' and 'Xanthus' means 'Fair'.
**Participants**: All male members of the Phratriai (hereditary Brotherhood groups linked by descent from a common male ancestor). Fathers bringing infant sons and adolescent sons for registration. Wives of newly married Phratria members: "on marriage members of some Phratriai at least were expected to mark the occasion by a victim at a feast at the next Apaturia." Whether female offspring needed to be registered "is not clear in the same way." The feastings of the Phratriai "will have been male junketings." The priest of the Demotionidai also attended.
**Sources**:
- Inscription of the Demotionidai of Decelea (sacrifice tariff and procedural decree)
- Lysias (orator): defendant's claim to belong to Decelea; barber's shop beside the Hermai where Deceleans foregathered — same word (verb) as in the Demotionidai decree
- Herodotus: uses Apaturia as a test of pure Ionian derivation
- [Parke's footnotes 101–106; ancient sources not all named individually in the text]
**Notes**:
- Whether female offspring were registered is explicitly flagged as unclear.
- The myth (Melanthus/Xanthus) is explicitly called "absurd" by Parke as an aition for an all-Ionian festival.
- Parke offers a "plausible explanation" for Dionysus's connection: in the mid-sixth century Dionysus the Wine God was winning a predominance in Attic cult and must have secured acceptance in some Phratriai worship.
- Different Phratriai celebrated on different dates within Pyanepsion; the spreading assured public life of Attica was not completely interrupted on any one day.
- Conjecture (Parke flags as such): Pyanepsion chosen because male children born after spring marriages would be "just in time to be registered with the Phratriai of their fathers." He notes "this hypothesis cannot be proved by any statistic."
- Epibda may explain why different Phratriai chose different dates: convenient to avoid the public hang-over coinciding.
- The Demotionidai decree was passed eight years after the end of the Peloponnesian War; it required sacrifices for infants and adult children to be made on the altar at Decelea with a fine of 50 drachmas for infringement, with saving clauses for plague or war.

### Chalkeia
**Day**: Last day of Pyanepsion (day 30, "the last day of the month").
**Deity**: Hephaestus (god of smiths) and Athena. Athena's epithets on this occasion: Ergane ("goddess of labour") and Archegetis ("founding goddess"). Parke: "In the classical period the festival was associated both with Hephaestus the god of smiths and with Athena, and modern scholars have argued at length over the question to which deity it was dedicated. In a polytheistic culture this is scarcely relevant."
**Ritual**:
- Sacrifices (evidently performed; no specific details survive beyond the council decree for state funds).
- Procession of craftsmen and metalworkers: Sophocles' fragment suggests "a procession of workers with baskets full of corn to be set up as offerings." Workers attended "with standing corn-baskets and by the anvil with weighty hammer."
- Holiday from toil in the workshops.
- Feast at night on the sacrificial meat.
- On the Acropolis: the priestess of Athena and the Arrephoroi set up the loom on which the *peplos* to be presented at the Panathenaia was woven. Parke: "The Chalkeia was the occasion of a piece of ritual connected with Athena personally."
**Aition**: None discussed by Parke. The name is derived from Greek *chalkos* (copper/bronze), "implying an origin reaching back into the Bronze Age."
**Participants**: Craftsmen and metalworkers ("all the host of handicraftsmen"). City-wide — in 277/6 BC the city council arranged a vote for state funds to pay for the sacrifice, making it an official civic festival. Priestess of Athena and the Arrephoroi (for the loom-setting ritual on the Acropolis). Athena invoked as patron of all handicraft.
**Sources**:
- City council decree of 277/6 BC (vote for state funds for sacrifice at Chalkeia to Athena Archegetis)
- Sophocles: fragment of a lost play with a chorus of workers at the Chalkeia (verbatim quote in Parke: "Step into the road then all the host of handicraftsmen who in honour of the grim-eyed daughter of Zeus, the goddess of labour (Ergane), attend with standing corn-baskets and by the anvil with weighty hammer…") [Plate 34]
- [Source on Arrephoroi and peplos loom implied; footnote 107]
**Notes**:
- Parke explicitly: "There is no evidence for any special features of cult at the Chalkeia." The ritual content is reconstructed from the Sophocles fragment and the council decree.
- Parke on the loom date: "The choice of the last day of Pyanepsion allowed just short of nine months" for the elaborate Panathenaic peplos to be produced. The date may also reflect: (1) practical need for sufficient time; (2) auspicious date for Athena's interest in handicrafts; (3) end of the fine-weather outdoor season — "from that date indoor activity such as weaving would be specially appropriate."
- The festival marks a thematic closure to Pyanepsion: "as a month it is noteworthy that the emphasis of its festivals rested on the act of sowing in the agricultural year" (the Proerosia, the Pyanepsia and the Thesmophoria); "the Oschophoria was related to the wine-harvest… a rather specialized and aristocratic festival ultimately derived from the cult practices of one small clan hailing from Salamis."

### Proerosia
**Day**: 5 Pyanepsion (announced on this day at Eleusis by the Hierophantes and Sacred Herald).
**Deity**: Demeter. Apollo also connected (the Delphic oracle commanded the sacrifice; separate sacrifice to Pythian Apollo at Eleusis two days later, on the 7th).
**Ritual**:
- The Hierophantes and Sacred Herald publicly announced the feast of the Proerosia and were assigned a small sum of money for the expense of the midday meal.
- First-fruits of the crops (tithed at one six-hundredth of barley and one twelve-hundredth of wheat for Attic farmers) were to be delivered at Eleusis by local magistrates (demarchs).
- Similar proportions collected from cities of the Athenian empire. Invitations sent to all Greek cities to contribute voluntarily.
- In later periods a sacrifice of oxen was made, the victims lifted by the Ephebol (as also at the festivals of Hephaestus).
- State ceremony centred on Eleusis. The scene of Euripides' *Suppliants* is set in front of the temple of Demeter at Eleusis — the occasion suited the play's subject (the burial of the Seven Against Thebes, shown at Eleusis in historic times). Prologue spoken by Aithra, mother of Theseus, who addresses Demeter and offers "preliminary sacrifice on behalf of the ploughing of the land."
**Aition**: The legend told that all Greece was smitten with a plague; the Delphic oracle commanded the Athenians to sacrifice to Demeter on behalf of all; in return tithes of crops were to be sent from the rest of Greece to Athens. Parke: "The legend of Apollo's command had probably some basis in fact, even if the pious picture of the whole of Greece or even of the whole world suffering from a plague is much overdrawn." Literary authorities suggest a date in the early sixth century BC; confirmed by archaeological storage buildings of sixth-century date at Eleusis.
**Participants**: State-managed. Attic farmers; cities of the Athenian empire; all Greek cities invited. The Hierophantes and Sacred Herald officiated.
**Sources**:
- Inscription from the period of uncertain peace during the Peloponnesian War (attempt to revive and reorganize the collection of first-fruits)
- Euripides, *Suppliants* (prologue of Aithra)
- [Parke's footnotes 76–77]
**Notes**:
- Parke: "From its absence from comedy and its place in tragedy and in the inscriptional record one can conclude that the Proerosia, though an important official occasion, was not what could be called a popular festival."
- Parke: "To the Athenian it must merely have seemed like something managed by the state to which he had to pay a tithe."
- The comic writers who are a good index of popular interest never mention it.
**Parke**: printed p. 73–75 / PDF p. 71–73

### Pyanepsia
**Day**: 7 Pyanepsion.
**Deity**: Apollo (his special day — the 7th of the month was dedicated to Apollo throughout Attica). At Eleusis the sacrifice was made to Pythian Apollo.
**Ritual**:
- A special dish called *panspermia* ("all seeds") was cooked: "a hotch-potch of the sort which the ancient Greeks called panspermia (all seeds), boiled together in one pot." Leguminous vegetables, beans, and cereals were included.
- A share of the pot was offered to Apollo; the rest was shared by those participating in the festival.
- **Eiresione**: a bough of olive wreathed with wool, decorated with models of a harp, a cup, and a vine-branch made of pastry, as well as actual fruits strung on it — "the result must have been as fanciful a piece of decoration as a Christmas tree." Fully equipped boughs were carried round by a group of boys who called at the doors of houses singing a begging song: *'The eiresione bears figs and rich cakes and honey in a jar and olive oil to anoint yourself with and a cup of mellow wine that you may drink and fall asleep.'* In the Athenian custom the bough was left at the house and allowed to exercise its beneficial influence for the year.
- Appears twice in Aristophanes: in *The Knights* Demos hears cries at the front door about intruders tearing down his *eiresione*; in *Ecclesiazousai* a withered old woman is compared to such a branch when it is faded.
**Aition**: Connected to the return of Theseus from Crete. On the 7th of Pyanepsion the religious rites of that day were all explained by legendary episodes in his home-coming: his companions, having made vows to Apollo at Delos, on landing mixed together what was left of their provisions into one common pot, boiled it, and ate it up together. The Eiresione was similarly linked — Theseus had used such suppliant boughs. Parke: "The origin of the rite is probably to be sought in the season of the year. At the time of sowing a general mixture of all the edible plants to be sown was cooked and offered to the deity."
**Participants**: A popular, widely attended festival. Boys (for the Eiresione procession). The panspermia was shared by all participants.
**Sources**:
- Aristophanes, *The Knights*; *Ecclesiazousai*
- Ancient scholars and Deubner (Deubner compares the Eiresione with May boughs set up on doors in Germany)
- [Parke's footnotes 78–81]
**Notes**:
- Parke: "One may question whether Apollo in particular had been the original god to receive the offering. He was not usually a god of fertility. But he may have attracted the festival to himself, as the 7th of the month — his special day — was a convenient occasion for it."
- Deubner argued the Eiresione was originally a blessing attached to houses, and the song developed later as an accretion. Ancient scholars felt the connection with Apollo needed further explanation, vaguely suggesting it was instituted to appeal to the god for relief from a plague (equating it with suppliant boughs in Sophocles' *Oedipus Tyrannus*).
- The panspermia and Eiresione are described as "very primitive cult practices suitable to seed-time" in contrast to the more sophisticated Oschophoria on the same day.
**Parke**: printed p. 75–77 / PDF p. 73–75

### Oschophoria
**Day**: 7 Pyanepsion (same day as the Pyanepsia).
**Deity**: Athena Skiras (primarily) and Dionysus. Parke: in origin the cult of one small clan (the Salaminians) which became accepted as a general state cult. Deubner argued for Dionysus as the central deity; the 1938 Agora inscription proved the primacy of Athena Skiras.
**Ritual**:
- **Main feature**: a procession from a sanctuary of Dionysus in Athens to the shrine of Athena Skiras at Phaleron. (Parke: "as the ceremony was evidently ancient, it is reasonable to suppose that it was the sanctuary in Limnai, not the theatre of Dionysus Eleuthereus whose cult was a sixth-century BC importation.")
- The objects conveyed (*oschoi*) were vine-branches with their bunches of grapes still growing on them.
- **Oschoporoi**: two young men chosen from those outstanding in birth and wealth led the procession in female robes (not female dress as a disguise but as a religious costume). They headed the procession and were followed by a choir singing special hymns known as "those of the Oschophoria." No specimens of the hymns survive, unlike the Eiresione song.
- **Deipnophoroi** ("Dinner Carriers"): a number of women who carried the other food needed for the meal in addition to the sacrificial meat; after the victims were killed they shared in the banquet. Their presence was unusual, as it was not customary in Athens for wives to accompany their husbands when going out to dinner.
- Legends were told during the banquet at Phaleron, all connected with Theseus.
- **Peculiar ritual features at the sacrifice**: (1) the herald did not wear a garland — instead he wound the garland round his herald's staff; (2) at the conclusion, when the libations had been poured, those present shouted "Eleleu, Iou, Iou" — a combination of a shout of encouragement or triumph and a cry of amazement/confusion.
- The two branches of the Salaminians each received equal halves of the meat and carried it away raw; the god's portion was burnt for Apollo; a banquet followed and the remainder was divided up among families for private dinners.
**Aition**: Entirely connected with the Theseus legend:
- The two young men in female dress were said to recall Theseus' trick on Minos: to make up the required number of seven maidens he had taken five and had dressed two of his male companions as girls. Plutarch: he gave them warm baths and cosmetics, taught them to resemble girls in speech and gesture, and they were not discovered in Crete.
- On the return, Theseus forgot to hoist white sails; King Aegeus watching from the Acropolis believed his son dead and threw himself off the cliff. So when the herald arrived to announce the safe return, the people were in sudden mourning for their king. He accepted garlands but wound them round his staff (unable to wear them given the sad news). The mixture of cries "Eleleu, Iou, Iou" reflected the mingled joy and grief of that day.
- The Deipnophoroi were said to imitate the mothers of the Twice Seven (the youths and maidens sent as tribute to Minos), who came to them at Phaleron before the ship set sail, bringing food and telling tales.
**Participants**: The two Oschoporoi (chosen from well-born and wealthy families; the inscription shows they were appointed by an official nominated by the Salaminians). The Deipnophoroi. A choir. All participants in the sacrificial banquet. The two branches of the Salaminian clan (who managed the festival).
**Sources**:
- Long inscription found in the Athenian Agora, published in 1938 (dispute between two branches of the Salaminians over their mutual rights in managing the festival — proved the origin of the Oschophoria in the cult of the Salaminian clan)
- Plutarch (on the Oschoporoi dressed as girls)
- [Parke's footnotes 82–88]
**Notes**:
- Parke: "These complicated mythological explanations are highly typical of the Greek love for accounting for abnormalities in ritual by such aetiological legends. Even if one were to suppose some kind of historic fact lay behind the legend of Theseus, these particular episodes were simply invented to fit pre-existing religious practices."
- The cult of Athena Skiras was brought by the Salaminians from their original home on Salamis (where the goddess's concerns included the grape harvest) when they were transferred to Attica in the late seventh or early sixth century BC, settling near Sunion and at Phaleron. On Salamis they may have practised a form of Oschophoria; in Attica it was appropriately linked with the local cult of a wine-god by making the procession start from his shrine.
- Parke on the scholarly dispute: "Until 1938 scholars used to devote much space to arguing whether the ceremony of the Oschophoria was consecrated to Dionysus or Athena, and Deubner came down decidedly in favour of Dionysus. He was right in arguing that in essence it was a vintage celebration, falling at the right time of the year and involving the carrying of boughs with grapes at its central ceremony, but he was wrong in refusing to associate these features primarily with Athena Skiras."
- The rules governing the festival in the fourth century BC were defined by an arbitration between the two branches of the Salaminians.
- Parke on the contrast with the Pyanepsia: "The practices of the Oschophoria, much more sophisticated and complicated, were inherently the ritual of a fairly small clan and were run by its aristocracy."

### Theseia
**Day**: 8 Pyanepsion.
**Deity**: Theseus himself (heroic cult). The 8th of the month was traditionally linked with Poseidon (as Theseus was son of Poseidon); the 7th was proper to Apollo, the 6th to Artemis. Plutarch says Theseus was also honoured on other eighths of the month.
**Ritual**:
- Procession and sacrifice; an athletic contest.
- The victims offered were many (late fourth-century accounts), with generous distribution of meat to the populace.
- A special type of porridge made with milk called *athara* was part of the feast.
- Athletic programme resembling the Panathenaia, with somewhat military flavour by the second century BC: prizes for trumpeters, for Manly Excellence, for Good Military Equipment; three classes of torch-race for boys, adolescents (Epheboi), and young men (Neaniskoi) as teams; usual track and field events in various age groups; races in armour and horse-races.
- A detailed record from about 160 BC lists the victors in the different events; competitors seem confined to Athenians except in events where resident aliens could compete.
**Aition**: Theseia was established as a chief state festival in 475 BC when Cimon was equipped with a Delphic oracle commanding him to find and restore Theseus' bones to Athens. Cimon captured the pirate stronghold of Scyros and found the relics, which were ceremonially brought to Athens and deposited in a shrine in the centre of the city near the Agora. The site may have been the site of minor private worship of Theseus before 475 BC by a family called the Phytalidai; at that point it was taken over by the Athenian state, backed by the authority of the Pythian Apollo. No temple was built there, but a major sacred area was laid out.
**Participants**: State festival. Athenians (primarily); some events open to resident aliens. The feast involved a generous public distribution.
**Sources**:
- Detailed victor list from c. 160 BC
- Plutarch (Theseus honoured on eighth of the month)
- [Parke's footnotes 89–91]
**Notes**:
- Parke: "Probably this place had been the site of some minor worship of Theseus before 475 BC which may have been the private privilege of a family called the Phytalidai."
- The festival was appropriate to the 8th because of Poseidon: Theseus as son of Poseidon made the date doubly fitting.
- Parke notes the Theseia had a "somewhat military flavour" by the second century BC; the ordinary citizen by then did not engage in athletic contests, so there were prizes for trumpeters and military equipment as well as heralds.
