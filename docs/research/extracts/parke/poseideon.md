### Poseidea (festival of Poseidon)

**Day**: 8th (traditional day for Poseidon, as 7th belonged to Apollo and 6th to Artemis), but the assignment is uncertain
**Deity**: Poseidon (no epithet given)
**Ritual**:
- No description survives; existence inferred from the name *Poseidea*
- Festival once every fifth year at Sunium in honour of Poseidon included a regatta; whether this fell in the month Poseideon is doubted (Parke: "one may seriously doubt the suggestion, accepted by Deubner, that it took place in this month")

**Participants**: unknown
**Sources**: sacrificial calendars of a local district and a private guild (for day 8); literary sources confirm the Sunium pentaeteris festival but do not assign it to this month
**Notes**:
- The festival name *Poseidea* is "not known to any great extent from our sources"
- Assignment of Poseidea to day 8 rests only on two sacrificial calendars; no literary source confirms it
- Parke expresses explicit doubt that the Sunium pentaeteris (with its regatta) was held in Poseideon given the season's weather; "the organizers would have been asking to have their programme cancelled because of bad weather"
- The month's association with Poseidon may be a secondary rationalisation: the block Maimakterion–Anthesterion belongs to non-Apolline festivals (Parke's two-block theory, pp. 97–98)

### Haloa

**Day**: 26th (Parke: "towards the end of the month, on the 26th")
**Deity**: Demeter (primarily); Dionysus also mentioned as connected (secondarily, and probably intrusively — Parke calls this "probable")
**Ritual**:
1. A secret rite confined entirely to women, held at Eleusis
2. Magistrates (male officials) prepared a rich feast of every sort of food except those forbidden in the Mysteries (pomegranates, apples, eggs, fowls, certain kinds of fish)
3. When the feast was ready the magistrates departed, leaving it to the women
4. Women engaged in uninhibited obscenity of language and mutual abuse
5. Women carried models of male and female genital organs
6. Priestesses went about whispering to individual women (content of whispering not recorded)
7. Phallic objects (clay models) were set up in a field and sprinkled with something, in connection with the new season's growth — attested on a red-figure vase, British Museum (Cook and Deubner's identification)

**Aition**: None given explicitly by Parke. The name *Haloa* was connected by ancient scholars with *halos* (threshing floor) or with gathering/assembly, but Parke rejects all proposed derivations as unconvincing: "The name is best left as unexplained."

**Participants**:
- Women only for the secret rite and banquet; men (including the magistrates who prepared the feast) were explicitly excluded
- Priestesses had an active role (whispering)
- Fourth century BCE onwards, courtesans are repeatedly connected with the festival in literary allusions — Parke notes these associations tend to "connect it with courtesans" but regards the charge against Archias the hierophant as politically motivated and the grounds as "impossible for us to check"

**Sources**:
- Scholiast on Lucian (main description of the secret rite and banquet)
- Red-figure vase, British Museum (ritual scene, identified by A. B. Cook and Deubner)
- Demosthenes [Against Neaira] — charge against Archias; Parke uses it to illustrate the festival's setting
- Inscription honouring Aristophanes of Eleusis (men's separate banquet)

**Notes**:
- The meaning and origin of the name are explicitly left uncertain by Parke ("best left as unexplained")
- Dionysus's presence in the cult is described by Parke as "probable" but "intrusive into what was originally a festival of corn"
- The phallic element is read as a fertility rite to stimulate growth of the seedling corn; Parke connects this with the timing (December / seed season)
- A reference to a procession in honour of Poseidon within the *Haloa* is noted (one inscription); men excluded from the banquet "found a way to assert themselves in worship of a male deity"
- Connection with courtesans (from fourth century BCE onwards) is described as a secondary development; "the tone of the banquet will have suited them perfectly"
- Parke considers Deubner's suggestion that the *Haloa* influenced the development of the *Country Dionysia* of Poseideon

### Country Dionysia (Rural Dionysia / *kata agrous Dionysia*)

**Day**: No fixed date — celebrated "according to the choice of each neighbourhood" in the latter part of Poseideon; no single assigned day
**Deity**: Dionysus (no specific epithet given; described as a primitive Dionysiac fertility festival)
**Ritual**:
1. Procession (*pompe*) in the old patriarchal style: a jar of wine, a vine, someone dragging a he-goat, another carrying a wicker basket of raisins, and above all a phallus
2. Phallic song (*phallikon*) sung by the father leading the procession
3. A daughter or maiden acted as Kanephoros (basket-bearer)
4. A slave (Xanthias in Aristophanes' parody) bore the phallus upright
5. Animal sacrifice (he-goat as the standard victim)
6. Traditional game: *askoliasmos* — a goatskin blown up and greased, contestants jumping on top of it on one leg; also hopping contests and counting jumps
7. Theatrical performances — tragedies and comedies — at local theatres constructed on the lines of the Dionysiac theatre at Athens
8. At the Peiraeus specifically: organized by the Demarchos (not left to local choice, but allotted from a short list by the central government); traditional procession plus formal dramatic performances

**Participants**:
- All members of the township, including slaves ("shared by all, even the slaves")
- Daughter of the household as Kanephoros
- Male head of household led the procession and sang the phallic song
- At the Peiraeus: formal Demarchos oversight; Hellenistic period visit by embassy from Colophon attested

**Sources**:
- Aristophanes, *Acharnians* (main evidence for the procession ritual; the parody of Dikaiopolis is described by Parke as "basically realistic")
- Plutarch (wistful picture of the primitive procession vs. Hellenistic extravagance)
- Timaeus (third-century BC historian: description of Dionysius of Syracuse's celebration with gold jugs)
- Demosthenes [Against Aeschines context] — reference to theatrical performances at local festivals in which Aeschines took part as third actor

**Notes**:
- No single fixed date; Parke explicitly says it "was not tied to a single date in the month"
- The festival is described as "the religious ceremony from which the great Athenian festivals of the *Lenaia* and the *City Dionysia* were derived"
- Parke considers both the *Haloa* and the Country Dionysia to be "evidently in origins fertility festivals" and "predominantly phallic in emphasis"
- The Peiraeus festival is singled out as the one that went beyond purely local celebration: it had a Demarchos rather than local choice, formal tragedies and comedies, and received foreign embassies
- Parke uses Aristophanes' *Acharnians* (produced at the *Lenaia* of 425 BC) extensively but acknowledges it is a comic parody; "the picture of the ceremony was basically realistic and meant to remind his audience of the joys of peace in the country"
- The contrast between old primitive simplicity (Plutarch) and Hellenistic extravagance (Timaeus on Syracuse) is discussed at length
- The *askoliasmos* game is attested for the fifth century too ("just like all the other features which we have mentioned")

## Chapter summary

Poseideon (approximately December) is characterized by Parke as a month with fewer festivals than average, owing to bad weather. Its two main attested festivals are:

1. The **Haloa** (day 26) — women-only fertility rite at Eleusis, honouring Demeter, with secondary Dionysiac and Poseidonian elements
2. The **Country Dionysia** (no fixed day, latter part of month) — the primitive Dionysiac procession-and-sacrifice festival held throughout the Attic townships and derived from, as well as ancestor of, the urban Lenaia and City Dionysia

The **Poseidea** (day 8 candidate) is almost entirely unknown; Parke treats it as a probable but evidentially thin entry, and doubts the Sunium pentaeteris belongs to this month.

Parke's closing note: "Poseideon and the two months that follow are notable for Dionysiac festivals" — a consequence, he argues, of Apollo's legendary winter absence at the Hyperboreans, leaving these months open to other divine patrons.
