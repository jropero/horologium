# Parke — Metageitnion
Source: H. W. Parke, *Festivals of the Athenians* (1977), printed p. 51–52 / PDF p. 49–50
Extracted: 2026-10-25

---

## Chapter summary

Parke opens by stating that Metageitnion "contained no major event." It is the second month of the Attic year, following the Panathenaia month (Hekatombaion). Only two festivals are discussed: a festival of Apollo Metageitnios (name attested, Athenian details absent) and the Herakleia at Kynosarges (the main focus of the chapter).

---

### Metageitnios (festival of Apollo)

**Day**: uncertain (day not given by Parke)
**Deity**: Apollo, cult-epithet Metageitnios
**Ritual**:
- No Athenian ritual details survive in literary or inscriptional sources
- Celebrated as a holy day in various Ionian centres (Miletus; month name attested at Delos, Ephesus, Samos)
**Aition**: Parke notes that the name's derivation "suggesting the idea of 'changing neighbours' is peculiar"; modern scholars prefer to regard it as a festival involving the neighbourhood, but Parke does not give a specific aition narrative
**Participants**: unknown for Athens
**Sources**: none cited explicitly; Parke cites fn. 35 for the Ionian attestations (Miletus, Delos, Ephesus, Samos)
**Notes**:
- The month name itself is the principal evidence that a festival of this name existed; it "was not treated as one of importance in our literary or inscriptional sources" (Parke's words)
- Modern scholarly interpretation (neighbourhood festival) is preferred but not certain
**Parke**: printed p. 51 / PDF p. 49

### Herakleia at Kynosarges

**Day**: uncertain (Parke says the Metageitnion sacrifice "must have been the chief annual feast at Kynosarges" but gives no specific day; the Alcibiades law also stipulated a *monthly* sacrifice)
**Deity**: Heracles (no cult epithet given)
**Ritual**:
1. Annual feast at the gymnasium of Kynosarges during Metageitnion
2. Feasting was a prominent element — Heracles' cult "generally included feasting as a prominent element in the ritual"
3. Twelve members of the club were chosen as *Parasitoi* to guarantee the god's meal at table
4. A law passed by Alcibiades required the priest and the Parasitoi to have a monthly sacrifice; the Parasitoi were to be drawn from the base-born (*nothoi*) and the sons of the base-born, under penalty of being brought to court for refusal
5. The peculiar lifting ritual: rather than pole-axing the victim and dismembering the carcase at the altar in the usual fashion, "the animal was lifted bodily aloft and placed on the altar for its throat to be cut" — Parke calls this "reasonably certain" though "some classical scholars have boggled at the feasibility"
6. The bull may first have been stunned with a mallet or thrown and roped before being lifted
7. Young men volunteered for the lifting; Theophrastus' Late Learner "cannot be prevented from throwing off his coat and joining in"

**Participants**:
- The gymnasium club at Kynosarges was "specially open to residents of non-citizen birth" — specifically children of mixed marriages between an Athenian and a foreign wife (*nothoi*)
- Even the officials of Heracles' cult were drawn from this class
- Twelve *Parasitoi* (selected members) had a formal role at the god's meal — Parke notes this is "probably the earliest use of the word which was to become 'parasite'"
- Young men generally for the lifting of the sacrificial bull
- The Alcibiades law required Parasitoi to be drawn from the base-born and sons of the base-born

**Sources**:
- Theophrastus, *Characters* (the "Late Learner" sketch: "Of course if ever he is invited to a shrine of Heracles, he throws off his coat and lifts the bull so that its neck can be cut")
- A law attributed to Alcibiades (on the Parasitoi and the monthly sacrifice) — cited by Parke; fn. 38 covers the lack of further particulars on the feast itself
- Classical literature generally on the lifting custom (Parke references "a number of references in classical literature" without naming them all; Hephaestus feast comparison cited for scale — "as many as two hundred Athenians were selected to lift the bulls")

**Notes**:
- Parke explicitly flags uncertainty: "we have no further particulars about it" (the chief annual feast in Metageitnion)
- The explanation that the lifting suited Heracles as a god of strength is offered but then qualified: "the occurrence of the custom at other festivals makes this explanation doubtful" — lifting was not unique to the Heracles cult
- The Parasitoi institution is flagged as significant for the history of the word "parasite"
- The Alcibiades law is the only specific document cited for the organisation of this cult
