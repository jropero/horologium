# Parke — Maimakterion
Source: H. W. Parke, *Festivals of the Athenians* (1977), printed p. 95–96 / PDF p. 93–94
Extracted: 2026-10-25

---

## Chapter overview

Parke opens by noting that Maimakterion (the 5th month, corresponding to November) was a poor month for festivals. It "contained no popular feast day at all" and "less than the average number of festivals." The reason was practical: a month of bad weather, with little occasion to go out in the open air, when seafaring was stopped and gatherings in the open air risked being spoiled by rain. Major Greek festivals involved processions and sacrifices that could not be held indoors.

The month's name derives from an epithet of Zeus as god of the sky and weather, meaning "Blustering." The month belonged to him as god of storms, implying there was a festival in it in which prayers were addressed to him to be kind to men.

---

### Maimakteria (implied festival)

**Day**: uncertain (not stated; the existence of a festival is inferred from the month's name)
**Deity**: Zeus Maimaktes ("Blustering")
**Ritual**:
- Prayers addressed to Zeus to be kind to men in his control of the wind's violence
- The literal and physical side of this appeal belonged more to primitive times when storm damage to buildings could have been more serious

**Participants**: Not specified by Parke; priestly officials implied
**Sources**: Footnote 108 (not expanded in the text; reference to the month's name implying the festival)
**Notes**: Parke does not describe the Maimakteria as a distinct festival with known ritual steps — its existence is inferred from the month's name. Nilsson is cited as the scholar who connected it with the *Pompaia* on the basis of the month's association with Zeus. Parke states: "Neither festival is mentioned in popular literature." He does not say the Maimakteria and Pompaia took place on the same day.

**Parke**: printed p. 95 / PDF p. 93

### Pompaia

**Day**: Last third of the month (exact day not given; Parke specifies only "the last third of the month")
**Deity**: Zeus Meilichios ("kindly" or "open to propitiation")
**Ritual**:
1. A sheep was sacrificed to Zeus and its fleece was removed and kept for magic purposes
2. Those being purified were required to stand on the sheepskin with their left foot
3. The sheepskin was regarded as capable of absorbing and retaining the guilty infection of those who stood on it
4. The skin was carried round in a procession — this procession gave the ceremony the name *Pompaia* (from *pompe*, procession)
5. The procession was accompanied by a *caduceus* (the rod with entwined snakes, the special badge of Hermes)

**Aition**: Parke notes a representational parallel: "In one well-known representation such an initiate is shown seated on a sheepskin, and Demeter herself is described in the *Homeric Hymn* as accepting such a chair after her wanderings." He uses this to illustrate the sheepskin's purificatory function, not to state an aition for the Pompaia itself. No foundation myth is discussed.

**Participants**: Priestly officials ("the responsibility of the priestly officials whose duty was to maintain the supernatural safety of the community by such magic rites"); the ordinary Athenian played no part. The Daiduchos at Eleusis is mentioned as having used the same purification method before admission to the Mysteries.

**Sources**:
- Polemon of Ilion (Hellenistic antiquary): dedicated "one whole treatise" to the subject of the Sheepskin of Zeus (*Dion Koidion*)
- *Homeric Hymn* (to Demeter): cited for the sheepskin as a seat of purification
- Footnote 109: cited for the conjecture that the procession made a circuit of the city (ancient authorities do not describe the route)

**Notes**:
- Parke doubts whether Hermes was originally the deity concerned in the festival: "In this context one may doubt whether Hermes as deity was originally concerned in the festival."
- The route of the procession is unknown: "our ancient authorities do not describe the route which it took. One would conjecture a circuit of the city."
- Parke raises the possibility that the sheepskin "really had no connection with Zeus originally, but was associated with him by a process of assimilation."
- He suggests the whole procedure may be apotropaic magic "which may have had no primitive link with the person of any deity, but simply have been directed at controlling the unindividualized forces of nature."
- On the relationship between the *Maimakteria* and the *Pompaia*: Parke follows Nilsson in connecting the two once Maimakterion became associated with Zeus, but explicitly states "we need not suppose that the *Maimakteria* and the *Pompaia* took place on the same day."
- The *Pompaia* of Zeus "provided a picturesque verb with the meaning 'to exorcize'."
