## Overview

The eighth month ANTHESTERION was named after a festival of Dionysus, the *Anthesteria*, which gave the month its name. The word derives from the Greek for flowers (*anthos*) and corresponded to the time of year when the first shoots of blossom appear. Children three years old were crowned with flowers on this day; miniature wine-jars with appropriate scenes were given as presents to children. The festival lasted three days, the 11th, 12th, and 13th of the month. Its two main themes were wine and the spirits of the unseen world.

Parke pp. 107–119 cover the Anthesteria. Pp. 119–122 cover the Diasia. Pp. 122–124 cover the Lesser Mysteries at Agrai.

### Anthesteria — Pithoigia

**Day**: 11 Anthesterion
**Deity**: Dionysus (shrine in the Marshes; sanctuary on the southward side of the Acropolis approximately, most ancient site of Dionysus worship)
**Ritual**:
1. The pithoi (large earthenware fermentation jars in which grape juice had been stored since the vintage) were opened.
2. Samples of the new sweet wine were taken out and carried to the shrine of Dionysus in the Marshes, where the main ceremonies of the Anthesteria were held.
3. At the shrine, wine was brought and mixed with water in the right proportion.
4. The first mixed wine was offered to Dionysus.
5. Afterwards, male worshippers could drink their first tastes of the season's wine.
6. Before drinking they prayed with a libation that their partaking of the new wine would be harmless and actually beneficial to them.
7. At least no further activities are assigned to it beyond drinking for the rest of the day.

**Participants**: Male worshippers
**Sources**: Thucydides (most ancient site of Dionysus worship); general ancient references to the *Pithoigia* (Parke cites fn. 128–129)
**Notes**:
- The site of the sanctuary of Dionysus in the Marshes is uncertain. Dörpfeld excavated a site below the Areiopagus in the last years of the 19th century; this is the only site within the city walls with a good case for identification, but Parke notes two objections. He concludes: "The site of the shrine of Dionysus in the Marshes is best regarded as still uncertain of identification" (fn. 129).
- The sanctuary at the Marshes was opened only once a year: on the 12th (Wine-jars day). The wine offerings to the shrine on the 11th appear to have taken place while the door was still open (new pithoi opened in the course of the day, samples brought at sunset per Greek reckoning) — Parke notes this possible resolution of the apparent contradiction (fn. 129, pp. 110–111).

### Anthesteria — Choes (Feast of the Wine-jars)

**Day**: 12 Anthesterion
**Deity**: Dionysus (shrine in the Marshes; Boukoleion in civic centre)
**Ritual**:
1. **Procession**: The god Dionysus came riding in a ship mounted on wheels (ship-chariot). The procession included Kanephoroi (basket-carriers), sacrificial victims, and young men carrying utensils for the service.
2. Dionysus was probably personified by an actor wearing a mask, accompanied by figures representing satyrs and playing double flutes.
3. The natural supposition is that the god was making his way to the shrine in the Marshes (arriving by sea — a primitive feature, cf. Homer and the *Homeric Hymns*).
4. **Sanctuary opened**: The sanctuary of Dionysus in the Marshes was opened (its only opening of the year).
5. **Basilinna's secret rites**: The wife of the Basileus (King Archon), the Basilinna, performed secret offerings on behalf of the city and saw "what no foreigner could fittingly see." She entered the sanctuary alone among Athenian women. The law governing her qualifications was inscribed on a stone *stele* set up beside the altar in the sanctuary.
6. **The Gerarai** ("the Revered Ones"): Fourteen women who were required to perform ritual functions under the direction of the Basilinna. Equal in number to the altars in the sanctuary, they made simultaneous offerings at each of the fourteen altars. What they offered is not recorded. They were appointed for each occasion by the Basileus. The Basilinna administered an oath to each Gerarai before they started their duties ("I sanctify myself and am pure and holy, from all things which are not purifying and particularly from intercourse with a man, and I shall act as Getara at the *Theoinia* and the *Iobaccheia* in the ancestral fashion and at the appropriate times") beside the baskets at the altar; the Sacred Herald administered the oath.
7. **Hieros Gamos (Holy Marriage)**: The Basilinna was married to Dionysus. No details of the ceremonial are recorded. The ceremony took place not in the shrine at the Marshes but in a building called the Boukoleion beside the Prytaneion in the civic centre. Aristotle noted that this was originally the official building of the Basileus.
8. **Wedding procession**: When the day's ritual at the shrine in the Marshes ended, a wedding procession took the Basilinna (accompanied by the god) to the Boukoleion where the marriage was celebrated at nightfall, as was customary in ordinary Greek marriages.
9. **Private drinking parties**: The evening of the 12th was the traditional occasion to invite friends. The host provided garlands, perfumes, and dessert; each guest brought their own food and their own drink in the form of a wine-jar (*chous*) and a cup. Guests drank in silence — each drank from their own jar, the complete antithesis of the symposium.
10. **Drinking contest**: Whoever drained their jar first was awarded a cake as a prize (at public banquets, a whole wine-skin). The start was signalled by a trumpet. At state banquets the priest of Dionysus presided and had everything prepared — couches, tables, cushions, rugs, garlands, scent, sweetmeats, cakes, and beautiful flute-players.
11. **Garlands ritual**: At the end of the feast each banqueter put their garland round their empty jug and carried it to the shrine of Dionysus in the Marshes where it was handed over to the priestess.

**Aition**: The peculiar customs of the Choes (individual jars, silence, garlands returned to shrine) were explained by the legend of Orestes: arriving at the Anthesteria infected with blood-guilt from murdering his mother Clytemnestra, he had not yet been tried and acquitted at the Areiopagus. To protect citizens from the deadly curse of blood-guilt, the king commanded that all temples be closed and that each guest be furnished with their own jar of wine to drink from, so that Orestes could not enter consecrated ground, contaminate the communal drink, or share the drink of the Athenians. The tradition also attributed to the same king the institution of a drinking contest; the practice of carrying garlands to the shrine rather than offering them to shrines at the end of the feast was attributed to Demophon king of Athens in Orestes' time, seen as a way of getting rid of garlands that had been under the same roof as the murderer (fn. 136, 137, 140).

Parke notes this legend is "fictitious" and "only hints that the underlying motive is that each individual separated himself from contact with his neighbour in what was a communal feast" (pp. 114, 119). He treats the separateness as a possible precaution against dangerous outside influences during the eve of the festival of the ghosts.

**Participants**:
- Basilinna (wife of the Basileus/King Archon): performed the secret rites; required by law to be a virgin before marriage, an Athenian citizen, and not previously married
- Gerarai: fourteen women, described as "solemn and elderly priestesses" (Parke); appointed by the Basileus for each occasion
- Sacred Herald (*hieros kêryx*): administered the oath to the Gerarai beside the baskets at the altar
- Actor impersonating Dionysus (probably the Basileus himself according to Deubner, whom Parke notes; Parke says the text "points to someone impersonating the god in the procession")
- Male worshippers: at the drinking parties and banquets
- Priest of Dionysus: presided at the state banquet

**Sources**:
- [Demosthenes] *Against Neaira* (speech attributed to Demosthenes; prosecutor sets out to create prejudice against Neaira and her daughter): law on the stele in the sanctuary; Basilinna's duties; the Gerarai; oath formula (fns. 132–133)
- Aristotle: Boukoleion was originally the official building of the Basileus (fn. 134)
- Aristophanes, *Acharnians*: Feast of Wine-jars scenes, Dikaiapolis; *Clouds*: Diasia; *Frogs*: garlands/frogs at Choes (fns. 135, 138, 140)
- Timaeus (3rd-century BC historian): account of Dionysius the Elder's elaborate Choes celebration with gold jugs (fn. 138); Xenocrates story (fn. 139)
- Plutarch: story of Timon the misanthrope at the Feast of Wine-jars (fn. 136); *Life of Demetrius*
- Attic vase representations (black-figure, late 6th century): ship-chariot of Dionysus; chous miniature wine-jars (fns. 127, 131)
- Homer and *Homeric Hymns*: Dionysus associated with the sea and seafaring, especially for the Ionians (fn. 131)
- Callimachus: reference suggesting swinging at Anthesteria (fn. 144)
- Deubner: interpretation of vase (skyphos) showing matron/satyr as Basilinna in wedding procession (fn. 144)

**Notes**:
- The *chous* was a full-scale vessel containing ~12 kotylai, just under three quarts (fn. 130).
- Parke notes "A large number of references survive to events which took place during the *Anthesteria*, but some doubts remain on which day they occurred" (p. 109).
- The ship-chariot procession: "There is no reference to this in Attic literature, but representations on Attic vases from the black-figure period of the later sixth century prove its existence by that date at least" (p. 109).
- The *Theoinia* and the *Iobaccheia* mentioned in the oath are obscure. "That these were some rites devoted to Dionysus under these titles is indicated by a few other references, but what they were and when they took place is lost to us" (p. 112).
- The Gerarai: "Certainly one is not led to picture them as maenads indulging in licentious ritual. Instead it suggests solemn and elderly priestesses" (p. 112).
- The Hieros Gamos: "No details of the ceremonial are recorded, as no doubt it was regarded as a sacred mystery" (p. 112). "A *Hieros Gamos* of this sort is not elsewhere found in Athenian ritual, and is rare in Greek religion" (p. 113). Parke considers several explanations — the ceremony as inducement of fertility in the vine; as ceremonial incorporation of Dionysus into the community; as an originally primitive rite.
- The identification of the Boukoleion scene remains "at least doubtful" given vase-scene ambiguity (fn. 144).

### Anthesteria — Chytroi (Feast of the Pots)

**Day**: 13 Anthesterion
**Deity**: Hermes of the Underworld (on behalf of the dead)
**Ritual**:
1. A mixture of all kinds of vegetables was boiled in daytime in a cooking pot (*chytros*).
2. This meal was offered to Hermes of the Underworld on behalf of the dead; no members of the household partook.
3. Individual householders smeared their doorways with pitch from a naive belief that pitch would hinder the entrance of ghosts.
4. Householders chewed buckthorn from daybreak, as it was supposed to have protective qualities.
5. The sanctuaries of Athens were closed.
6. When the day was over, the householder went round shouting: "Get out, Goblins (Keres), the Anthesteria is over!" — an iambic verse of the sort usual in Greek popular sayings.
7. **At the Olympieion**: The state had a special ritual connected with a chasm about a cubit wide in the ground at the site near the Olympieion. Annually, wheaten flour mixed with honey was thrown into this chasm (identified as the gap through which the waters had drained at the end of Deucalion's Flood, and regarded as an entrance to the underworld).
8. **Hydrophoria** ("Water-carrying Festival"): Water was also poured into the chasm — "a feast of mourning at Athens for those who were killed in the Flood, as explained by Apollonius of Acharnai."

**Aition**: The Flood myth (Deucalion). The legend connecting the customs of day 13 with Orestes' blood-guilt is treated by Parke as fictitious (see under Choes above). Parke notes the coincidence of the two festivals (chthonic rites + wine festival) was "probably originally accidental, but that in the course of a long period they became to some extent interpenetrated" (p. 119).

**Participants**:
- Individual householders (privately)
- State (at the Olympieion chasm — public)
- No household members ate the pot-meal

**Sources**:
- Pausanias: chasm at Olympieion, Deucalion's Flood association (fn. 143)
- Apollonius of Acharnai: Hydrophoria, "a feast of mourning at Athens for those who were killed in the Flood" (fn. 143)
- Aristophanes, *Frogs*: frogs reference to the Choes/garlands/revellers on way to priestess past the Marshes (fn. 140)
- General ancient sources on the day of ill omen and spirit-appeasement (fn. 141–142)
- Roman comparison: *Lemuria* (9th, 10th, 11th of May), beans offered, "Out, ghosts!" formula (fn. 142)

**Notes**:
- Parke notes the phrase "Get out, Goblins (*Keres*)" was later rationalised by commentators who changed *Keres* to *Kares* ('Carians') and explained it as a master's dismissal of slaves — Parke judges this "certainly wrong" as an explanation (p. 117).
- The association of the Chytroi with Dionysus (who was not offered the pot-meal — that went to Hermes) is treated as probably accidental conjunction of originally separate traditions (p. 119).
- The swinging custom (girls on swings, linked to the Anthesteria by a Callimachean reference and Attic vase scenes) may belong to the Choes or be connected with the Chytroi; Deubner's argument that swinging was a purification rite is noted by Parke, who treats the vase identification as "at least doubtful" (fn. 144, p. 118–119).

### Diasia

**Day**: 23 Anthesterion (ten days after the Anthesteria — "ten days after the *Anthesteria* there was another religious occasion, the *Diasia*," p. 120)
**Deity**: Zeus Meilichios ("kindly" or "open to propitiation")
**Ritual**:
1. The whole populace made offerings outside the town at the sanctuary near the Ilissos (where Peisistratos was later to found the temple of the Olympian Zeus; in the seventh century "well outside the town").
2. Bloodless offerings of a native kind — not sacrifices. The ancient commentators explained these as cakes made in the shape of animals.
3. Wealthy families could afford to offer whole burnt-offerings of animals (sheep or pig); ordinary families made pastry substitutes in the shape of a sheep or pig.
4. Families assembled for a communal meal (though no good joints were readily available, as the festival was one for whole burnt-offerings; the best an ordinary family could do was purchase offal — such as the maw of a sheep — and make a party-dish of it).
5. General tone of appeasement: solemn and depressing in religious significance, but a popular holiday with presents for children.

**Aition**: None discussed by Parke. The festival's connection with the Cylon affair (632 BC): Thucydides' account shows the *Diasia* was understood as the greatest Attic festival of Zeus, which is why Cylon mistakenly chose the Olympic games rather than the Diasia for his coup attempt — the oracle had told him to seize the Acropolis on the greatest feast of Zeus (pp. 120–121).

**Participants**: All citizens; the whole populace; families with children

**Sources**:
- Thucydides: description of the *Diasia* in the account of Cylon's attempted tyranny; "The *Diasia* is the name of the greatest feast of Zeus Meilichios held outside the town. On it the whole populace make offerings to him, not sacrifices, but bloodless offerings of a native kind" (fn. 145)
- Xenophon, *Anabasis*: testimony of whole burnt-offerings of animals at Diasia by wealthy Athenian families (fn. 147)
- Aristophanes, *Clouds*: Diasia as a popular holiday; Strepsiades reminiscing about his son asking for a toy cart at the Diasia; scene of a haggis bursting (fn. 148)
- Ancient commentators: cakes in shape of animals as substitute offerings (fn. 146)

**Notes**:
- The site of Zeus Meilichios' sanctuary was probably on the banks of the Ilissos near the Olympian Zeus temple location.
- Parke notes the Diasia "had a feeling of appeasement connected with it" like the Feast of Pots, and was "dedicated to Zeus under his title of Meilichios" (p. 120).
- "The general tone of a festival of appeasement and whole burnt-offerings is not likely to have been very cheerful" (p. 122), yet the *Clouds* presents it in contexts that suggest a popular holiday.
- Parke calls the Diasia "a very primitive institution" (p. 120).
- The same month and the same general area (near the Ilissos) also contained the shrine of Zeus Meilichios and the Lesser Mysteries at Agrai — their proximity is noted but not interpreted.

### Lesser Mysteries at Agrai

**Day**: Anthesterion (exact date uncertain; sometime in the month, held in the suburb of Agrai on the Ilissos)
**Deity**: Demeter and Persephone (the Two Goddesses; a local cult of the goddess of fertility at Agrai, later combined with Eleusinian pattern)
**Ritual**:
1. Preliminary initiation rites — required of those intending to be initiated at Eleusis seven months later (though how strictly this rule was applied is impossible to prove).
2. Use of the Ilissos river for ablutions (purification by water); literary references mention the Ilissos for this purpose.
3. Preliminary proceedings similar in structure to the preliminary stages of the Eleusinian initiation.
4. Contents of the Mysteries themselves: never described (kept secret). Reticence in ancient sources expected.
5. Daiduchos (Torch-bearer) officiated — mentioned in connection with Demetrius Poliorcetes' request in 307 BC.

**Aition**: The Mysteries at Agrai were said to have been instituted first for the benefit of Heracles, who wished to be initiated at Eleusis but was inadmissible as a foreigner. The suggestion that the initiation at Agrai was not as old as Eleusis is "no doubt wrong. Each was a local cult of the goddess of fertility and it was only after the political incorporation of Eleusis in Attica that its rites had to be combined with those of Agrai in one pattern" (p. 123). Parke notes: "The legend may stress correctly the relationship of the two festivals in this later period. The Lesser Mysteries as a preliminary to the Greater may well have emphasized the aspect of purification" (p. 123).

**Participants**:
- Prospective Eleusinian initiates (those intending to be initiated at Eleusis in Boedromion)
- Foreigners and others who had missed the February celebration could in Hellenistic times attend a second celebration
- Daiduchos (Torch-bearer) officiated

**Sources**:
- General ancient writers who mention the Agrai Mysteries as a preliminary to Eleusis (imply it was strictly obligatory; fns. 149–151)
- One late literary reference: mentions use of the Ilissos for ablutions (p. 123)
- Heracles story: "One late reference has been taken to indicate that the Lesser Mysteries included dramatic representations of the legend of the Two Goddesses, but the inference is unsafe" (p. 124)
- Carvings showing Heracles' purification from blood-guilt with Demeter and Persephone (fn. 151)
- Episode of Demetrius Poliorcetes (307 BC): Pythodorus the Torch-bearer (*Daiduchos*) protested; Plutarch "was duly shocked at the impious flattery" (fn. 150)
- Mylonas: cited as arguing against identifying purification scenes as showing central acts of the Mysteries (fn. 151)

**Notes**:
- Parke: "The content of the Mysteries at Agrai is never described. This reticence is only to be expected" (p. 124).
- In 307 BC, when Demetrius freed Athens from Cassander, he wrote asking to be admitted to all grades of initiation at once in the month of Munichion (April); a politician proposed renaming the month Anthesterion and holding the Lesser Mysteries for Demetrius' benefit, then renaming it Boedromion for the Greater Mysteries — an episode Parke uses to illustrate that the rule of sequential initiation was not invariable.
- "Agrai remains as obscure as Eleusis and less significant" (p. 124).
- Whether the Mysteries at Agrai were held before or after the Anthesteria proper within the month is not specified by Parke.
