### Procession of Maidens to the Delphinion
**Day**: 6th
**Deity**: Artemis (and Apollo) at the Delphinion — Parke argues the ceremony was "really directed to Artemis rather than to her brother"
**Ritual**:
- Procession of the maidens of Athens to the Delphinion, the shrine on the bank of the Ilissos near the Olympieion
- Girls carried boughs of sacred Athenian olive bound with white wool
- Made supplication at the shrine
**Aition**: Plutarch connects the ceremony with Theseus, who dedicated a suppliant bough to Apollo at the Delphinion before sailing to Crete. Parke rejects this connection as a secondary rationalisation: "Theseus in legend was closely connected with the cult of Apollo, but though Plutarch is not explicit on the point it is much more probable that this ceremony was really directed to Artemis rather than to her brother."
**Participants**: Maidens of Athens; conducted as a women's ceremony
**Sources**: Plutarch (sole ancient source cited by Parke)
**Notes**: Parke notes that the 6th was the traditional date for festivals of Artemis, while the 7th was Apollo's day. He regards the Theseus connection as having "nothing whatever to do with" the original ceremony, which was "simply a general supplication on behalf of the community." Performed by women because the goddess was being invoked as particularly protective of the female sex.

### Munichia
**Day**: 16th
**Deity**: Artemis at the hill of Munichia (inland from the Peiraeus)
**Ritual**:
- Procession on the 16th of Munichion
- Special round cakes called *amphiphontes* ("shining all round") carried in the procession; lighted candles stuck in a circle around each cake
- Cakes also brought privately as offerings to the goddess (in addition to the public procession)
- Philemon's comedy preserves a woman's prayer: "Artemis, dear mistress, to you I carry, lady, this cake shining all round and what is to serve as a drink-offering"
- By the Hellenistic period: Epheboi (young men in military training) held a regatta culminating in a race at sea round the promontory from the harbour of the Peiraeus to the harbour of Munichia
**Aition**: The sacrifice of a she-goat has its own founding legend: a she-bear entered the shrine and was killed by the Athenians; the goddess sent a plague in punishment; the oracle declared that a daughter must be sacrificed to Artemis; a man named Embaros volunteered on condition that the priesthood of the goddess be awarded to him and his family for life; he solved the problem of sacrifice by bringing his daughter to the inner sanctuary, hiding her there, dressing a she-goat in female clothing, and sacrificing it in her place.
**Participants**: Athenians generally; Epheboi (by Hellenistic period) for the regatta
**Sources**: Philemon, *The Poor Girl* (or *The Girl from Rhodes*); Plutarch (for the general character of the Hellenistic festival)
**Notes**: The festival grew in importance after the Peiraeus became Athens' naval base in 492 BC. Parke notes the she-bear motive in the aition connects to Artemis as "mistress of beasts and goddess of the wild, but another element in the festival shows her as moon-goddess." The candles on the cakes are connected by ancient commentators with the moon's circle. Parke explicitly notes the Embaros legend may point to a primitive period when a human sacrifice of a girl was made to Artemis, and raises the question of whether in classical times the she-goat was dressed in female garments or jewellery ("The form of the legend seems to require some such detail to explain its origin"). The Epheboi's regatta had "little to do with Artemis" and was simply convenient for their training exercises.
**Parke**: printed p. 137–139 / PDF p. 135–137

### Arrephoria (discussed within Munichion chapter; date uncertain)
**Day**: Uncertain. A lexicon states the feast to Athena was performed in the month Skiraphorion. Parke notes: "The date on which the ceremony took place is not at all clear." An alternative form *Errephoria* or *Ersephoria* (connected with Erse, goddess of the dew) adds further confusion.
**Deity**: Athena Polias (primary); ceremony linked underground to Aphrodite "in the Gardens" and Eros
**Ritual**:
- Arrephoroi live on the Acropolis near the temple of Athena Polias
- When the feast comes round by night, the priestess of Athena gives them objects to carry on their heads (neither the priestess nor the girls know what these are)
- The girls descend via a natural underground passage to the open-air sanctuary of Aphrodite in the Gardens below the Acropolis
- They leave what they were carrying and take up something else wrapped up, bringing it back
- They are then released and replaced by other girls
- The Arrephoroi also performed a token service of grinding meal for sacred cakes offered to Athena, and carried in baskets on their heads (as kanephoroi when older)
- They wore white robes; if they put on gold ornaments, these became consecrated
- They received a special kind of light bread called *anastatos*
- A ball-court named after the little girls existed on the Acropolis
**Participants**: Arrephoroi — girls between seven and eleven years of age, four chosen annually by the Basileus from aristocratic families, confirmed by vote of the people. Broneer's excavation of the Acropolis confirmed the underground passage and the Aphrodite sanctuary.
**Sources**: Pausanias (detailed description of the nocturnal ritual); Aristophanes, *Lysistrata* (chorus listing female sacral duties: corn-grinder at seven, bear at Brauronia at ten, kanephoros when grown, arrephor); Isocrates' biography (bronze statue of him as a boy hockey-player in the ball-court on the Acropolis); inscription from mid-fifth century found by Broneer in the Aphrodite sanctuary
**Notes**: Parke calls this "a magical fertility ritual: the secrecy, the performance by night and the fact that it is done only by women all point that way." He sees the link between Athena and Aphrodite as primitive: Aphrodite was a female deity of fertility "whether human or vegetable," and the notion of descending with mysterious objects and returning with others parallels the *Thesmophoria*. Nilsson believes the cults became confused; Parke holds they "represent distinct developments of the same primitive ritual." Parke discusses this ceremony in the Munichion chapter as contextual background to Artemis/Brauron material and female cult roles, not because it is specifically a Munichion event. Whether hockey was also played by boys at the Acropolis ball-court "remains unanswered."
**Parke**: printed p. 140–143 / PDF p. 138–141

### Feast of Eros
**Day**: 4th
**Deity**: Eros (worshipped alongside Aphrodite; the 4th was traditionally dedicated to Aphrodite)
**Ritual**: No details of the ritual are available
**Participants**: Unknown
**Sources**: Inscription dating from the mid-fifth century, found by Broneer in the sanctuary of Aphrodite in the Gardens on the Acropolis; no other ancient references to this feast
**Notes**: Parke states: "As there are no other references to this particular feast, there are no details of the ritual available, nor is it explained why the inscription was set up at this particular date in the mid-fifth century." He interprets it as evidence that the cult of Eros was either established at this date in the Aphrodite sanctuary or revived at this period.
**Parke**: printed p. 143 / PDF p. 141

### Olympieia
**Day**: 19th
**Deity**: Zeus Olympios (Olympian Zeus)
**Ritual**:
- Procession in which the cavalry rode past (as the main body of participants)
- Cavalry exercises in the Hippodrome (by Hellenistic period, if not earlier)
- The most important competition: *Anthippasia* ("Riding opposite") — tribal cavalry contingents flee from and pursue each other in turn; after riding through and halting opposite each other, they ride at each other faster at the trumpet signal; after a third charge they bring horses to a halt in line and ride to face the city council
- Large-scale sacrifice: the proper offering to Zeus was a bull; in 334/3 BC official records show 671 drachmas earned from the sale of hides of victims
- The day ended "in a large number of dinners of beef"
- City council seated in some sort of grandstand as official spectators; judges awarded points or deducted them for faults
**Participants**: Athenian cavalry (main active participants); city council (spectators/presiders); judges
**Sources**: Plutarch (describes the scene at Phocion's execution in 318 BC, when the cavalry procession rode past the prison); Xenophon (fourth-century description of the *Anthippasia* with the enthusiasm of a professional cavalryman); official financial record from 334/3 BC (671 drachmas from hides)
**Notes**: Parke considers the festival was "probably instituted by Peisistratos in the latter half of the sixth century when he laid the foundations of the colossal temple near the Ilissos." The temple was designed in the Ionic style to rival Ephesus, Samos and Miletus; work was suspended on the fall of the tyranny and the building remained incomplete until the Emperor Hadrian finished it. The festival continued on the same date, which was probably the anniversary of the foundation. Parke identifies the occasion as "mainly an occasion for the Athenian cavalry." The *Anthippasia* is described by Xenophon as a military training exercise of practical use in battle, not a mere race.
**Parke**: printed p. 144–145 / PDF p. 142–143
