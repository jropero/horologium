### Panathenaia

**Day**: 28 Hecatombaion (for both the annual festival and the Great Panathenaia; not
explicitly restated on pp. 45–50, which are a continuation — the day is given on the
earlier pages outside the read range)

**Deity**: Athena — described on these pages as "city's goddess", "Athena City Goddess",
"Athena, Goddess of Health (Hygieia)" (second sacrifice), and "Athena Victory" (Wingless
Victory / Nike Apteros, third sacrifice point). The epithet *Polias* does not appear on
pp. 45–50.

**Ritual**:
- **Pannychis** (all-night vigil, the eve of the main day): held on the breezy top of the
  Acropolis around the Parthenon; young men sang hymns and maidens performed choral
  dances, beginning at nightfall and running until the procession set out at sunrise.
  Hieropoioi responsible for making it "as possible in honour of the goddess"; anyone who
  failed to send the procession at sunrise was liable to penalties provided by law.
- **Procession** at sunrise: the peplos (woven robe for the cult statue) was presented to
  Athena. [Parke's description of the peplos, the ship-cart, and the procession route is
  on pp. 29–44, outside the read range. These pages only note it in passing as having been
  the other main ritual alongside sacrifice.]
- **Torch race**: single runners (not a relay) competed from the altar of Eros in the
  Academy (outside the Dipylon Gate) to the Acropolis altar of Athena; distance "over two
  miles" with a steep ascent at the end. The winner's torch was used to light the altar
  flame for the sacrifices; runners arriving first or second with extinguished torches did
  not count. An ancient commentator called it "the long race". Mid-4th-century prize list:
  winner received 30 drachmas and a water jug (*hydria*).
- **Sacrifice (main)**: cows purchased from the income of a 41-minas public land-lease
  were driven in procession to the Acropolis and sacrificed on the great altar of Athena
  (in front of the Parthenon), except one animal of the best quality chosen in advance and
  offered separately at the altar of Wingless Victory. The Hieropoioi also made one
  sacrifice to Athena Goddess of Health (Hygieia) and another at "the old temple".
- **Portion distribution (per c. 335 BC decree)**: 5 portions to the Prytaneis; 3 to the
  archons; 1 to the treasurers of the goddess; 1 to the Hieropoioi; 3 to the generals and
  divisional officers; portions to those in the procession and the Kanephoroi "in the usual
  manner" (manner not specified in the decree); remainder of the meat to the Athenians.
- **Meat distribution at Kerameikos**: after the sacrifice on the Acropolis, the meat was
  transported down to the Kerameikos (where the procession had started) and distributed to
  the people by deme, in proportion to the number of each deme's members in the procession.

**Aition**: Parke identifies Peisistratos as the founder of the altar of Eros in the
Academy, and therefore as the likely instigator of the torch-race ritual (since he had
dedicated the altar, the inference is natural). The Academy was already the site of a
torch-race in honour of Prometheus; Peisistratos modelled Athena's race on it. The
underlying ritual idea, Parke suggests, was to bring fresh fire from one sacred site to
another as speedily as possible; the competitive element may also have included sortition
(lots), allowing the gods room for choice.

**Participants**:
- All Athenians, citizens and non-citizens alike; demes represented in proportion to
  members participating in the procession.
- Athenian colonists and Athenians overseas: required by the standard Greek colonial rule
  (not normally involving political allegiance) to send one cow and a suit of armour as an
  offering on each occasion.
- Allied cities under the Athenian empire: from the mid-440s BC, Athens began applying the
  requirement to subject cities; a general order c. 425/4 BC required all ~400 tribute-
  paying communities to send a cow and armour. Parke notes enforcement was uneven and many
  smaller communities probably escaped it.
- Procession officials: Hieropoioi (managers, accountable for both sacrifice and
  Pannychis), Kanephoroi (basket-bearers), Prytaneis, archons, treasurers of the goddess,
  generals and divisional officers.
- "Cattle buyers" (*hieropoioi* specialised subcommission): a state commission responsible
  for buying cattle for sacrifice in consultation with the "cattle buyers"; little mentioned
  in literary sources but attested in the decree.
- Pannychis performers: young men (singing hymns) and singing maidens (choral dances);
  both sexes performed through the night.
- Viewing: no public stands; only one documented private viewing platform, erected by the
  politician Demetrius of Phaleron in the mid-3rd century in the Agora for his Corinthian
  mistress, "higher than the stoa of the Hermai" — noted by Parke as causing indignation.

**Sources**:
- Pausanias — torch-race explanation (single runners, not relay; winner's torch lights the
  altar)
- Aristophanes, *Frogs* — Aeschylus' accusation against Euripides regarding torch-bearers;
  the slow, doubled-up runner from the Kerameikos (anecdote)
- Aristophanes, *Clouds* — meat/stew distribution; scholiast: "because of the abundance of
  meat they all filled themselves, eating beyond what they ought"
- Euripides, *Heraclidai* — Pannychis: "songs of young men and choirs of dancers… on the
  windy mountain ridge the shrill voices of maidens echo to the rhythmic beat all night of
  dancing feet" (chorus dated to early Peloponnesian War)
- Mid-4th-century prize list — records prize of 30 drachmas and a *hydria* for the torch-
  race winner (no olive oil, unlike athletic victors)
- Decree of c. 335 BC (Lycurgus-era reorganisation) — cited by Parke at length; deals
  explicitly with the *annual* Panathenaia but regulations deemed applicable on larger
  scale to the Great festival
- General order of 425/4 BC — requiring all tribute-paying communities to send cow and
  armour
- Plate 19 (visual source: relief frieze showing procession participants, designer omitted
  non-citizens)
- Demetrius of Phaleron — private viewing platform (implicit source; hostile tradition
  reported by Parke)

**Notes**:
- Parke distinguishes the **annual Panathenaia** from the **Great Panathenaia**
  (quadrennial). The c. 335 BC decree concerns the annual festival; Parke infers the Great
  festival operated on the same pattern at larger scale.
- The prize of a *hydria* (not olive oil) for the torch-race winner is explicitly flagged
  as "strange, and unexplained" by Parke.
- The frieze designer's omission of non-citizen participants is noted as a deliberate
  artistic choice, not an accurate representation of who actually took part.
- The vast number of tribute-cities (~400) would have produced a very large procession of
  animals; Parke supposes many smaller communities evaded the requirement through non-
  enforcement.
- The Aristophanes torch-race anecdote (slow man from the Kerameikos) is described by
  Parke as "perhaps not all mischievous fantasy and certainly a corrective to any romantic
  and sentimental notion of classical Greeks who were all perfect athletes."
- The Pannychis as preliminary: Parke argues it was the ritual precursor to the main
  procession — young men and maidens would have been "rather exhausted" by the time the
  procession ended and the meat was distributed at the Kerameikos.
- The "usual manner" for portioning meat to procession participants is not defined in the
  decree; Parke notes this was "evidently something already understood."
- The Decree's preamble is missing; Parke reconstructs the purpose from the preserved
  portions.
- Parke acknowledges that Peisistratos' responsibility for the torch-race is an inference
  ("it is natural to suppose"), not an attested fact.

### Chapter introduction (printed p. 29)

The first month of the Attic year, Hecatombaion, began about midsummer with the new moon before the summer solstice. The name derives from a cult-title of Apollo as the god to whom hecatombs (hundreds of victims) were offered. The festival of the *Hecatombaia* had vanished from importance in Attica before classical times, leaving only the month-name as its survival. Athenians had no state festival for New Year's Day.

Only three festivals occurred in Hecatombaion: the *Panathenaia* (most famous and important), and — among the least renowned — the *Kronia* and the *Synoikia*.

---

### Kronia

**Day**: 12 Hecatombaion

**Deity**: Kronos — originally a god of the grain harvest (reaping hook his symbol); survived chiefly in mythology as the father of Zeus. His place in agricultural worship had gradually been taken by other deities.

**Ritual**:
- A holiday for slaves; they were allowed to dine with their masters.
- Originally a celebration of the end of the farmer's activity: a kind of harvest supper in which all who worked on the farm took part.
- By the classical period, the only survival was a traditional privilege for the slaves (Athens had grown as a city and arable farming had declined).
- There was a sanctuary of Kronos near the temple of Zeus, and presumably an annual sacrifice took place there, but it has left no trace in the records.
- Aristophanes can treat the festival as out of date.
- State business was normally suspended on the day: the *boulē* (state council) was "let go."
- Plutarch (several centuries later): "whenever the servants are having their Kronia banquet – you would not endure their howling and uproar." The master probably appeared for the first course or two and then took an early opportunity to leave the slaves to their for once rather uninhibited enjoyment.

**Aition**: In the days when Kronos was still an important god, the festival marked the final end of the grain harvest. Athenians believed that in earlier times the month had been called "Kronion" and only later Hecatombaion. The name "Kronion" was actually used in Samos and its colonies for the last month of the old year, pointing to the existence of a similar festival in Ionia somewhat earlier in the season.

**Participants**: Slaves and their masters. The day was a holiday for the entire household.

**Sources**:
- Aristophanes — treats the festival as out of date and otherwise unremarkable
- Demosthenes, prosecution of Timocrates — in 353 BC the politician Timocrates arranged for a meeting of the Ecclesia (popular assembly) to be held on the 12th of Hecatombaion; Demosthenes implies this was so as to get through a piece of crooked legislation ("when this was the Kronia and on that account the *boulē* had been let go")
- Plutarch — description of slaves' banquet and masters' attendance

**Notes**:
- By the classical period the Kronia was treated as antiquated and largely ignored.
- Parke describes the masters' attendance at the feast as "probably more than a perfunctory gesture" in earlier times, but by the sophisticated period more so.
- The festival is mentioned only incidentally; no detailed description of it survives.

### Synoikia

**Day**: 16 Hecatombaion (annually); a larger, two-day celebration on 15th and 16th every second year

**Deity**: Athena (state festival *Synoikia* in her honour); also Zeus Phratrios (second day)

**Ritual**:
- A state festival celebrating the *Synoecism* (Synoikismos): the combining into one community of the people of Attica.
- Thucydides mentions it in the place where he is carefully establishing early history from contemporary survivals — he accepts its origin as dating from Theseus.
- Fifth-century code of sacrificial regulations found in the Agora records it among festivals held every second year as the *earliest* in the calendar — sacrifices on the 15th and 16th of Hecatombaion.
- The *annual* Synoikia was on the 16th; every second year a larger and more extended form was held over the two days of the 15th and 16th.
- First day (15th): the third (*trittys*) of the tribe of the Geleontes, described as "those of the white head-bands," had a special right to the sacrifice. Victim: one young ewe.
- Second day (16th): sacrifice offered to Zeus Phratrios (god of the tribal brotherhoods); tribe of the Geleontes mentioned again. Victims: two young bullocks.
- The deme Skambonidai offered a mature victim — presumably a bull — to Athena; "the meat is to be sold raw" (not roasted on the Acropolis and shared among worshippers; the parts reserved for the goddess had been burnt, the rest was disposed of by sale).
- By the fourth century BC another goddess was also worshipped on the same date: in 374 BC an armistice with Sparta was celebrated by the institution of a new cult of Eirene (Peace). The cult was celebrated in the Agora. Four centuries later Plutarch records that the Athenians still sacrifice the *Synoikia* on this date.

**Aition**: The union of Attica was traditionally created by Theseus and should therefore have predated the Trojan War. Modern scholars doubt his historical existence. The festival's origin and its dedication to Athena as "city's goddess" reflected the political union of Attica, but when this resolve was made is not known.

**Participants**: State officials; the Phylobasileīs (kings of the four Ionic tribes, which survived in classical times as archaic religious officials) were closely linked to the celebration — expenses were paid from a fund named after them, and they controlled the shares of the sacrifices. Tribe of the Geleontes had a special role on both days.

**Sources**:
- Thucydides — mentions the festival by name while discussing the foundation of Athens; accepts the Thesean origin
- Fifth-century code of sacrificial regulations (Agora inscription) — records the festival as the earliest in the calendar, every second year on 15th and 16th Hecatombaion
- Further inscription proving the demes had a traditional part in the ceremony (Skambonidai offering to Athena, meat to be sold raw)
- Plutarch — records that sacrifice was still performed on this date centuries later

**Notes**:
- Parke describes the whole picture as "a highly archaic rite involving out-of-date forms of aristocracy." Not likely to have been instituted before the Trojan War, but nothing improbable in supposing it dates from the eighth century BC at least.
- The lack of later references to the feast-day is "sufficient proof that it was not one of popular interest in the classical period."
- The two-yearly vs. annual distinction: Thucydides does not mention a two-yearly celebration; one would naturally expect an annual commemoration. The annual Synoikia was probably always on the 16th; every second year it was extended into a larger form.
- The new cult of Eirene (374 BC) represents the fourth-century trend of personifying and worshipping specific aspects of deities (Eros, Himeros, Pothos, Eirene). The cult image was made by Cephisodotus (father of Praxiteles) — showing Eirene holding the child Ploutos (Wealth). The wars of Athens had led to her impoverishment; the cult may also have had political overtones.

### Panathenaia (opening: origins, athletic games, peplos, ship-cart, procession)

**Day**: 28 Hecatombaion (both annual and Great Panathenaia)

**Deity**: Athena — her birthday; entitled to receive a new dress (*peplos*) periodically

**Background and origins** (printed pp. 33–34):
- "A great contrast in importance was presented by the other festival of Athena held in Hecatombaion." The Panathenaia "truly became the ceremony in which all the inhabitants of Attica and even of the Athenian empire combined to do honour to the great goddess of the city."
- Originally Athena had one day particularly dedicated to her per year; legend identified it as her birthday.
- From early times the day had another significance: Athena was entitled to receive a new dress periodically, and the annual festival was the appropriate occasion for its presentation. This required a suitable procession to bring the dress to the goddess.
- The festival day and presentation of the *peplos* existed at least from the seventh century BC, probably much earlier.
- In 566/5 BC a programme of athletic contests was attached to the religious occasion, matching the contemporary fashion of establishing recurrent series of contests. The actual founder was probably Hippocleides, Athenian nobleman and chief magistrate (archon) in 566/5. Hippocleides was the subject of Herodotus' famous story of the suitor who lost the hand of Agariste (daughter of the tyrant Cleisthenes of Sicyon) because he persisted in performing a dance on his head.
- The occasion was known as a *Great Panathenaia* to distinguish it from the annual ceremony, held every four years like the Olympic and Pythian games.
- Peisistratos developed the festival further: at least the procession had already become elaborate; he also laid down the rules for a competitive recitation of the works of Homer by the rhapsodes — an event that did not occur at Olympia or Delphi and which may have had a permanent effect on the transmission of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*. Various musical contests were also included.

**Athletic and musical programme** (printed pp. 35–37):
- An inscription of the first half of the fourth century BC lists the value of prizes, preserved in two fragments, covering many of the competitions.
- **Musical events** (held in the Odeion, built by Pericles):
  - Rhapsodes (Homer recitation) — prizes lost in the inscription
  - Singers to harp (*Citharoidoi*): victor received crown worth 1000 drachmas and 500 silver drachmas; second a crown worth 700 drachmas and 50 in cash; third 600 drachmas and 10 in cash; fourth 400 drachmas; fifth 100 drachmas
  - Singers to flute: only first and second prizes — crowns worth 300 and 100 drachmas and cash of 50 and 10 drachmas
  - Solo performers on harp and flute: crowns but no cash prizes
  - Musical performers were professionals; the prizes were crowns (wearable at future performances) plus cash
  - Boys competed in singers to flute and solo harp but received plain crowns of olive (of no specific value) rather than recorded prizes
- **Athletic events**: five standard events — foot-race (*stadion*), pentathlon, wrestling, boxing, all-in-wrestling (*pancration*); three classes — boys, beardless youths, men
  - Boy winner of foot-race: 50 jars of olive oil; second: 10
  - Boy winners of other four events: 30 jars each; seconds: 6
  - Beardless youths: *stadion* victor 60 jars; second 12; other events 40 jars; seconds 8
  - Men's prizes unfortunately missing from the inscription
- **Equestrian events**: prizes were jars of olive oil, proportions varying from five-to-one to four-to-one between first and second; horses raced in four-horse chariots, two-horse chariots, ridden singly; races for foals and full-grown horses; special classifications including war horses and a race of pairs; javelin-throwing from horseback
- **Team events** (tribal basis):
  - Pyrrhic dance (three classes — boys, beardless youths, men): traditional dance performed in armour, legendarily first danced by Athena to celebrate defeat of the Titans; winning team received 100 drachmas in money and an ox in each of the three classes; ox's meat shared between team members and families
  - Manly Excellence (*evandria*): unique to Athens, organized by tribes, each supplying a team with a *leitourgiai* (public duty holder) as team manager; winner received same award as Pyrrhic dance victors plus shields for individual members; large size and strength scored points
  - Torch-race: organized on a tribal basis; winning tribe received 300 drachmas for the crew and 200 more designated "for the feasting" — a special celebration by the victorious tribe
  - Regatta: ran from the main harbour to Munichia (same course used at other festivals); winning tribe received 300 drachmas for crew and 200 for feasting
- **Venues**: horse-races on open ground between city and Phaleron; athletic events in a stadium roughly on the site of the present one in Athens; musical events in the Odeion; regatta at Peiraeus; contests involving tribes and the torch-race confined to citizens; musical and athletic games Panhellenic and open to competitors from anywhere in the Greek world

**Peplos** (printed pp. 38–41):
- The central feature of the Panathenaia was the *peplos*, the dress for the goddess.
- Woven by a team of maidens called the *Ergastinai* (Workers), chosen from the aristocratic families of Athens.
- The warp was set on the loom on the last day of the month Pyanepsion — the festival of the *Chalkeia*. This was nearly nine months before the Panathenaia; also a festival of craftsmen, and the date was probably felt appropriate for a beginning. The late source that mentions the date also states that the work of setting up was done by the priestesses together with the *Arrephoroi* (little girls specially dedicated each year to the worship of Athena). The solemn act of starting the work was begun by a group particularly linked to the goddess's service, then carried on by the Ergastinai.
- Material: wool, the traditional stuff of early Greek clothing. The main preoccupation was not the weaving of the material but its decoration — woven in the manner of tapestry, using terms used to describe pictures produced.
- Traditional subject repeatedly mentioned in ancient authors: Athena's exploit in overcoming Enceladus in the battle of the Gods and Giants (popular subject on Attic vases after the Persian Wars).
- Design executed in bright colours — yellow and blue are mentioned — and was a conventional motive in the New Comedy to describe a maiden leading a sheltered life as only coming to town to see the *peplos*.
- By the late fifth century at least the offering was of such colossal size that it was as big as a ship's sail.
- To convey it to the Acropolis it was fixed on a model of a ship mounted on wheels: the **ship-cart**.
- The ship-cart had a crew of priests and priestesses wearing golden and colourful garlands. One of these priests is honoured in a late inscription for having three times driven the vessel to the temple of Athena.
- The cart was hauled from the Kerameikos Gate to the shrine of the Eleusinian Demeter on the slopes of the Acropolis. To have conveyed it up the final climb to the Propylaia was obviously too great a task; but the *peplos* was struck at that point. How it was brought the remainder of the route to the Parthenon is not told.
- The ship-cart itself was docked near the Areiopagus (Pausanias records it among the sights for the tourist, kept there from one Panathenaia to the next).
- Why a ship? Athena has no associations with the sea in her cult or epithets. The explanation suggested: the idea of a ship-cart was borrowed from the worship of Dionysus where it seems to have been a long-established tradition. Probably the use of this form of vehicle for Athena's robe was devised at the time when the robe itself was first made of colossal size — possibly mid-fifth century when the Parthenon's cult-image of Athena was planned on a similar scale. Pheidias' statue of gold plates and ivory was over 30 feet high.
- The ship-cart was kept from festival to festival, but the robe was re-made for each occasion. Its design was the subject of severe competition. Aristotle records "once the council used to judge the samples for the *peplos* but now they are judged by a court appointed by lot" — implying the council had shown favouritism.
- 307/6 BC: Demetrius Poliorcetes occupied Athens; Straocles voted extravagant honours to Antigonus and his son, including weaving their portraits into the pattern of Athena's *peplos* in the company of the goddess herself. This may not have been done for the Great Panathenaia of 306 BC, but was introduced into the *peplos* in 302 BC — a disastrous omen: when the ship-cart with the *peplos* as sail was passing through the Kerameikos in the procession, a sudden squall struck it and broke the mast, tearing the middle of the robe. Next year at Ipsus Antigonus was killed. Athens rejected its allegiance to Demetrius; the dramatic poet Philippides attacked Straocles:
  > Through him it was that hoar-frost blasted all the vines,  
  > Through his impurity the robe was rent in twain,  
  > Because he gave the god's own honours unto men.
- 298 BC: Lysimachus (king of Thrace) supplied a new mast and yard-arm as a political gesture, for the Great Panathenaia of 298 BC.
- The ship-cart does not appear on the Parthenon frieze, probably because it would have been quite out of scale with the human figures. The *peplos* is probably represented, but in a somewhat mysterious manner: the central panel over the main east door shows two young girls approaching a tall dignified woman (priestess of Athena Polias), a bearded man holding the edges of a large oblong piece of cloth, and a boy facing him folding the cloth. Parke: "This being folded so as to be laid away" is the ultimate stage in the reception of the *peplos*. Alternative interpretation: the stools they carry were intended to hold the *peplos* folded to lie before the temple until the conclusion of the ceremony. The Twelve Gods are shown seated on either side on just such stools.
- The oblong cloth on the frieze is much too small for the robe as big as a ship's sail — four feet by seven at the scale of the figures (rather more than human dimensions). Either the colossal size had not yet been introduced when the frieze was designed (earliest literary reference is early fourth century BC), or the designer chose, probably for aesthetic reasons, to represent it as it used to be handled.

**Agonothetai** (printed pp. 42–43):
- The *Agonothetai* were the board specially appointed to organise the Great Panathenaia. The other four-yearly festivals were run by an annual board of ten Hieropoioi appointed by the *boulē*.
- The Agonothetai were chosen by the *boulē* by lot, one from each of the ten tribes, so that representation was spread evenly among the Athenians. They were in office for four years from one Great Panathenaia to the next.
- Their duties (Aristotle): "they manage the procession of the Panathenaia, the musical and gymnastic contests and the horse-racing; they arrange for the making of the *peplos* and the oil jars in association with the *boulē* and they present the olive oil to the athletes."
- Starting on the 4th of Hecatombaion they remained in continuous session for the last twenty-four days and were given dinner at public expense in the Tholos where the *boulē* dined.

**Procession participants** (printed pp. 43–44):
- Head of procession on either side: groups of maidens — most shown carrying bowls, jugs and incense-burners (apparatus for sacrifice). The leading pair on either side are empty-handed: the *Ergastinai*, given the place of honour at the head of the procession when the *peplos* is presented to Athena. Late inscriptions give lists of possibly 100 names at least in one year.
- **Kanephoroi** (basket-bearers): chosen before each festival from the noble families; their function was to carry on their heads baskets containing the meal which would be sprinkled on the victims. They are not shown on the frieze, but must be pictured as elaborately equipped for the occasion. In the late fourth century Lycurgus provided out of state funds gold ornaments for a hundred Kanephoroi.
- **Diphrophoroi** (chair-bearers): described as following the Kanephoroi, carrying chairs and sunshades for the comfort of the aristocratic Kanephoroi during the lengthy ceremony in the blazing sun.
- **Thallophoroi** (green branch-bearers): old men carrying boughs, probably of Athena's olive-trees. The frieze shows a group of older men halted while one adjusts his garland. They were chosen for their good looks — it was typical of the fifth-century Athenian that while he respected old age, it was not sufficient to qualify for a procession meant to be a beautiful spectacle. There was no place in the procession for old ladies.
- **Military contingent**: literary sources always mention foot-soldiers; the frieze shows horsemen (the artist selectively depicted the cavalry in all stages from mounting to gallop). Four-horse chariots also depicted — each chariot driven by a charioteer in the long robe traditional for the function, with a passenger who was a mature man in full hoplite armour (*apobates*, "dismounter") whose task was to leap from the chariot when in motion, run beside it, and then remount again without stopping it.
- **Metoikoi** (resident non-Athenians): prominent and wealthy body in fifth-century Athens.
  - Young Metoikoi men who could not be in the proper military procession: *Skaphephoroi* (tray-bearers), carrying trayfuls of offerings; trays were made of bronze or silver by the state; the offerings on them were cakes and honeycombs, and the bearers themselves wore purple robes.
  - Daughters of metics: carrying water jars.
  - Freed slaves and other barbarians (non-Greeks): carrying branches of oak-trees.
  - These groups cannot be identified on the frieze, but Parke notes this may well be of set purpose.

**Sources**:
- Herodotus — Hippocleides story (founder of games)
- Aristotle — Agonothetai duties; note on council vs. court judging *peplos* samples; *leitourgiai* for team events
- Aristophanes — various allusions
- Diogenes Laertius / Xenophon — *evandria* details
- Vegetius — parallel for *apobatai*
- Pausanias — ship-cart docked near Areiopagus; Plate references
- Fourth-century BC prize inscription (preserved in two fragments) — musical and athletic prizes
- Lycurgus-era reference — gold ornaments for Kanephoroi
- Demetrius Poliorcetes episode — Plutarch / Diodorus; poet Philippides quoted
- Parthenon frieze — visual evidence for procession, *peplos* scene, cavalry, chariots, maidens, gods
- New Comedy — allusions to *peplos* design and the maiden coming to town to see it

**Notes**:
- The Great Panathenaia was quadrennial; the annual festival operated on the same pattern at smaller scale.
- The Panathenaic amphora remained the standard conventional pattern for two centuries.
- Parke's inference: the ship-cart was devised in the mid-fifth century when the robe first became colossal and the Parthenon's cult-image was planned on a similar scale.
- The *peplos* represented on the Parthenon frieze is much smaller than the literary sources describe; Parke offers two explanations without resolution.
- The musical performers were professionals of high standing; the athletic and equestrian competitors were a mix of citizens (tribal events, torch-race) and Panhellenic (athletic, musical).
- Old men were selected for their good looks for the Thallophoroi; this reflected fifth-century Athenian aesthetic sensitivity.
- The designer of the Parthenon frieze was deliberately selective and probably reduced the monotony of infantry by showing cavalry; he also omitted non-citizen participants.
