### Elaphebolia

**Day**: 6 (presumed — Parke: "her usual day"; not stated explicitly in an ancient source)

**Deity**: Artemis Elaphebolios ("Shooter of the Deer")

**Ritual**:
- Sacrifice of stags to Artemis; in the classical period actual stags were replaced by pastry substitutes
- Substitute offerings: cakes called *elaphoi* ("stags"), moulded from dough, honey, and sesame-seeds

**Aition**: None discussed by Parke.

**Participants**: General community; Parke says by the fourth century the cult was "not likely to have been at all widespread in the community" and finding fresh stags had become practically difficult.

**Sources**: Footnote 152 (Plate 49); no specific ancient text named in the body of Parke's discussion for this festival.

**Notes**:
- The month Elaphebolion is named after this festival, but the festival had "dropped into unimportance" by the classical period.
- The use of pastry models as substitutes Parke compares to the Diasia (where poor families could not afford an animal victim).
- The cult title Elaphebolios is attested elsewhere in Greece; catching a stag was an ancient tradition associated with Artemis.
- Elaphebolion was dominated instead by the City Dionysia.

### City Dionysia (Dionysia en astei / Great Dionysia)

**Day**: 9 (Proagon); 10 (Pompe / main procession and sacrifice); dramatic contests continuing on following days (four days total in the fifth century, three during the Peloponnesian War, possibly extended further in the Hellenistic period)

**Deity**: Dionysus Eleuthereus

**Ritual**:

Pre-festival — Transfer of the statue (some days before the festival):
- The old wooden statue (*xoanon*) of Dionysus Eleuthereus was removed from its temple in the city and carried to a small shrine in the gymnasium — the sanctuary of the Academy, outside the city walls on the road to Boeotia (the route from Eleutherai)
- The statue remained there for some days and was the object of sacrifices
- On the evening of the 8th or 9th the statue was brought back with an appropriate escort to its temple beside the Dionysiac theatre in time for the festival
- This ceremony was known as the "Bringing in from the sacrificial hearth" (*eisagoge apo tes hestias*)
- Parke distinguishes this from the main procession (*Pompe*), which was a separate rite

Day 9 — Proagon (Preliminary to the Contest):
- Choregoi together with their teams were paraded before the public on a platform set up in Pericles' Odeion (from mid-fifth century)
- Actors appeared in their ordinary clothes without masks or costumes — a preview of the competing companies
- An announcement of the poet and the titles of his plays
- "This publicity was probably needed": no placards, no programmes

Day 10 — Pompe (main procession):
- Consisted essentially in bringing victims; bulls were the traditional animal
- Carrying of phalli in procession (a standing feature; the foundation charter of the Athenian colony of Brea, c. 445 BC, required the colonists to send a cow and a panoply to the Great Panathenaia and a phallus to the Dionysia)
- Resident aliens (*Metoikoi*) put on purple robes and carried trays of offerings (*skaphia*); hence called "tray-bearers" (*Skaphephoroi*)
- Citizens wore whatever clothes they pleased and carried leather bottles (*askoi*) on their shoulders; hence called "bottle-carriers" (*Askophoroi*)
- One high-born maiden was chosen as the *Kanephoros*, walking in procession carrying a golden basket full of first-fruits (presumably grapes)
- Special loaves of bread (*obeliai*, "like spits") — long thin rods — were also offered
- Route passed by various shrines where the procession paused for the performance of dances in honour of the gods
- Climax: arrival at the altar near the Dionysiac theatre and slaughter of the victims; in 333 BC financial accounts show 240 bulls were offered
- Following the procession: a large dinner ending with a revel (*Komos*)

Night of the Komos:
- Men escorted by torches, accompanied with music of flutes and harps, went out singing and dancing in the streets
- Depicted frequently on Athenian pottery

Dramatic festival (contests):
- Originally: chorus of men singing and dancing; first actor added by Thespis (first performance of a tragedy 534 BC)
- By the end of the sixth century: groups of three successive plays (tragedies) followed by a satyr play
- 486 BC: comedies with a comic chorus and actors added to the programme
- About 509 BC: choral singing of dithyrambs formally included
- Pattern in the fifth century: three tragedies in morning + satyr play; then (after interval) a comedy in the late afternoon
- Five comedies were produced (five choregoi for comedies)
- Three choregoi for the tragedies, three for dithyrambs sung by men, three for dithyrambs sung by boys
- Tribes grouped in pairs to form five choirs of men and five of boys, each containing fifty singers
- Victors received a tripod, dedicated to Apollo in the Pythion near the Ilissos
- The archon chose three wealthy individuals (*choregoi*) every year, one from each of three different tribes, to finance training, costumes, etc.
- Each *choregos* also had actors and a poet assigned to him (up to three actors each)
- Panel of judges: names of candidates put into ten jars sealed and deposited on the Acropolis; ten names drawn at the start of the festival; at the end they wrote verdicts; archon drew five from a jar; majority decision accepted

Audience and access:
- Admission obtained by purchasing a ticket costing two obols; Pericles credited with founding the Theoric fund to pay this sum per head to citizens
- Front seats reserved for magistrates, chief officials, priests, and distinguished visitors from abroad; presiding person was the priest of Dionysus
- Ordinary citizens joined a stream making their way to the theatre at some early hour
- Wine and savouries/sweetmeats handed round during performances; some could also be dispensed by sellers in the gangways
- Whether women were admitted to see tragedies is debated: some references imply their presence; Aristophanes' comedies seem to imply only males in the theatre; Parke: "Perhaps in the fifth century at least the distinction may have existed that women could attend the tragedies, but not the comedies"

Civic displays at the Dionysia (fifth century):
- The tribute of the empire was delivered in Athens at mid-March and exhibited before the assembled audience in sacks on the dancing-floor
- Orphan sons of those killed in battle were maintained by the democracy; at the City Dionysia they marched on to the dancing-floor in full hoplite armour while a herald proclaimed they were the sons of those who died heroically; the state then sent them out into life equipped with arms

Civic displays (fourth century):
- Practice developed of voting a golden crown to politicians or other benefactors and proclaiming the award through a herald at the new tragedies (subject of Aeschines' attack on Demosthenes' proposal; Ctesiphon prosecuted)
- A slave-owner wishing to free a slave would arrange to have the fact broadcast to the audience

Post-festival civic meeting:
- On one of the days immediately after the Dionysia, a meeting of the people was held in the Dionysiac theatre presided over by the Council of Five Hundred
- A public inquest was held into the conduct of the festival
- The festival could end satisfactorily with the council proposing a vote of a crown to the archon; or any member of the public could come forward and lodge a complaint

**Aition**: Parke discusses two versions of the coming of the cult from Eleutherai:
1. Historical/legendary: inhabitants of Eleutherai voluntarily acceded to Athens to escape the Boeotians, likely in the mid-sixth century under Peisistratus. Tradition credited a certain Pegasos of Eleutherai as the man responsible; Pausanias adds a Delphic oracle response supporting the transfer.
2. Mythological (a second legendary version reported by Pausanias): a building in the Kerameikos contained terracotta statues showing Amphictyon entertaining Dionysus and gods at a banquet; Pegasos was among the guests. In this version the Athenians had not received the god with proper honour; his wrath manifested in a disease attacking the genital organs; the oracle advised paying due worship and holding a procession with a phallus in his honour; on doing this the sickness was cured. Parke: "The myth of Dionysus being repulsed and then enforcing recognition of his cult is universal throughout Greece."
- The historical transfer is placed in the mid-sixth century; the earliest temple at the Dionysiac theatre dates to approximately this date archaeologically.

**Participants**:
- Archon (responsible for organising the dramatic and choral performances)
- Choregoi (three per tragic contest, five for comedies, six for dithyrambs) — wealthy individuals from three tribes
- Actors (up to three per tragic chorus)
- Chorus members — men and boys recruited from different tribes
- Metoikoi (resident aliens) — purple-robed, carrying trays (*skaphia*)
- Citizen men — carrying leather bottles (*askoi*)
- Kanephoros — one high-born maiden carrying the golden basket
- General Athenian citizens (and resident aliens and foreigners at the theatrical performances)
- Council of Five Hundred — presided over post-festival inquest
- Priest of Dionysus — presiding person, given a front seat

**Sources**:
- Pausanias: transfer of Dionysus Eleuthereus cult; legendary account with Pegasos; the building in the Kerameikos
- Foundation charter of Athenian colony Brea (c. 445 BC): phallus requirement
- Philochorus (third century BC): picturesque account of Athenians going to the theatre after breaking their fast and drinking; wine and sweetmeats handed round
- Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*: spectators eating sweetmeats during bad actors
- Aristophanes, *Birds*: advantages of having wings in the theatre; also *Acharnians* (Komos description implied)
- Aeschines (attack on Demosthenes / prosecution of Ctesiphon): golden crown voted at the Dionysia
- Financial accounts of 333 BC: 240 bulls offered (calculated by Ferguson)
- Thespis: first performance of tragedy 534 BC
- Isaeus speeches: Phratria introducing a child at a festival (actually this is Thargelia — not Elaphebolion; Parke does not cite Isaeus for City Dionysia)

**Notes**:
- The festival fell about mid-March, the traditional time for the opening of the sailing season; this made it suitable for visitors from the Aegean islands and the coast of Asia Minor.
- The official organizing the dramatic and choral performances was not the Basileus but the archon; Peisistratus controlled nominations to magistracies and thus the new festival was under his direct influence.
- Drama is described as originating in the worship of Dionysus of Eleutherai; the tragic chorus was originally a chorus of men singing and dancing; Thespis added the first actor; two and three actors developed gradually.
- In the fifth century performances were newly written works; in the fourth century revival of earlier tragedies was allowed and systematically arranged (works of Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides).
- The City Dionysia was open to resident aliens and foreigners (unlike the Lenaia, which was for citizens only).
- Women's admission to the comedies is Parke's tentative reconstruction — explicitly flagged as debated.
- The introduction of Dionysus at its new festival is placed "much more difficult to date" than Asclepius' arrival (which is chronologically certain at 429 BC).

### Asclepieia

**Day**: 9 of Elaphebolion (same day as the Proagon)

**Deity**: Asclepius (introduced to Athens from Epidaurus in 420 BC)

**Ritual**:
- A considerable sacrifice
- Probably followed by a common meal (the usual form in the worship of Asclepius)
- No other details of the ceremony are known

**Aition**: Parke does not discuss an aition for the Asclepieia specifically. He notes that Asclepius was housed in a sanctuary on the higher slopes of the Acropolis above the Dionysiac theatre. The annual festival of his *arrival* in Athens was celebrated at the time of the Eleusinian Mysteries; the Asclepieia was his other Athenian festival.

**Participants**: Not specified beyond the general inference from Asclepius worship; the cult became established in Athens from 420 BC.

**Sources**: Footnote 170; Parke states the introduction of Asclepius in 429 BC [sic — 420 BC in text] is "chronologically certain"; Sophocles is mentioned as possibly having influence in establishing the local festival on the date of the Proagon.

**Notes**:
- The claim that Sophocles arranged the site and date of the Asclepieia is Parke's inference: "Was it through his effort that the site assigned to Asclepius adjoined the precinct of Dionysus ... and can he also have arranged for the local festival to take place on a date covered to some extent by Dionysiac patronage?" Parke marks this with "?" — explicitly speculative.
- Parke calls it "remarkable that this intrusive cult managed to stake a claim within the feast days of two such cults as the Eleusinian Demeter and Dionysus Eleuthereus."
- The linkage of Asclepius with Eleusis: Asclepius' arrival seems connected to Eleusis, which may have given him admittance to the City Dionysia circle.

**Parke**: printed p. 135 / PDF p. 134

### Pandia

**Day**: Uncertain; Parke says it was held "almost immediately after the City Dionysia." The day following the Pandia was the day set aside for the public meeting (inquest on the Dionysia). Parke implies it immediately followed the festival but gives no specific day number.

**Deity**: Zeus (festival of Zeus)

**Ritual**:
- No details survive; Parke says "almost nothing is known about it"
- The ancient commentators who mention it are troubled only by its etymology/derivation

**Aition**: None discussed by Parke.

**Participants**: Parke says on the analogy of the Panathenaia the name should mean a festival of Zeus "in which all combined" and therefore involving all communities of Attica — but there is no evidence for this. Only one district (Plotheia) is known to have celebrated it. The tribe Pandionis may have had a special connection.

**Sources**:
- Ancient commentators (unnamed by Parke)
- Deubner's conjecture: the tribe Pandionis had some special link with the festival (evidenced by an assembly decree in honour of one of its members, Demon — a cousin of Demosthenes — and a priest, for services at the festival)
- Pandion (mythical king of Attica, eponymous hero of one of the ten tribes): Parke notes the connection but says "the myth has vanished"

**Notes**:
- Parke: "It was probably a survival from the archaic past which had become fossilized."
- On analogy of Panathenaia the name *Pandia* should mean "all Zeus" (pan + Dia), implying all communities — but no evidence supports this.
- Connected with Pandion should be derived from the festival (Pandion = founder), but the myth that would explain this has vanished.
- Establishment of the ten tribes by Cleisthenes in the late sixth century complicates any reconstruction of the Pandionis connection.
- The Pandia is "scarcely mentioned in our sources."

**Parke**: printed p. 136 / PDF p. 135
