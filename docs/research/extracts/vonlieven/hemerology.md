# Von Lieven — Hemerology in Ancient Egypt
## Source: Von Lieven, Alexandra (2016). "Tagewählerei im Alten Ägypten / Hemerology in Ancient Egypt." *Mythos* 10, pp. 31–41. DOI: 10.4000/mythos.424.

PDF: mythos-424.pdf (12 pages, full article). Article originally delivered as a paper on 27 March 2001 at the XXVIII Orientalistentag in Bamberg, published here with updated literature.

---

## PAPYRUS SOURCES DISCUSSED

### 1. pKahun XVII.3 (= pUC 32192) — Middle Kingdom
- Found among the Kahun papyri from Illahun in the Fayum
- Dateable to the 12th Dynasty (1976–1794 BCE)
- **Oldest extant hemerology**
- Contains a list of 30 entries, each "good day" or "bad day," written one below the other
- The preserved portion does not indicate which month the listed days belong to
- Almost certainly a personal extract from a fuller work that would have included rationales
- Content: minimal — only the ratings, no mythological explanations

### 2. pCairo JE 86637 — principal manuscript of the great calendar
- One of two full copies of the great hemerology *Ḥꜣ.t nHH pH.wy Ḏ.t* ("The Beginning is Eternity, the End is Infinity")
- The *content-complete* manuscript (though physically damaged)
- Dated to the New Kingdom (1550–1070 BCE) on palaeographic grounds
- Likely origin: Deir el-Medineh
- Contains full entries: date, rating (good / uncertain / dangerous), mythological event, behavioural instructions, birth prognoses, death-cause prognoses

### 3. pSallier IV — New Kingdom parallel manuscript
- Second full copy of the great calendar *Ḥꜣ.t nHH pH.wy Ḏ.t*
- New Kingdom, probably also from Deir el-Medineh
- Sometimes differs from pCairo where pSallier IV uses "uncertain" (ꜥHꜥ) where pCairo has "dangerous" (oHꜣ) — a likely euphemism
- Also occasionally uses rubricized nfr ("good") where pCairo has oHꜣ ("dangerous") — this occurs only four times: pSallier IV 1,3; 1,5; 1,9; 11,9
- Von Lieven's explanation: the scribe began copying from a source that used euphemistic nfr for dangerous entries, and switched to oHꜣ on page two, maintaining this until a slip on page eleven

### 4. pBM 10474 — Late Period Abrégé
- British Museum papyrus
- Dateable to the 26th or 27th Dynasty (664–401 BCE)
- Contains only date and day-rating, no mythological explanations — analogous to pKahun XVII
- Shows the tradition continued into the Late Period

### 5. oMalinine and oGardiner 109 — ostraka
- Each contains only one month's material
- Smaller texts, clearly excerpts from larger works
- Show that multiple different hemerological calendars existed

### 6. pTurin 54022 — unpublished, Ramesside
- From Deir el-Medineh
- Described by Demichelis (1998): contains not only day-ratings but also the deity assigned to each day, the deity's taboo, and festival listings for each day
- More strongly priestly/scholarly in character than pCairo / pSallier IV
- Von Lieven flags this as the most informative of the still-unpublished texts
- Also unpublished: ppTurin 54016, 54023, 54029 (parallels to pSallier IV)

### 7. Berlin-Strasbourg-Brooklyn Cult Calendars (pBerlin 14472 + pStrasbourg BNU hier. 38a + pBerlin 29065)
- Edited by Quack (2012)
- From two hands/manuscripts:
  - **Berlin-Strasbourg** (pBerlin 14472 + pStrasbourg BNU): possibly from the Hermopolite nome; ca. 2nd–1st century BCE
  - **Berlin-Brooklyn** (additional fragments): probably from the temple library of Elephantine; ca. 26th Dynasty
- Content per day: protective deity, deity's taboo, type of wind, a protective prayer for Pharaoh to recite, day-rating, festival listings
- Von Lieven says this is "obviously analogous" to pTurin 54022 in content

### 8. Chronocrator List from the Ptolemaic temple of Edfu
- Edfou XV 45–62 (Cauville, Devauchelle 1985)
- Not a hemerology in itself, but contains individual day-ratings embedded in a list of day-deities
- Shows hemerology tradition surviving into the Ptolemaic period

### 9. Festival Calendar from the Roman-period temple of Esna
- Spalinger (1991)
- Contains scattered day-ratings
- Not a hemerology in toto, but shows continued use of the genre

### 10. Onomastikon I from Tebtynis (2nd century CE)
- Contains rudimentary remains of a calendar with day-ratings
- Roman-period Fayum; temple library context

### 11. Menology on a writing tablet (18th Dynasty, ca. 1475 BCE)
- Edited by Vernus (1981)
- Unique so far — the only attested menology (month-level, not day-level ratings) on a writing tablet
- The text itself may be linguistically much older (possibly Old Kingdom, 3rd millennium)
- Egyptian title: ꜣbḏy.t, derived from ꜣbḏ "month"
- Content: assigns a deity to each month and gives omens (good and bad) for that month

## DATED DAY-RATINGS MENTIONED

### Example entry: IV Akhet 21 (= app month 3, day 21)
Von Lieven quotes this entry verbatim as her primary illustrative example (p. 35):

> "4. Monat der Überschwemmungszeit, Tag 21. **Gefährlich, gefährlich, gut.** Jener Tag des Hervorgehens der Großen aus der Unterwelt, um das Wirksame Auge zu suchen. **Du sollst nicht nach draußen gehen am Mittag des Tages.**"

Translation: "4th month of the inundation, day 21. **Dangerous, dangerous, good.** That is the day of the going-forth of the Great Ones from the underworld to seek the Effective Eye. **You shall not go outside at midday.**"

- Rating: three-part — first third of day DANGEROUS; second third DANGEROUS; last third GOOD
- Mythological basis: the going-forth of "the Great Ones" (major deities) from the underworld to search for the Effective Eye (Udjat Eye, Eye of Re / Horus)
- Behavioural instruction: do not go outside at midday (the dangerous second third)
- App month: this is **IV Akhet = app month 3**, day 21 — coinciding exactly with Bleeker's "opening of the window in the sanctuary of Sokaris" (see Bleeker extraction)

### Menology example: II Akhet (Phaophi = app month 1)
Von Lieven quotes the menology entry for the 2nd month of the inundation (p. 36):

> "Zweiter Monat der Überschwemmungszeit. **Schentait** ist die Wirksame dieses Monats. Wenn du aus deinem Haus herauskommst und jemand neben dir sagt 'Nicht ich bin es, der hitzig ist', oder wenn eine Frau sagt 'Nicht ich bin es, die hitzig ist', dann sollst du dazu sagen 'Wie gut ist dieser Monat! Es wird kein Fieber darin geben.'."

Translation: "Second month of the inundation. **Šentait** is the Effective One of this month. If you come out of your house and someone says 'It is not I who am feverish,' or a woman says 'It is not I who am feverish,' you shall reply 'How good is this month! There will be no fever in it.'"

- Deity: Šentait (a funerary cow-goddess, associated with Osiris)
- Two omens are usually given (one good, one bad) in these menology entries
- Format: "If X (condition), then say Y" — identical to the formulaic structure of Egyptian medical diagnoses

### Epagomenal day 3 (Harpocrates/Horus' birth) — Late source
Von Lieven notes that **Plutarch's De Iside 12** contains a remark about the **3rd day of the Epagomenal days**, showing that Plutarch's informants still had access to traditional mythological day-ratings in the Roman period. Griffiths (1970) provides the commentary. This confirms that the tradition of rating the epagomenal days mythologically was still alive in Roman Egypt.

### I Akhet 16 — birth prognosis (mentioned in passing)
> "dem an jenem Tag Geborenen der Tod durch ein Krokodil prophezeit wird"

Translation: "the one born on that day is prophesied death by crocodile." Von Lieven uses this as an example of birth prognoses — one of the categories of entries in the great calendar. The specific day is I Akhet 16 (= app month 0, day 16).

### The "dangerous" / "uncertain" rating distinction
Von Lieven identifies a scribal issue between pCairo and pSallier IV:
- pCairo: oHꜣ (Aleph) = "dangerous"
- pSallier IV: often ꜥHꜥ (Ayin) = "uncertain" where pCairo has "dangerous"
- This is likely not a real theological difference but a euphemism — "uncertain" as a polite word for "dangerous" (since red = negative colour in Egypt)
- Rubricized nfr for oHꜣ: four instances in pSallier IV, all apparently where the scribe's source used the euphemistic form

### Divergent ratings across witnesses — III Akhet 18
Von Lieven's key methodological caution: for **III Akhet 18 (= app month 2, day 18)**, all three witnesses give different ratings (one each of the three possible values: good / uncertain / dangerous). This shows the tradition was not monolithic — local variation, productive reception, and individual scribal choices all played a role.

## STRUCTURE OF HEMEROLOGIES

### The civil calendar framework
All Egyptian hemerologies use the **schematic 360-day year** (12 months × 30 days) plus 5 epagomenal days at year-end. This is the administrative/civil calendar, not the observational lunar calendar.
- 3 seasons: Ꜣḫ.t (inundation, app months 0–3), pr.t (winter/growing, app months 4–7), Šmw (summer/harvest, app months 8–11)
- Each month: 3 decades (10-day "weeks")
- Epagomenal days: 5, treated separately as especially dangerous

### Three-part day rating
Each day has **three separate ratings** for its three parts (morning, midday, evening — or, more precisely, the three thirds of the day):
- "gut" (good) = nfr
- "ungewiß" / uncertain = ꜥHꜥ (rubricized red)
- "gefährlich" / dangerous = oHꜣ (rubricized red)

The three parts can have different values. Von Lieven's example: IV Akhet 21 = "dangerous, dangerous, good." The midday third is when one should not go outside.

### Content of each entry in pCairo / pSallier IV
Fixed schema for each day (in scriptio continua):
1. **Date** (month, day)
2. **Day-rating** (three values, one per third of day)
3. **Mythological event** on that date (what the gods did) — the justification for the rating
4. **Behavioural instructions** for the user (mostly prohibitions: "do not go outside at midday", "do not eat fish", etc.) — derived from the mythological event
5. (Sometimes) **Birth prognosis**: fate of one born on this day
6. (Sometimes) **Death prognosis**: how one born on this day will die

### Title and authority claim of the great calendar
The title of the main hemerology (*Ḥꜣ.t nHH pH.wy Ḏ.t*) is:

> "Beginning of the (book) 'The Beginning is Eternity, the End is Infinity,' written by the gods of the shrine, assembled by the gods of the Ennead, redacted by the Majesty of Thoth in the Great State Sanctuary beside the Lord of All, found in the library in the inner house of the Ennead."

Von Lieven notes: no other Egyptian text claims authority with such intensity — authorship by the entire top tier of the pantheon, found in one of the most important temples. Each month begins with "House of Re, House of Osiris, House of Horus" — possibly assigning each decade to one of the three gods.

### Structure of the menology
Different from the main hemerology:
- Each month (including the 5 epagomenals) is assigned a **tutelary deity** (the "Effective One" of that month = Wirkungsmächtige, a type of Chronocrator)
- Two omens per month (one good, one bad), in the diagnostic formula: "If X (condition), then say Y"
- Format identical to medical diagnosis texts

### Leitz's 10 systematic principles for rating derivation
Christian Leitz (1994, the standard modern edition of pCairo/pSallier IV) identified 10 principles by which ratings and their mythological bases are systematically derived:
1. Seasonal natural events
2. Attachment to existing festival dates
3. Connection to immediately adjacent calendar day
4. Two or more days form a thematic unit
5. Equation of days of the month with hours of a day
6. Equation of month-days with lunar-month days
7. Identical calendar days in different seasons share the same or opposite connotations
8. Days exactly half a year apart have opposite interpretations
9. Allusion to the numbers in the date
10. Multiple days connected such that the sum of their year-days yields a notable number

Von Lieven notes: these principles are well-founded but some applications are problematic. Specifically, she questions Leitz's interpretation of alleged "lions" (mꜣj) — she suspects some instances are spellings of mꜣꜣ ("to see") rather than actual lions, which would undermine his meteorological theories about those entries.

### Behavioural instructions
Instructions are not only ritual/cultic — they also cover general everyday life:
- Whether to start a new undertaking
- Whether to go outside (especially at dangerous times of day)
- Going to the qnb.t (council/court assembly) — Leitz found that for days where the calendar advises against attending the qnb.t, no session is actually attested in administrative documents from Deir el-Medineh

## NOTES FOR APP VALIDATION

### Authority hierarchy of papyri
Von Lieven does not rank the papyri in terms of authority, but implies:
1. **pCairo JE 86637** is the principal manuscript — most complete, content-authoritative
2. **pSallier IV** is the main parallel — useful for comparison but prone to euphemistic substitutions
3. **pBM 10474** (Late Period Abrégé): shows which ratings the tradition found most important to preserve
4. **pTurin 54022**: most informative about the full priestly tradition but unpublished as of 2016

### Warning on variant ratings
"Die verschiedenen Textzeugen sich keineswegs stets einig sind" — the different witnesses by no means always agree. For III Akhet 18, all three possible values appear across the witnesses. Ratings from the Cairo Calendar should be treated as the primary tradition but never as uniform or universal.

### IV Akhet 21 is specifically cited
The example entry chosen by Von Lieven to illustrate the hemerology system is **IV Akhet day 21** ("4th month of inundation, day 21") — rating "dangerous, dangerous, good" — which corresponds directly to the Sokaris cycle in Bleeker: day 21 is "opening of the window in the sanctuary of Sokaris." This coincidence (Principle II in Leitz: attachment to existing festival dates) likely explains the "dangerous" rating.

### Egyptian concept of "dies aegyptiaci"
The Latin term *dies aegyptiaci* (Egyptian days = especially unlucky days) derives ultimately from the Egyptian hemerology tradition. Von Lieven notes it is unclear whether the term originally referred to the traditional hemerology or to the astrologised variant. The concept survived into Late Antiquity.

### Astrological overlay (later period)
From the Late Period onward, traditional hemerologies were supplemented and eventually overshadowed by astrology. In Roman-period Tebtynis, astrological (demotic) material dominates the divinatory texts. PGM III 275–281 and PGM VIII 284–299 show zodiac-based divinatory recommendations. Traditional hemerologies were less popular by this time.

### Social context
The preserved hemerological texts (where provenance is known) come from:
- New Kingdom: Deir el-Medineh (royal tomb workers — not a representative cross-section)
- Middle Kingdom: Illahun settlement
- Greco-Roman period: temple libraries at Tebtynis and Dime

Von Lieven's conclusion: hemerological knowledge was "priestly science" that spread outward from the temple to the upper classes, then gradually to the wider population. The common people probably relied on oral tradition. The texts themselves, however, are unambiguously priestly products.
