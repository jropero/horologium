# Review: Boedromion
Sources: Parke (1977), Deubner (1932)
Date: 2026-10-25

---

## Genesia — Day 5

**Parke**: State celebration in honour of the dead. Jacoby may be right that originally it belonged to aristocratic clans. Solon may have converted it into a national celebration — a kind of Remembrance Day recognizing the state's debt to the dead (mainly those fallen in battle). Ceremony modelled on Thucydides' account of a public funeral: bones in a tent, coffins on wagons by tribe, public burial place, funeral oration. No deity other than the dead mentioned. No mythological aition given.

**Deubner**: State public festival (δημοτελής) for commemoration of the dead, with sacrifice also to Ge (Earth); private family commemorations coincided on the same day. Derives name from γενήτης = "festival of the ancestors."

**App**: Deity "Los Muertos." Desc: "Día de recuerdo y luto público por los muertos, originalmente de los clanes aristocráticos." Aition: "Solón impuso este mito social: los caídos ya no pertenecían a sus familias, sino que la diosa Tierra (Ge) absorbía su sangre para convertirla en el cimiento invencible que unificaba a toda Atenas."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION + MISSING_DETAIL

**Issues**:
1. The aition is invented. Neither Parke nor Deubner describes Ge absorbing the blood of the fallen to form an "unbeatable foundation" unifying Athens. Parke gives no mythological narrative at all; the origin is historical (Solon converting an aristocratic rite to a state festival), not mythological.
2. Ge is missing from the deity field. Deubner explicitly notes sacrifice was also made to Ge (Earth). The app lists only "Los Muertos."

**Fix**: Remove the invented Ge-blood-foundation aition entirely. Replace with the historical explanation Parke gives: Solon transformed an aristocratic clan rite into a state day of public mourning, recognizing the polis's debt to its fallen. Add Ge as a secondary deity recipient (Deubner). A safe aition, if one is wanted, is simply: "Solon instituted this festival as a state Remembrance Day for all citizens fallen in battle, converting what had been private aristocratic clan commemorations into a public acknowledgment of collective debt to the dead."

## Artemis Agrotera — Day 6

**Parke**: 500 she-goats sacrificed annually; Polemarchos officiated; Epheboi paraded under arms in procession. The vow was made before Marathon (one goat per Persian killed). The compromise of 500 yearly because the original goat population of Attica "would have been wrecked." Xenophon confirms the annual 500 were still performed in his day. The 6th was chosen because Artemis already had her festival on this day (Apollo's birthday was the 7th; his twin was evidently thought born a day earlier). The actual battle did NOT take place on the 6th — Parke argues the 6th was more likely the date when the Athenian army resolved to march; the battle was ~10 days later.

**Deubner**: Polemarchos sacrificed 500 goats annually, fulfilling a vow made before Marathon; epheboi participated in full armour; the 6th was chosen because Artemis already received a sacrifice on this day.

**App**: Deity "Artemisa." Desc mentions 500 goats, Polemarchos, Epheboi. Aition: "más de 6.400 enemigos" killed at Marathon; original vow would extinguish Attic goats; renegotiated to 500 yearly.

**Verdict**: MISSING_DETAIL (otherwise accurate)

**Issue**: The app omits the reason the 6th was chosen — Artemis already had a festival on that day (Apollo's birthday on the 7th, his twin born the day before). This is noted by both Parke and Deubner and is the actual calendrical logic for the date. The app also does not flag Parke's point that the battle itself was NOT on the 6th, though this is a scholarly nuance rather than an app error.

**Fix**: Add to the description or aition: "El 6 fue elegido porque Ártemis ya recibía culto en esta fecha (el 7, cumpleaños de su hermano gemelo Apolo, le correspondía a él)." The 6,400-Persian figure is consistent with Herodotus as cited by Parke — keep it.

## Boedromia — Day 7

**Parke**: Thanksgiving to Apollo Boedromios. Only ONE ancient literary reference attests the celebration, and it mentions a procession (Demosthenes, sarcastically). The Ion legend: in the reign of Erechtheus, Eumolpus led the Eleusinians against Athens; Ion came hastily to help and brought about victory; Erechtheus fell in battle; Ion became king after him. Parke calls this "a typical story invented to explain a cult, but it is unnecessary." He suggests the date may reflect end of campaigning season. Very little evidence — Parke is clear the festival is poorly attested.

**Deubner**: Sacrifice to Apollon Boedromios with a pompe; month named after festival.

**App**: Deity "Apolo Boedromios." Desc: "Acción de gracias militar marcando el fin de la temporada de campañas." Aition: "El nombre significa 'correr a ayudar al oír un grito'. El mito cuenta que, cuando los ejércitos de Eleusis iban a masacrar Atenas, el héroe Ión escuchó el clamor desesperado y bajó a la carga para salvarlos."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION (embellishment) + MISSING_DETAIL

**Issues**:
1. "los ejércitos de Eleusis iban a masacrar Atenas" — embellished. Parke says Eumolpus was winning when Ion came to help; it does not say Eleusis was about to massacre Athens. The app adds dramatic urgency not in the source.
2. The end-of-campaigning-season explanation ("marcando el fin de la temporada de campañas") is Parke's speculative suggestion, not a fact. The app presents it as the established description.
3. The app presents the Ion legend without noting Parke's explicit skepticism ("a typical story invented to explain a cult, but it is unnecessary").
4. The app omits that this festival has only one ancient literary source and is very poorly attested — overclaims certainty.

**Fix**: Either flag the Ion legend as an explanatory myth of disputed value, or lead with the more certain material: a general thanksgiving to Apollo as patron of military rescue, marked by a procession. The Ion legend can be presented as the Attic aition with a note that Parke considers it secondary. Remove "masacrar Atenas" — say "estaban venciendo a los atenienses" instead.

## Day 13 — Escort of the Hiera (MISSING FESTIVAL)

**Parke**: On the 13th, Epheboi went out to Eleusis to escort the Holy Things (*Hiera*) to Athens. The Hiera were carried in round boxes (*kistai*) tied with purple ribbons. The priestesses dismounted at the Rheitoi streams to carry them across a narrow bridge; the wagon went round via the ford. On arrival, the Phaidryntes (Cleaner) reported to the priestess of Athena on the Acropolis. The conveyance was a high state occasion.

**Deubner**: Day 13: epheboi went to Eleusis and escorted the sacred objects (cists) back to the urban Eleusinion below the Akropolis.

**App**: No entry for Day 13.

**Verdict**: MISSING_FESTIVAL

**Fix**: Add a Day 13 entry for the escort of the Hiera. Suggested content: festival name "Procesión de las Hieras" or "Escolta de las Cosas Sagradas"; deity: Deméter y Perséfone; desc: "Los efebos marchan a Eleusis para escoltar las 'Cosas Sagradas' (Hiera) a Atenas, transportadas en kistai (cestas redondas) atadas con cintas de púrpura. A su llegada, el Phaidryntes sube a la Acrópolis para notificar a la sacerdotisa de Atenea."

## Eleusinian Mysteries, Day 1 — Agyrmos — Day 15

**Parke**: The Gathering (*Agyrmos*). Assembly in Agora at the Painted Portico (*Stoa Poikile*). Hierophantes and Daiduchos delivered formal proclamation. Candidates must be of pure hands (especially re blood-guilt) and 'soul conscious of no evil, lived well and justly.' Excluded: those with blood-guilt, barbarians (non-Greeks; Romans later accepted). Fees paid (15 drachmas total in the late 4th century). Mystagogoi assigned to guide foreign initiates.

**Deubner**: Assembly of the mystai; Hierophant and Daduch formally excluded barbarians and murderers from the Stoa Poikile.

**App**: Desc: "Proclamación oficial invitando a los candidatos puros a la iniciación." Aition: "El heraldo ruge advirtiendo a bárbaros y asesinos que se alejen. Este rito revive el terror neolítico a la impureza (miasma): un solo asesino oculto contaminaría la mirada de las Diosas y provocaría una plaga."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION

**Issue**: The aition's "terror neolítico" framing and the specific claim that "a single hidden murderer would contaminate the gaze of the Goddesses and cause a plague" are not in Parke or Deubner. The concept of miasma (ritual pollution) is genuine, but neither source uses this specific plague-from-murderer's-gaze formulation. The "neolithic terror" language is the app developer's invention.

**Fix**: Replace the invented aition with the sourced explanation: the proclamation required initiates to be free of blood-guilt and 'soul conscious of no evil.' The underlying Greek concept of miasma (ritual pollution) meant that impure participants could harm the rite and the community. This is grounded in Parke's text without inventing specifics. Remove "terror neolítico."

## Eleusinian Mysteries, Day 2 — Halade Mystai — Day 16

**Parke**: Command "Seaward, Initiates!" (*Halade Mystai*). Initiates travelled to the Piraeus or Phaleron coast (~8 miles). Each Mystes walked into the water carrying a piglet supplied by the authorities. Piglet purified by immersion then sacrificed; initiates sprinkled with the blood. Probably ended with a feast of suckling pig. Parke notes the pig was "the conventional victim offered to Demeter, perhaps as a natural emblem of fecundity" — not as a sin-bearer. Mylonas: more like a holiday outing. Shark incident in 339 BC.

**Deubner**: Initiates and their sacrificial pigs bathed in the sea at the Kantharos harbour (Piraeus); the pigs were subsequently sacrificed.

**App**: Desc: "Los iniciados marchan a Falero para purificarse bañándose en el mar salado junto con un lechón." Aition: "Cada iniciado debe bañar un cerdito en el mar y luego degollarlo. Creían que el agua salada curaba el alma y que el pecado humano se transfería al cerdo, quien moría llevándose las culpas al inframundo."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION + MINOR_ERROR

**Issues**:
1. "a Falero" — Deubner specifies Kantharos harbour (Piraeus). Parke says Piraeus or Phaleron. Phaleron alone is slightly off; Piraeus is the more specifically attested location.
2. The scapegoat interpretation ("el pecado humano se transfería al cerdo, quien moría llevándose las culpas al inframundo") is not in either source. Parke says the pig was the conventional Demeter victim, "perhaps as a natural emblem of fecundity" — the opposite of a pollution-bearer. The app inverts the pig's ritual role.
3. "el agua salada curaba el alma" is a reasonable interpretation of sea-purification but not explicitly stated by Parke or Deubner.

**Fix**: Remove the scapegoat/sin-transfer interpretation — it contradicts Parke's framing of the pig as a Demeter offering (fecundity, not pollution). A safe aition: the pig was the traditional offering to Demeter; the salt sea was a classical purifier; the rite combined two modes of cleansing before the initiation. Change "a Falero" to "al Pireo (puerto de Kantharos)" or "al Pireo o Falero."

## Eleusinian Mysteries, Day 3 — Hither the Victims — Day 17

**Parke**: Probably called 'Hither the victims' (*Deuro hai hiereia*) — the names of Demeter and Persephone were too sacred to utter, so this paraphrase was used. Likely the main state sacrifice in Athens for the Two Goddesses. Very little ancient evidence survives for this day; the name itself is tentative.

**Deubner**: Sacrifice for Demeter and Kore at the urban Eleusinion, attested in a private Attic calendar.

**App**: Festival day name left in English "Hither the Victims." Desc: "Gran sacrificio oficial del estado a las Dos Diosas." No aition.

**Verdict**: MISSING_DETAIL

**Issues**:
1. The day-name "Hither the Victims" is left in English while all other day-names are in Spanish. Inconsistency.
2. The app calls it the "gran sacrificio oficial" — Parke calls it the likely date of the "main state sacrifice" and notes very little evidence. The certainty is overstated slightly, but the core content is correct.

**Fix**: Translate the day name: "Deuro hai hiereia (¡Aquí, las víctimas!)" or "El Gran Sacrificio." Add a note that the day-name is tentative (Parke notes it is "probable, not certain").

## Eleusinian Mysteries, Day 4 — Epidauria — Day 18

**Parke**: Asclepius cult introduced to Athens from Epidaurus in 420/19 BC. The Mystai were expected to stay indoors. An independent festival of Asclepius (the *Epidauria*) filled this vacuum, organized with a pompe by the Basileus. The myth: Asclepius had come to Athens to seek initiation but arrived too late for the preliminary ceremonies, so they were all rushed through again for his benefit — giving mythological cover to any other late arrivals. Sacred snake sent from Epidaurus. The founding inscription names the archonship of Astyphilos (420/19 BC).

**Deubner**: Asklepios festival inserted when his cult was admitted to the Mysteries; pompe organized by the Basileus; mystai stayed home.

**App**: Day name "Epidauria." Desc: "Llegada de Asclepio. Los rezagados se purifican y se rinde culto al dios de la medicina." No aition.

**Verdict**: MISSING_DETAIL + partial conflation

**Issues**:
1. "Los rezagados se purifican" — the myth about latecomers is Parke's secondary explanation for the Epidauria's mythological function; it is not the primary description of the day. The primary content is an independent Asclepius festival with a pompe organized by the Basileus while the Mystai stay home.
2. The 420/19 BCE date of the cult's introduction — an important historical datum Parke gives — is omitted.
3. "Llegada de Asclepio" conflates the historical introduction of the cult (420/19 BCE) with the mythological narrative (Asclepius arriving to seek initiation). These are distinct.

**Fix**: Lead with the festival structure: an independent Asclepius festival (Epidauria) held while the Mystai stayed indoors on their day of quiet preparation; pompe organized by the Basileus. Add the 420/19 BCE founding. The latecomers myth can be mentioned as the aition for why latecomers could complete their purifications on this day.

## Eleusinian Mysteries, Day 5 — Great Procession — Day 19

**Parke**: The Iacchos image (young man with torch) carried from near the Dipylon Gate. Holy Things escorted from the Eleusinion. 14-mile procession to Eleusis. Mystai wore myrtle garlands and carried *bacchoi* (myrtle branches tied with wool) and a bundle of provisions on a stick. Gephyrismos: men with heads COVERED to conceal their identities sat on the Kephisos bridge and hurled ribald insults at distinguished persons — Parke notes it is "impossible to decide how far this had a magic intention to forestall any ill-luck, or again how far it was just a traditional opportunity for popular licence." Procession arrived at Eleusis after dark by torchlight.

**Deubner**: Great procession along the Sacred Road to Eleusis with sacrifices, libations, hymns, and dances at stations. Night 19/20: torch-lit dances and songs at the Kallichoron well; Iakchos received solemnly by the Epimeletes.

**App**: Desc: "Gran procesión escoltando las 'Cosas Sagradas' de vuelta a Eleusis." Aition: "Gephyrismos (Los insultos del puente): Durante la marcha, los peregrinos cruzan un puente mientras los lugareños les gritan insultos y obscenidades. Esto imita a las ancianas que insultaron a Deméter, pero servía para espantar el 'mal de ojo' y la soberbia antes del rito."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION + MISSING_DETAIL

**Issues**:
1. "los lugareños les gritan insultos" — Parke says the insulters were men with COVERED heads concealing their identity, not "locals." The anonymity is ritually significant.
2. "Esto imita a las ancianas que insultaron a Deméter" — Parke makes no connection between the gephyrismos and any old women insulting Demeter (that belongs to the Iambe/Baubo tradition in the Hymn). The app invents this aition. Parke explicitly says the purpose is unknowable.
3. Night 19/20 at Eleusis: torch-lit dances and songs at the Kallichoron well, Iakchos received solemnly by the Epimeletes (Deubner) — not mentioned in the app.
4. The Iacchos figure and the bacchoi (myrtle branches with wool) are not mentioned in the app.

**Fix**: Remove the old-women-insulting-Demeter aition — it is invented. Replace with Parke's honest framing: the gephyrismos was a traditional custom of anonymous ribald insults at the Kephisos bridge, possibly apotropaic, possibly just popular licence — its exact meaning is unknown. Add mention of Iacchos. Consider adding the Night 19/20 torch-lit arrival at Eleusis (Deubner).

## Eleusinian Mysteries, Day 6 — Telete — Day 20

**Parke**: Night initiation in the Telesterion. Kykeon drunk (water, flour, pennyroyal) — aition from the Homeric Hymn: Metaneira offered Demeter wine; Demeter refused in her mourning and commanded the kykeon mixture, establishing the precedent. Darkness followed by brilliant light: the Hierophantes appeared "in a brilliant light" and revealed the Hiera. The Anaktoron (holy of holies) at the centre. The aphorism attributed to Aristotle: initiates "rather had something done to them than had received instruction."

**Deubner**: Initiation ceremony (kykeon-drinking, handling of ciste-objects, dromena: Koremythos drama, Hieros Gamos, birth proclamation of Brimon, "Ue Küe" formula, reaped ear of grain shown to Epoptai); Epopteia for those initiated the previous year; closing plēmochoai ceremony.

**App**: Desc: "Noche de vigilia, ayuno y bebida del Kykeon. Revelación en el Telesterion." Aition: "Los iniciados beben 'Kykeon' (cebada y poleo) en lugar de vino porque Deméter, de luto por Perséfone, rechazó el vino ofrecido por la reina de Eleusis y pidió esta mezcla humilde, convirtiéndola en ley ritual."

**Verdict**: OK (minor imprecision)

**Issue**: The kykeon is described as "cebada y poleo" but Parke says "water, flour, and the herb pennyroyal." "Flour" (barley-meal) is close to "cebada" but strictly the kykeon is the mixed drink, not barley alone. The Homeric Hymn specifies barley-meal (*alphita*), water, and pennyroyal — so "cebada" is defensible but "harina de cebada" would be more precise. The aition itself (Demeter refusing wine, asking for kykeon from Metaneira) is correctly sourced from the Homeric Hymn as cited by Parke.

**Fix**: Minor: change "cebada y poleo" to "harina de cebada, agua y poleo" to match the Homeric Hymn's three-ingredient kykeon.

## Eleusinian Mysteries — Segundo día de revelaciones — Day 21

**Parke**: Not explicitly assigned to its own day. Parke discusses the epoptai ("those who gazed at something") as either those allowed to see more than ordinary initiates or those allowed a closer look at the Holy Things. Plutarch compared the progress of the initiate to the development of the philosopher. The initiation's effect on the fear of death is implied by sources (e.g., Cicero's statement on the Mysteries giving comfort in death) but Parke does not specifically cite Plutarch for the claim about eliminating fear of death.

**Deubner**: Epopteia grouped within "days following 20" alongside the Myesis and Telete — not assigned a separate day.

**App**: "Los epoptai (iniciados avanzados) recibían la visión suprema de las Cosas Sagradas." Aition: "El segundo día culminaba con la Epopteia: la visión definitiva que, según Plutarco, eliminaba el miedo a la muerte para siempre. Lo que se veía era tan secreto que revelarlo se castigaba con la muerte."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION (day division and specific claims)

**Issues**:
1. Neither Parke nor Deubner assigns the Epopteia to a separate Day 21 — it is grouped with "days following 20." The day-by-day breakdown is the app's own construction.
2. "según Plutarco, eliminaba el miedo a la muerte para siempre" — Parke cites Plutarch in the context of the initiate and philosopher comparison, but does not specifically quote Plutarch saying the Mysteries "eliminate fear of death forever." This specific Plutarch attribution needs a direct source.
3. "revelarlo se castigaba con la muerte" — a common claim about the Mysteries, but not stated in this Parke extract.

**Fix**: Either collapse Days 20–21 into a single entry for the initiation night(s), or flag that the day-by-day division is a reconstruction not directly attested. Verify the Plutarch citation before keeping it.

## Eleusinian Mysteries — Plemochoai — Day 22

**Parke**: Named after a spinning-top-shaped vessel with a firm base. Initiates took a pair, filled with water, tipped one toward the east and the other toward the west, uttering a mystic formula. "The words are not recorded." Parke: "perhaps suggests a magic rite meant to encourage rain, and such may have been the original purpose in the ceremonies of the goddess of corn."

**Deubner**: Listed as part of the closing ceremony in "days following 20."

**App**: Desc: "Rito vertiendo vasijas de agua al este y al oeste pidiendo fertilidad." Aition: "Los iniciados vierten agua desde vasijas en forma de peonza murmurando una fórmula secreta. Era magia empática: un truco prehistórico para invocar la lluvia e hipnotizar a las semillas para que crecieran."

**Verdict**: OK (minor embellishment)

**Issue**: "hipnotizar a las semillas para que crecieran" is the app's elaboration beyond Parke's careful "perhaps suggests a magic rite meant to encourage rain." The "prehistoric trick" framing is not in either source. Parke's hedged language ("perhaps suggests") should be honoured.

**Fix**: Minor: change "hipnotizar a las semillas" to "estimular la lluvia y la germinación" and flag Parke's uncertainty ("quizás un rito mágico para invocar la lluvia").
