# Review: Maimakterion
Sources: Parke (1977), Deubner (1932)
Date: 2026-10-25

---

## Maimakteria — Day 10

**Parke**: Existence of the festival is inferred solely from the month's name. No day is given. No ritual content is described. Parke says: "Neither festival is mentioned in popular literature." The only ritual implied is prayers addressed to Zeus to be kind in controlling the wind's violence. No ram sacrifice is mentioned.

**Deubner**: Day unknown. "No ritual content is preserved." Deubner supposes the festival concerned averting damage from the onset of winter storms, but gives no specifics.

**App**: Places the festival on day 10. Describes "Sacrificio solemne de un carnero para apaciguar a Zeus" (solemn sacrifice of a ram). States the libations were wineless (nephálio). Aition explains the month's storms sinking ships and ruining late harvests.

**Verdict**: WRONG_DAY | UNSOURCED_RITUAL | UNSOURCED_AITION

**Issues**:
1. **Day 10 is invented.** Neither Parke nor Deubner gives any day for the Maimakteria. The only dated entry for Maimakterion in the sources is the Pompaia (last third of month; private calendar: day 20). Assigning day 10 is undocumented guesswork.
2. **Ram sacrifice is unattested.** No ancient source cited by Parke or Deubner records a ram sacrifice at the Maimakteria. Parke describes only prayers; Deubner records nothing of the ritual.
3. **Nephálio (wineless) character is unattested.** Neither source says anything about wineless libations at this festival. The nephálio epithet belongs to Demeter's rites, not this ceremony.
4. **Aition about ships and harvests** is a reasonable extrapolation of Parke's weather discussion, but is framed as fact rather than modern inference.

**Fix**:
- Remove day assignment (or note it as "day unknown, month uncertain").
- Remove ram sacrifice. Replace ritual description with: prayers addressed to Zeus Maimaktes to moderate storm violence; no ritual details preserved.
- Remove nephálio claim entirely.
- Mark aition as editorial inference, not ancient source.

## Pompaia — Day 20

**Parke**: "Last third of the month" (φθίνοντος); exact day not given. Deity: Zeus Meilichios. Ritual: (1) a sheep sacrificed to Zeus; (2) the fleece (dion koidion) kept; (3) those being purified stood on it with their **left foot** (specific detail); (4) the sheepskin was carried in procession — this procession gave the rite its name; (5) accompanied by a caduceus. Route of procession unknown; Parke calls a circuit of the city a "conjecture." Ordinary Athenians played no part; this was the responsibility of priestly officials. Parke explicitly doubts Hermes was "originally the deity concerned."

**Deubner**: Last third of month (φθίνοντος); a private calendar entry connects it to Maimakterion 20. Procession carried: the dion kōdion (fleece from **a Zeus Meilichios sacrifice**), a kērykeion (herald's staff), and possibly a cauldron of Hermes. Interprets the ritual as an apotropaic circumambulation of the newly sown fields. Purificatory material was cast at crossroads.

**App**: Day 20. Deity: Zeus Meilikhios. Description: purificatory procession with the "Dios Koidion (vellón sagrado del carnero sacrificado en la Maimakteria)" carried through the city to absorb accumulated impurities. Aition: "whoever stood on it was purified; it was carried in procession so the whole city could benefit from the absorptive power of the sacred wool."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION | MISSING_DETAIL (×3) | WRONG_DETAIL (fleece origin)

**Issues**:
1. **Day 20 is defensible.** Deubner's private calendar entry places the Pompaia on Maimakterion 20. This is the only specific day in either source, and the app correctly uses it.
2. **Fleece origin is wrong.** The app states the fleece came from the ram sacrificed at the Maimakteria. Deubner explicitly says it was "fleece from a Zeus Meilichios sacrifice." There is no source linking the Maimakteria ram to the Pompaia fleece; the app invents this connection to create a narrative bridge between the two festivals.
3. **Left-foot detail missing.** Parke specifically records that those being purified stood on the sheepskin with their left foot — a notable and well-attested ritual detail absent from the app.
4. **Caduceus / kērykeion missing.** Both Parke and Deubner name the caduceus/herald's staff as a key element of the procession. The app omits it entirely.
5. **"Toda la ciudad se beneficiara" is wrong.** Parke says explicitly that "the ordinary Athenian played no part"; the rite was performed by priestly officials responsible for the community's supernatural safety. The app's aition implies universal civic participation, which contradicts the source.
6. **Crossroads element missing** (Deubner): purificatory material was cast at crossroads at the end of the procession. Not in the app.
7. **Aition mixes the standing-on-fleece purification with the procession.** The purificatory standing belonged to specific individuals (those being formally purified, e.g. before the Eleusinian Mysteries, per Parke's parallel with the Daiduchos). The app generalizes this incorrectly to the whole city.

**Fix**:
- Change fleece origin: the fleece came from a sheep sacrificed **to Zeus Meilichios** (not from the Maimakteria ram).
- Add left-foot detail: those being purified stood on the fleece with their left foot.
- Add caduceus to the ritual description.
- Correct the participant description: priestly officials, not the general public.
- Add the crossroads element from Deubner.
- Remove the claim that "toda la ciudad se beneficiara."

## Festivals in sources but NOT in app

Neither source records any other festival for Maimakterion beyond the Maimakteria and the Pompaia. The app's coverage of this month is complete at the name level, though both entries contain errors as documented above.

---

## General note on the month

Parke's opening observation — that Maimakterion "contained no popular feast day at all" and "less than the average number of festivals" due to bad weather — is the scholarly consensus. Both festivals were minor, priestly affairs, not popular celebrations. The app's framing of both as significant ritual events somewhat overstates their importance relative to what the sources record.
