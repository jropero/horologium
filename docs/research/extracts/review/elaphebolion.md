# Review: Elaphebolion
Sources: Parke (1977), Deubner (1932)
Date: 2026-10-25

---

## Elaphebolia — Day 6

**Parke**: Day 6 is presumed on the grounds that the 6th is Artemis's monthly sacred day; no ancient source states the day explicitly. Original sacrifices were stags; by the classical period these had been replaced by pastry substitutes called *elaphoi*, moulded from dough, honey, and sesame. Parke discusses **no aition** for this substitution. He notes the cult had "dropped into unimportance" by the classical period and the festival was overshadowed by the City Dionysia.

**Deubner**: Month only — no day specified. Originally deer (*elaphoi*) were sacrificed to Artemis Elaphebolos; later, substitute cakes (*plakountes*) shaped like deer, made of wheat-dough, honey, and sesame. **No aition** discussed.

**App**: Day 6. Deity "Artemisa Elaphebolos, Cazadora de ciervos." Description: pastries shaped like deer (elaphoi) made of dough, honey, and sesame. Aition: "Se hornean pasteles con forma de ciervos porque Artemisa exigía piezas reales, pero los atenienses las extinguieron cazando. El mito asegura que la diosa aceptó piadosamente estos sustitutos mágicos."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION

**Issue**: The core description (deer-shaped pastry substitutes, ingredients) is accurate per both sources. Day 6 is a reasonable inference from Parke. However, the aition — that Athenians hunted deer to extinction and the goddess graciously accepted the pastry substitutes — is **invented**. Neither Parke nor Deubner records this story. Parke explicitly has no aition; Deubner likewise. The comparison Parke does make is to the Diasia (where poor families offered cake animals instead of real victims), a practical economic substitution, not a mythological one.

**Fix**: Delete the aition entirely, or replace it with a factual note: "Por el siglo IV, la caza real de ciervos se había vuelto prácticamente imposible. Los pasteles sustituyeron al animal de modo similar a como los pobres ofrecían animales de masa en otras fiestas (cf. Diasia)." Do not invent a myth of extinction or divine acceptance.

## Asclepieia — Day 8

**Parke**: **Day 9** (the same day as the Proagon). A considerable sacrifice, probably followed by a communal meal. No other ritual details known. Parke explicitly states the annual commemoration of Asclepius's *arrival* in Athens was the Epidauria in **Boedromion** (month 3), not this festival. The Elaphebolion Asclepieia is simply his local Athenian festival, positioned adjacent to the Dionysiac precinct. Parke speculates (with a "?") that Sophocles may have arranged the site and date.

**Deubner**: **Day 8** — "sacrifice in the Asklepios sanctuary neighbouring the theater (just before the Dionysia, per hide-money accounts)." Deubner's source is the hide-money accounts that place it immediately before the Dionysia.

**App**: Day 8. Description: "Conmemora la llegada del dios a Atenas. Comida sagrada y sacrificios." Aition: "El festival ocurre en medio de los Misterios porque el propio Asclepio llegó tarde para iniciarse. Los atenienses repitieron los ritos solo para él. Desde entonces, el festival acoge a los peregrinos impuntuales."

**Verdict**: WRONG_DEITY_DESC + UNSOURCED_AITION (day is defensible per Deubner)

**Issue (1) — Festival description**: "Conmemora la llegada del dios a Atenas" is wrong for this festival. That commemoration belongs to the **Epidauria in Boedromion** (month 3, day 18), when Asclepius was formally introduced to Athens in 420 BCE and inserted into the Eleusinian Mysteries cycle. The Elaphebolion Asclepieia is his second Athenian festival, situated beside the Dionysiac theater, with no arrival narrative attached.

**Issue (2) — Aition**: The story that Asclepius "arrived late to the Mysteries" and the Athenians "repeated the rites for him alone" is the traditional aition for the **Epidauria** (Boedromion), not the Elaphebolion Asclepieia. Parke recounts this connection for Boedromion; it is misassigned here. For the Elaphebolion festival Parke says only "almost nothing is known."

**Issue (3) — Day**: App places Asclepieia on day 8 (agreeing with Deubner) while Parke says day 9. Both are defensible; this is a real scholarly discrepancy. The app's choice of day 8 is the less commonly cited date but has Deubner's support.

**Fix**: Change festivalDesc to: "Sacrificio y probable banquete sagrado en el santuario de Asclepio, adyacente al teatro de Dioniso." Remove the aition about the Mysteries entirely — it belongs to Boedromion 18. If an aition is desired, note only that "Parke speculates Sophocles may have arranged for the festival to fall within Dionysiac patronage, though this is explicitly marked as uncertain."

## Proagon — Day 9

**Parke**: **Day 9**. Choregoi together with their dramatic teams were paraded before the public on a platform set up in Pericles' Odeion (from the mid-fifth century). Actors appeared in ordinary clothes, without masks or costumes. The poet announced himself and the titles of his plays. Parke's explanation: "This publicity was probably needed" — a practical matter, since there were no placards or programmes.

**Deubner**: **Day 8** — "poets and actors presented themselves publicly before the dramatic competitions." Deubner places both the Proagon and the Asclepieia on day 8, with the Eisagoge on the evening of day 8.

**App**: Day 9. Description: "Ceremonia preliminar a las Dionisias. Los dramaturgos y actores se presentaban sin máscaras ante el público." Aition: "Los actores desfilan sin sus máscaras. En una cultura donde la máscara implicaba ser poseído por el dios, el Proagon demostraba que debajo de la magia divina seguían siendo ciudadanos mortales."

**Verdict**: UNSOURCED_AITION (day agrees with Parke, conflicts with Deubner)

**Issue (1) — Day**: The app's day 9 agrees with Parke but contradicts Deubner (day 8). This is a genuine scholarly discrepancy; the app's choice is the better-attested option.

**Issue (2) — Aition**: The interpretation that the maskless procession "demonstrated to the city that under divine magic the actors remained mortal citizens" is the app's own invention. Neither Parke nor Deubner offers this theological reading. Parke gives only the practical explanation: there were no programmes and the audience needed advance notice of what they would see.

**Fix**: Replace the aition with the source-based explanation: "El Proagon era la única forma de publicidad disponible: sin carteles ni programas, el público necesitaba saber de antemano qué obras y autores competirían."

## City Dionysia (Grandes Dionisias) — Days 10–13

**Parke**: Day 10 = Pompe (main procession): bulls as victims; phalli carried; Metoikoi in purple carrying trays; a high-born Kanephoros; special loaves (*obeliai*); procession to the altar near the theater; 240 bulls offered in 333 BCE; followed by dinner and the Komos revel. Dramatic contests continued over multiple days. Organized by the **Archon Eponymos** (not the Basileus). The aition of the genital plague is from Pausanias: Athenians refused to honor the cult; Dionysus sent a genital disease; the oracle prescribed a phallus procession; the plague was cured.

**Deubner**: Day 9 (morning) = Pompe; Day 9 (day) = dithyrambic choruses; Day 9 (evening) = Komos. Day 10 = comedies. Days 11–13 = tragedies. All organized by the Archon Eponymos.

**App**: Days 10–13, combined entry. "El evento teatral supremo del mundo antiguo. Tragedias, comedias, procesión fálica masiva..." Aition: "Se desfila con falos gigantes montados en carros porque, cuando los atenienses despreciaron la estatua de Dioniso, el dios los castigó con una plaga genital." Mentions Archon, 2-obol admission, Theoric Fund, Choregia, tribute exhibition. No separate entry for the Pompe.

**Verdict**: MISSING_DETAIL + minor WRONG_DAY concern + minor embellishment

**Issue (1) — Day range**: The app assigns days 10–13 to the Dionysia content. Per Deubner, the Pompe itself falls on **day 9** (morning), and per Parke on **day 10**. By assigning days 10–13 as a block, the app implicitly pushes the Pompe to day 10 (agreeing with Parke) but misses it as a named, discrete event. The Pompe is the central hieratic rite and should be named separately rather than absorbed into a generic block.

**Issue (2) — Aition**: The genital-plague story is directly traceable to Parke (citing Pausanias). This is correctly sourced. However, "falos gigantes montados en carros" adds carts that neither Parke nor Deubner specifies for the City Dionysia. Phallus-carts are a feature of the **Rural Dionysia** (cf. Aristophanes' *Acharnians*); for the City Dionysia, Parke says phalli were "carried in procession," not mounted on carts. This is a minor but attributable conflation.

**Issue (3) — Missing Komos as a named rite**: Deubner and Parke both describe the Komos (nocturnal revel with torches, flutes, harps, singing in the streets) as a distinct named event following the Pompe. The app mentions it only obliquely within the combined block.

**Fix**: Split the block into at minimum: (a) Day 9 — Pompe and Komos; (b) Days 10–13 — dramatic contests. Or, following Parke's chronology: (a) Day 10 — Pompe + Komos; (b) Days 11–13 — dramatic contests. Remove "montados en carros" from the aition (phalli were carried, not cart-mounted, at the City Dionysia).

## Pandia — Day 14

**Parke**: Day uncertain — "almost immediately after the City Dionysia." "Almost nothing is known about it." Zeus festival. The tribe Pandionis had some special connection (evidenced by a decree). The myth of Pandion as eponymous founder "has vanished." Parke: "probably a survival from the archaic past which had become fossilized." No specific day given.

**Deubner**: "A few days after the Dionysia (Wilamowitz suggests full moon)." Festival of Zeus; phyle Pandionis celebrated specially; the day *after* the Pandia = mandatory popular assembly in the theater to review the festival.

**App**: Day 14. "Festival de clausura tras las Dionisias, posiblemente un festival de la antigua tribu de Pandion." Aition: "Tras el éxtasis de las Dionisias, este festival honra al rey Pandión. Funcionaba como un freno psicológico: Atenas debía recordar la estricta moralidad y el dolor del mundo real tras días de locura báquica."

**Verdict**: WRONG_DAY (unattested; Wilamowitz's full-moon date would be ~day 14–15, so plausible but explicitly uncertain) + UNSOURCED_AITION

**Issue (1) — Day**: Neither source gives day 14. Wilamowitz's full-moon suggestion (cited by Deubner) lands around day 14–15 of Elaphebolion, making day 14 a reasonable inference. It should be flagged as uncertain in the UI.

**Issue (2) — Aition**: The "psychological brake" reading — that the Pandia served as a sober return to Athenian morality after Bacchic ecstasy — is entirely invented. Both sources agree that essentially nothing is known about the Pandia's content or meaning. Parke explicitly says the myth has vanished. The app's aition is narrative fiction.

**Fix**: Remove the aition entirely. Replace with: "Festival de Zeus escasamente documentado. Casi nada se sabe de su contenido. La tribu Pandionis tenía algún vínculo especial. Al día siguiente se convocaba la asamblea obligatoria para revisar el desarrollo de las Dionisias." Mark the day as approximate.

## MISSING FESTIVAL: Eisagoge — Evening of Day 8

**Parke**: Describes the pre-festival transfer of the *xoanon* (old wooden statue) of Dionysus Eleuthereus: removed from its city temple to the Academy sanctuary on the road to Boeotia (the route from Eleutherai); on the evening of the 8th or 9th it was brought back by torchlight escort to the theater precinct. Parke calls this the *eisagoge apo tes hestias* ("Bringing in from the sacrificial hearth") and explicitly distinguishes it from the main Pompe.

**Deubner**: **Evening of Day 8** — "Eisagoge": epheboi escorted the image of Dionysos Eleuthereus by torchlight from the Eschara (chapel near the Academy) to the theater precinct, re-enacting the original transfer from Eleutherai; a general banquet preceded the procession.

**App**: Not present. The app has no entry for the Eisagoge.

**Verdict**: MISSING_FESTIVAL

**Issue**: The Eisagoge is documented by both sources as a named, distinct rite on the evening of day 8 — the torchlight procession that formally opened the City Dionysia. It is the re-enactment of the cult's foundation myth (transfer from Eleutherai) and directly precedes the Pompe. Its absence leaves the festival sequence incomplete: the app moves from Proagon (day 9) to the Dionysia block (days 10–13) with no opening rite.

**Fix**: Add a day 8 evening entry: `{ festivalName: "Eisagoge", deity: "Dionisio Eleuthereus", category: "Dramático y Dionisíaco", festivalDesc: "Procesión nocturna con antorchas: los efebos escoltaban la antigua estatua de madera (xoanon) de Dioniso desde la capilla junto a la Academia hasta el recinto del teatro. Recreaba la transferencia original del culto desde Eleutherai. Le precedía un banquete general." }` No aition is needed — the historical founding narrative (voluntary accession of Eleutherai, Pegasos, Delphic oracle) is the aition for the City Dionysia as a whole.

## MISSING FESTIVAL: Galaxia — Month, day unknown

**Parke**: Not mentioned.

**Deubner**: "Month probable; inferred from the Delian month Galaxion = Attic Elaphebolion." Festival of Meter Theon (Mother of the Gods). A barley-and-milk porridge (*galaxia*) named the festival; epheboi sacrificed and dedicated a phiale to the goddess.

**App**: Not present.

**Verdict**: MISSING_FESTIVAL (low priority)

**Issue**: Deubner places this in Elaphebolion based on the Delian calendar parallel. The day is unknown and the Athenian attribution is inferred rather than certain. Its absence is less critical than the Eisagoge, but it is a documented Elaphebolion festival.

**Fix** (optional): Add a floating entry (no specific day): `{ festivalName: "Galaxia", deity: "Meter Theon", category: "Agrario y Fertilidad", festivalDesc: "Ofrenda de gachas de cebada y leche (galaxia) a la Gran Madre. Los efebos sacrificaban y dedicaban una pátera a la diosa." }` Flag as day-uncertain.
