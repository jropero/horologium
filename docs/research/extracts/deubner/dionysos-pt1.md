# Deubner — Dionysos, Part 1 (Anthesteria)
Source: L. Deubner, *Attische Feste* (1932), printed p. 93–122 / PDF p. 102–131
Extracted: 2026-10-25

---

## Overview

The Anthesteria were celebrated on the 11th, 12th, and 13th of Anthesterion. The three days bore their own names: Pithoigia, Choes, and Chytroi. The notice that Choes and Chytroi occurred on the same day is explained by the ancient calendar day beginning at evening (so the evening of the 12th = start of the 13th). The main day was the 12th, as the 12th of a month was in general associated with Dionysiac festivals.

Thucydides (2.15.4) names only the 12th as the date of the "older Dionysia" celebrated for Dionysos ἐν Λίμναις (in the Marshes) — clearly the Anthesteria. This also fixes the sanctuary: Dörpfeld's identification of the precinct on the west slope of the Acropolis with the Limnaion (Dionysion in the Marshes) is accepted by Deubner as very probable. The precinct was open only one day a year (the 12th), accessible only through a door, its regulations inscribed on a stele next to the altar.

**Sources**: Didymos in Schol. Aristoph. Ach. 1076; Schol. Thukyd. 2.15.4; Thuk. 2.15.4; Dörpfeld, Ath. Mitt. 20 (1895); [Demosth.] 59.76; Isaeus 8.35.

### Pithoigia
**Month/Day**: Anthesterion 11
**Deity**: Dionysos (in the Limnaion / ἐν Λίμναις)
**Ritual**:
1. People brought their pithoi (storage jars) and drinking vessels to the Limnaion and its immediate surroundings.
2. Before drinking, they poured a libation from the new wine to the deity.
3. They prayed that the use of this φάρμακον (the new wine, which Deubner explains as a dual-natured force, potentially harmful or beneficial) would be "harmless and wholesome" (ἀβλαβῆ καὶ σωτήριον).
4. They drank, danced, and sang, invoking Dionysos with epithets: Εὐανθής, Διθύραμβος, Βακχευτάς, Βρόμιος (Athenaeus 465a, citing Phanodemos).
5. Slaves were permitted to participate in the general feast.

**Aition**: None discussed for the Pithoigia's wine-opening aspect. Deubner explains the logic structurally: libating first-fruits to the god before consuming them is a universal mechanism for breaking the "taboo of beginnings" (Tabu des Anfangs), paralleled by the Roman Meditrinalia formula ("I drink old and new wine, I cure old and new illness"). He explicitly rejects Jane Harrison's alternative interpretation (that the name refers to opening grave-jars to release souls — see Notes).

**Participants**: All Athenians (free and slave); Deubner says the slaves' participation is confirmed by Schol. Hesiod Erga 366.

**Sources**:
- Plutarch, *Quaest. conv.* 655e (prayer formula, date as 11th Anthesterion)
- Plutarch, *Quaest. conv.* 735e
- Athenaeus 465a (Phanodemos: Athenians carrying new wine in pithoi to the sanctuary of Dionysos ἐν Λίμναις)
- Schol. Hesiod, *Erga* 366 (slaves not excluded from the wine of Dionysos)
- Demosthenes 21.52 (oracle: citizens to remember Bakchos, stand in the streets)
- Kallimachus, *Hekale* frg. 94 Kapp

**Notes**:
- Deubner firmly rejects the sepulchral interpretation of Jane Harrison (*JHS* 20, 1900; *Prolegom.* 43f.), who connected the Pithoigia to the opening of grave-jars (pithoi used as coffins) to release souls — analogous to the Roman *mundus patet*. She also adduced the Jena lekythos (Taf. 8,2) showing eidola flying from a pithos with Hermes. Deubner counters: (1) no such ritual is attested at Attic graves; (2) the lekythos shows a libation vessel with a perforated bottom (a Totengefäß for the grave), not a coffin-pithos; (3) not all eidola are exiting — one re-enters; (4) the Eustathios passage (to Il. 24.526) sets the opening of Pandora's jar in explicit contrast to the joyful Dionysiac Pithoigia. Harrison's view was followed by Mommsen, Pfuhl, Harrison herself, Roussel (BCH 51, 1927); opposed by Farnell, Nilsson, Meautis.
- The vase image Taf. 8,1 (youth ladling wine from a garlanded pithos into a garlanded jug) may show the Pithoigia, but could also anticipate the Choes.

**Deubner**: printed p. 93–95 / PDF approx. p. 102–104

### Choes (Day of Pitchers / Kannenfest)
**Month/Day**: Anthesterion 12 (the main day, ἐπίσημος ἡμέρα)
**Deity**: Dionysos ἐν Λίμναις; also Dionysiac aspects related to wine and marriage

#### 1. Drinking Contest

**Ritual**:
1. A herald proclaimed the city-wide drinking contest (Aristophanes, *Ach.* 1000).
2. Officially it took place in the Thesmotheteion (the official meeting hall of the Archons), but private gatherings occurred everywhere.
3. Each participant received his own chous (a measure of just over 3 Attic litres) of already-mixed wine; normally symposia shared a common mixing-bowl.
4. The contest was drunk in silence (Eur. IT 951), though the official event in the Thesmotheteion may have had trumpet accompaniment (*Ach.* 1000 with schol.).
5. The winner received a wineskin of wine from the Basileus (Schol. Aristoph. *Ach.* 1002; Aristoph. *Ach.* 1224); prizes also included cakes (πλακοῦς) and a garland of leaves (φύλλινος στέφανος).
6. After the drinking, each symposiast placed his own garland around his chous and brought the garlands to the priestess of Dionysos at the sanctuary ἐν Λίμναις; there the wine dregs were libated.

**Participants**: All Athenians (free and slave); officiants at the Thesmotheteion = the Archons. The Basileus presided over the official event.

**Sources**:
- Aristophanes, *Acharnians* 1000, 1002, 1087, 1224
- Aristotle, *Ath. Pol.* 3.5 (Bukoleion location)
- Plutarch, *Quaest. conv.* 613b (Thesmotheteion, Orestes story)
- Plutarch, *Quaest. conv.* 643a (each guest given cup, chous, separate table)
- Phanodemos at Athenaeus 437c–e (Orestes aition)
- Euripides, *IT* 949ff. (Orestes aition)
- Schol. Aristoph. *Ach.* 961, *Kn.* 95; Suid. *Χόες*
- Eratosthenes at Athenaeus 276c; Plutarch, *Anton.* 70.3
- Krates of Mallos at Athenaeus 495b (on the chous as a vessel consecrated to the festival)
- IG 2² 1252.11 (Amynos club, exemption from chous contribution = ἀτέλεια τοῦ χοός)
- Hegisandros at Athenaeus 365d (Argives call the symposion-contribution a χοῦς)
- IG 2² 167.2 (an ox granted to the public slaves at the Choes)

**Notes**:
- Deubner clarifies that participants drank from kylix/cups or other vessels into which wine was poured from the chous; they did not drink directly from the chous (its form is that of a jug, not a cup). He corrects Fivel and P. Gardner.
- The younger Dionysios staked a golden garland as Choes prize; Xenokrates won it (Timaeus at Athenaeus 437b).

#### 2. Aition for the Individual Choes

**Aition**: Orestes, arriving in Athens on the festival day to stand trial at the Areopagus for matricide, was hospitably received. Because his participation in the common mixing-bowl would have ritually polluted the Athenians, and because they didn't want to dishonour him by excluding him, King Pandion (= Demophon in Phanodemos) devised a solution: each guest was given his own chous and table, with orders to drink from his own cup alone. Thus the festival of individual wine-pitchers came into being. Already known to Euripides (IT 949ff.).

**Sources**: Schol. Aristoph. *Ach.* 961, *Kn.* 95; Suid. *Χόες*; Phanodemos at Athenaeus 437c–e; Euripides, IT 949ff.

**Notes**: Deubner treats this as a secondary aetiological myth attached to a pre-existing custom. He stresses the "Greek aetiological tendency" (ausgeprägte ätiologische Tendenz) without denying that the myth was already known to Euripides. The connection of the chous-contest to the Orestes myth is aetiology proper, not historical explanation.

#### 3. Hieros Gamos (Sacred Marriage)

**Ritual**:
1. Dionysos arrived in the city on a ship-wagon (Schiffskarren), drawn through the streets — evidenced by black-figure vase paintings (Taf. 11,1 etc.) and a parallel at Smyrna (where the priest steered a ship on wheels from the harbour into the city at the Anthesteria/Katagogia).
2. The ship-wagon procession: headed by a kanephore; followed by a youth with thymiaterion, men with the sacrificial bull, the ship-wagon itself with Dionysos and two flute-playing satyrs; mocking carts (ἁμαξηλάται) bringing up the rear and insulting bystanders (attested by Phot./Suid., Dionysios of Halikarnassos 7.72.11, Harpokration s.v. πομπεία).
3. The procession went first to the Limnaion.
4. At the Limnaion: the Basilinna and fourteen Geraeren (γεραιραί / γεραράδες — "the Venerable Ones", wives of the noblest men) performed the ἀπόρρητα ἱερά at fourteen altars. These rites, conducted in secret, probably involved sexual matters (fitting the Hieros Gamos).
5. Before touching the sacred objects, the Geraeren were sworn by the Basilinna (with the help of a Hierokeryx) at the altars over the sacred baskets (κανοῖ): they attested to their sexual purity and promised to perform the Theoinia and Iobakcheia for Dionysos at the prescribed times.
6. The Basilinna entered a secret inner room of the Limnaion — the only person permitted there — and brought out the sacred objects (ἱερά) which the Geraeren then handled.
7. The wedding ceremony (ἐξεδόθη τῷ Διονύσῳ γυνή): the Basilinna was "given in marriage" to Dionysos.
8. A wedding procession from the Limnaion to the Bukoleion (old official hall of the Archon Basileus, near the Prytaneion), with the ship's stylis (Taf. 11,3–4, the New York kanna with children enacting this scene) carried by three boys — confirming connection to the ship-wagon.
9. The sacred act (ἱερὸς γάμος) was consummated in the Bukoleion.
10. In the evening, the Basilinna received the garlands of the Choes-drinkers at the Limnaion — Deubner infers (following Petersen, Nilsson) that the "priestess" receiving the wreaths is the Basilinna herself, and the garlands are a bridal blessing.

**Deity**: Dionysos (ἐν Λίμναις); Basilinna = sacred bride
**Participants**: Basilinna (wife of Archon Basileus); 14 Geraeren; Hierokeryx; the Basileus (who Deubner believes played the role of Dionysos in the Hieros Gamos)

**Sources**:
- [Demosthenes] 59 (*Against Neaira*), 73–78 (the fullest account: oath of the Geraeren, oath text, law requiring the Basilinna to be a virgin, ἐξεδόθη formula, the secret precinct)
- Aristotle, *Ath. Pol.* 3.5 (Bukoleion location, the wife's marriage to Dionysos takes place there: γαμεῖ δὲ τοῦ βασιλέως γυνὴ ἐνταῦθα τῷ Διονύσῳ)
- Bekker, *Anecd.* 1.228.9; 1.231.32 (γεραιράδες defined as priestesses of Dionysos ἐν Λίμναις, 14 in number)
- Hesychius s.v. γεραιραί; Etymologicum Magnum 227.35 (γεραιραί)
- Pollux 8.108
- Vase: British Museum Choenkanne (Taf. 10,1–2), showing the wedding of Dionysos and the Basilinna
- Vase: New York Choenkännchen (Taf. 11,3–4): children enacting the procession, with girl as Basilinna, boy as παρανύμφος, stylis from the ship
- Inscriptions from Miletos and Priene on the Katagogia (parallel to the ship-wagon entrance)
- IG 2² 1368.111ff. (Iobakchen: Katagogia in Imperial period, at the site above the Limnaion)
- Philostrat. v. Apoll. 4.21 (orphic performances at the Anthesteria in Imperial period)
- Schol. Aristoph. *Frö.* 216 (the Limnaion has an οἶκος and a ναός)

**Aition**: None for the Hieros Gamos specifically in Deubner. He explains it structurally: it is a fertility-magic rite (Fruchtbarkeitszauber) based on the belief that human and divine procreative power, when exercised, directly influences the fertility of the earth. Originally the Basileus himself was the Basilinna's partner; Dionysos took over this role when his cult arrived. Deubner explicitly says this was not politically motivated but religiously: the great fertility god who had won hearts was to place his overwhelming abundance in the service of the land.

**Notes**:
- Controversy over whether Dionysos was represented by a person or a statue: Deubner, following Farnell (p. 217) and Pfuhl (*De pompis* 70), argues for a living representative — most likely the Basileus himself. Reasons: (a) the satyrs on the ship-wagon must be persons; (b) no seated idol was brought to the Basilinna; (c) the rite predates cult-images; (d) the New York kanna shows a boy, not a statue.
- Nilsson doubted the Hieros Gamos belonged to the Choes (Jahrb. 31, 1916; later more reserved); Buschor connected the New York kanna to the ephebes' escort of Dionysos Eleuthereus at the City Dionysia. Deubner rebuts both in detail.
- The 14 Geraeren: their number has been linked to the 14 Daidala burnt by 14 Boeotian cities at the Hera festival — Deubner rejects this as a groundless use of an unknown parallel.
- The word γεραιραί (or γεραράδες): Deubner follows Wackernagel in deriving from γεραρός ("the Venerable Ones"), against Nilsson's derivation from γεραίρειν.
- Dispute over whether the secret precinct entered by the Basilinna alone was the Bukoleion or a room within the Limnaion: Deubner argues for a room (οἶκος) inside the Limnaion itself, from which she fetched the sacred objects; the Bukoleion was only the final location of the consummation. This resolves Nilsson's earlier objections.
- Deubner reviews and dismisses van Hoorn's argument that a Hermes-pillar (not a person) represented Dionysos in the Hieros Gamos.

#### 4. Dark Side of the Choes: Dead Souls

**Ritual**:
1. The dead came up (ἀναβαίνειν) to the upper world on the Choes day.
2. In the morning, people chewed whitethorn (ῥάμνος), a known apotropaic.
3. Doors were smeared with pitch to repel souls (its sharp smell was believed to keep them away).
4. Temples were cordoned off with ropes (as at the Plynteria) to protect them from unwanted visitors.
5. The day was therefore called a ἡμέρα μιαρά ("polluted day") or ἀποφράς.
6. No weddings during the Anthesteria (the ill-omen lingered; parallel in Corfu: February weddings bring bad luck).

**Participants**: All Athenians.
**Sources**:
- Photius s.v. μιαρὰ ἡμέρα (souls of the dead ascend in Anthesterion; chew ῥάμνος in morning; smear doors with pitch)
- Hesychius s.v. μιαραὶ ἡμέραι
- Schol. Aristoph. *Ach.* 961 (ἀποφράδες ἡμέραι)
- Alkiphron 4.18.11; Pollux 8.141 (ropes around temples)
- Aristophanes, *Frogs* 218ff. (drunken crowd at the sacred Chytren/Limnaion area on the night of the 12th)
- Ovid, *Fasti* 2.563; 5.485f. (Roman parallel: temples closed at soul-festivals)

**Notes**: The totenkundliche (funerary) element combines with the Dionysiac on the Choes, but is completely dominant on the Chytroi. Deubner rejects the claim (Schol. Aristoph. *Ach.* 961) that the Choes involved actual τοίαι (libations for the dead) — he says this rests on a confusion of χοῦς (pitcher) and χοαί (libations), which coincide in the genitive.

#### 5. Children's Festival on the Choes

**Ritual**:
1. Children in their third year of life were crowned with a wreath of flowers (Philostratos, *Heroic.* 12.2) — a ritual "confirmation" (confirmatio, Deubner's term).
2. The crowning with flowers was intended to transmit the powers of the newly stirring vegetation and protect the child against every misfortune.
3. Children received gift-choes (miniature choes-jugs, Kinderkännchen, hundreds are preserved) and little wagons (ἁμαξίδες) as presents.
4. Other children's activities at the Anthesteria: boxing contests (Hesych. Λιμνομαχία), torch-races (IG 3.93; Taf. 16,3), running with a full jug, athletic events, lyre-playing contests (with tripod as prize), pyrrhic dance, young actors' performances.

**Participants**: Children in their third year; older children also participated in agones.

**Sources**:
- Philostratos, *Heroic.* 12.2 (third-year-olds crowned with flowers at the Anthesteria in Anthesterion)
- IG 2² 1368.130 (Iobakchen contribution-list: γάμου, γεννήσεως, χοῦν, ἐφηβείας — the Choes-initiation placed between birth and ephebia)
- IG 3.1342 (epitaph: "he reached the Choes-age, but the daimon got ahead of the Choes")
- Proklos to Plato, *Tim.* 21b (Kureotis of Apaturia: boys aged 3 or 4 inscribed in phratry lists)
- Vases: Taf. 13,4 (boy with wreath of buds and white flowers, chous in right hand, wagon in left); Taf. 16,1 (relief: boy who died before his Anthesteria-crowning); Taf. 16,2 (infant crawling near gift table with chous and fruit)
- Hesychius s.v. Λιμνομαχία (children's boxing contests)
- IG 3.93 (torch-race in Anthesterion)

**Notes**: Deubner connects the Choesconfirmation to the phratry registration (Kureotis) in autumn: children crowned at the Choes (spring, 3rd or 4th year) and registered in the phratry at the following Apaturia — the age data match precisely. Parallel: the Vedic Cūḍākaraṇa ceremony in the third year.

**Deubner**: Choes overall: printed p. 95–116 / PDF approx. p. 104–125

### Chytroi
**Month/Day**: Anthesterion 13
**Deity**: Hermes Chthonios (exclusively; no Dionysiac rite on this day)
**Ritual**:
1. Pots (χύτροι) containing a mixture of all kinds of seeds (παμπέρματα) boiled together were set out for chthonic Hermes.
2. Prayer to Hermes on behalf of the dead (the first known European intercessory prayer for the dead, as Farnell noted).
3. No one could eat from the offering (Theopompus explicitly states this was so even the survivors of the Flood).
4. Hydrophoria (water-libations) for the victims of the Kataklusmos (Deucalion's flood): water offerings were poured into the χάσμα ("chasm") in the sanctuary of the Olympian Zeus, where the flood-waters were said to have drained away; honey-cakes (ἄλφιτα with honey) were also thrown in annually.
5. At the end of the festival, the expulsion-cry was uttered: **θύραζε, κῆρες, οὐκέτ' Ἀνθεστήρια** ("Out of doors, Keres, the Anthesteria are over!"), driving away the souls who had been roaming the city.
6. Comedy contests (κωμικοὶ ἀγῶνες) are attested for the Chytroi ([Plut.] *Vit. X orat.* 841f).

**Aition**: Theopompus gave the aition: after the Kataklusmos (Deucalion's flood), the survivors cooked seed-pottage in pots and offered it to chthonic Hermes to propitiate him for the drowned. Deubner identifies the Hydrophoria with the Chytroi, citing IG 2² 1367.22 (a sacrifice "into the Loutron" in Anthesterion) and Pausanias 1.18.7 (the chasm in the Olympeion).

**Participants**: All Athenians. The festival was exclusively for the dead (no Dionysiac component on this day — "An dem Lichttage der Chytren hat keine rituelle Handlung mehr im Dienste des Dionysos stattgefunden").

**Sources**:
- Schol. Rav. Aristoph. *Ach.* 1076 (= Suid. s.v. Χύτροι): Theopompus on the seed-pottage and the aition; no one may eat; offered to Hermes chthonios
- Schol. Aristoph. *Frö.* 218 (χύτροι)
- Photius (= Suid.) s.v. Ὑδροφόρια: "a mourning festival in Athens for those who drowned in the Flood, according to Apollonios"
- Hesychius and Etymologicum Magnum 774.56 s.v. Ὑδροφόρια
- Photius s.v. θύραζε Κάρες (full text of the expulsion-cry and its variants; discussion of whether Κῆρες or Κᾶρες is original)
- Hesychius s.v. θύραζε; Zenobios 4.33 (expulsion-cry)
- Pausanias 1.18.7 (Olympeion chasm for the flood-waters, annual honey-cake offering)
- IG 2² 1367.22 (Prott's sacrifice "into the Loutron" in Anthesterion, connected to the Hydrophoria)
- IG 2² 2130 (192/3 CE): agoranomoi overseeing the Chytroi market
- [Plut.] *Vit. X orat.* 841f (comedy contests at the Chytroi)

**Notes**:
- The expulsion-cry: Deubner defends κῆρες ("souls / death-spirits") against the variant Κᾶρες ("Carians"), which he says is a later misreading used to refer to the slaves who participated in the festival and now are told to go back to work. He argues the cry's connection to the roaming souls, sustained by the festival's customs, could not have been lost or reinterpreted while those customs remained alive. Wilamowitz (*Glaube d. Hell.* 1.272) argued κῆρες here means "ghosts" rather than "souls" — Deubner disagrees, citing ethnological parallels.
- Deubner dismisses (Schol. Aristoph. *Ach.* 961) the claim that the Choes involved actual χοαί (libations for the dead) — a confusion of χοῦς and χοαί in the genitive.
- The Hydrophoria is not otherwise directly attested as part of the Chytren ritual; the connection rests on the interpretation of Photius and the Olympeion chasm evidence.
- Comedy contests at the Chytroi: [Plut.] *Vit. X orat.* 841f. Deubner notes (fn. 76) that Philostrat. *v. Apoll.* 4.21, which mentions comic and tragic performances during the Anthesteria in Imperial times, is placed by Wilamowitz at the City Dionysia — Deubner disagrees (Thucydides already calls the Anthesteria τὰ ἀρχαιότερα Διονύσια).
- The festival name "Anthesteria": Deubner accepts Fraenkel's derivation from the verb ἀνθεῖν (to bloom) ← *ἀνθέω (via *ἀνθέσω), based on the role of flowers in the festival (children crowned with flower-wreaths). He rejects Verrall's derivation from θέσθαι / ἀναθέσσασθαι ("to supplicate" the dead) on both linguistic and factual grounds: linguistically, Attic-Ionic does not regularly syncopate ἀνα-; factually, the Athenians took every measure to keep the dead at bay, not summon them.

## Structural Summary (Deubner's Three-Layer Analysis)

Deubner identifies three strata in the Anthesteria:

1. **Dionysiac** (Dionysos's arrival from the sea, wine-opening, Hieros Gamos) — concentrated on Pithoigia and Choes.
2. **Chthonic / soul-festival** (the dead ascend; apotropaic measures; Chytren offerings) — concentrated on Choes (dark aspect) and Chytroi (entirely).
3. **Blessing-rites / vegetation** (children crowned with flower-wreaths; the Hieros Gamos as fertility-magic; the spring theme) — cutting across all three days.

He frames the Dionysiac layer theologically: Dionysos arrives from the sea (Lydian / eastern origin; the ship-wagon reenacts his first arrival). The Hieros Gamos predates Dionysos (originally Basileus as partner) and was absorbed into the Dionysiac layer when the cult arrived.

The order of events on the Choes as Deubner reconstructs it:
- Morning: Ship-wagon procession → arrival at Limnaion → wedding-ceremony (ἀπόρρητα ἱερά + oath of Geraeren + ἐξεδόθη) → wedding procession with stylis from Limnaion to Bukoleion → consummation of Hieros Gamos in Bukoleion.
- Afternoon/evening: Drinking contest (with trumpet, in Thesmotheteion officially; privately everywhere) → garlands brought to the Basilinna/priestess at the Limnaion → wine dregs libated.
- Night (beginning of the 13th by ancient reckoning): The drunken crowd moved through the city toward the Limnaion area (Aristoph. *Frogs* 218ff.).
