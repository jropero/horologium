# Deubner — Kronos, Zeus, Hera chapter
Source: L. Deubner, *Attische Feste* (1932), printed p. 152–178 / PDF p. 165–192
Extracted: 2026-10-25

---

### Kronia
**Month/Day**: Hekatombaion 12
**Deity**: Kronos (and Rhea / Göttermutter jointly)
**Ritual**:
- Masters of household and their slaves dine together (joint banquet) after the completion of the harvest
- General festivity with loud merriment (laute Lustigkeit)
- Cakes in animal form (ναυστός, a rare bread-shape in ox form) are attested in a sacrifice calendar for a related occasion; Deubner takes this as suggesting that a bloody sacrifice for Kronos cannot be entirely excluded
**Aition**: None explicitly discussed by Deubner.
**Participants**: Heads of household with their slaves; celebrated both in the countryside and in town
**Sources**:
- Demosthenes 24,26 (date: 12th Hekatombaion)
- Schol. Aristoph. Clouds 397; Hesych s.v. Κρόνια (month/general)
- Pausanias 1,18,7 (shared Athenian temple of Kronos and Rhea)
- Etym. m. 321,4; Plut. Thes. 12,2 (month formerly called Kronion)
- Philochoros (via Macrob. Sat. 1,10,22): masters and slaves feast together after harvest
- Accius frg. 3 (ed. Morel p. 34): Roman Saturnalia derived from Attic Kronia
- Phot. Κρόνια
**Notes**:
- The name Kronion for Hekatombaion is attested across the Ionic dialect region; Nilsson calls it merely a hypothesis (RE 11, 1976, 9ff.), but Deubner and Wilamowitz (Berl. Sitz.-Ber. 1929, 36) consider the tradition sound.
- Accius' claim that "most Greeks" (maxima pars Graium) celebrated the Kronia is tendentiously exaggerated (Preller-Robert, Pohlenz).
- The element of slaves being served by masters (famulos procurant) in Accius reflects a Roman Saturnalia feature, not an Attic one (Pohlenz).
- Wilamowitz proposed an Elaphebolion 15 sacrifice was the same Kronos cult borrowed from Olympia (Paus. 6,20,1); Nilsson objects that the Olympia feast has a solar fixation too late to be original. Deubner leaves the question of Kronos' origin aside.
- Wilamowitz denied that Kronos had independent divine status ("only something in relation to Zeus"); Deubner calls this untenable. Most scholars now assume Kronos is a pre-Greek deity (name unhellenisch): Pohlenz, Farnell (Essays Evans 24), Nilsson.
**Deubner**: printed p. 152–153 / PDF p. 165–166

### Diasia
**Month/Day**: Anthesterion 23 (= ἡ φθίνοντος, 8th from month-end)
**Deity**: Zeus Meilichios
**Ritual**:
- Celebrated outside the city, probably in the area of the Ilissos
- Holocausts (whole burnt offerings) of sacrificial animals: the animals are burned entirely (ὁλοκαυτεῖν), as befits a chthonic deity
- The common people (πανδημεί) sacrifice not animals but ἁγνὰ θύματα (unbloody offerings), which the scholiasts interpret as cakes molded in animal shapes; Deubner notes the scholiast's interpretation is questionable but the unbloody gifts were likewise burned completely
- Private banquets held alongside the gloomy public rite; Hermes in Lukian recalls "the hecatomb banquets" Timon used to give at the Diasia (Lukian Timon 7); Strepsiades in Aristophanes hosts relatives at this feast
- Children receive gifts: Strepsiades buys the young Pheidippides a small toy cart (ἁμαξίδα) at the Diasia — similar to the carts depicted on Choes jugs
- Possible literary agon with a garland of ears of grain as prize (Charidemos 1, pseudo-Lukian); Deubner considers this very doubtful
**Aition**: None discussed by Deubner.
**Participants**: All Athenians (πανδημεί); private families
**Character**: Gloomy (σκυθρωπός), solemn; yet private joy exists alongside the public solemnity
**Sources**:
- Thuc. 1,126,6 (largest Zeus-festival of Athens, Zeus Meilichios, σεμνή, πανδημεί, ἁγνὰ θύματα)
- Xen. Anab. 7,8,4–6 (Xenophon sacrifices pigs by holocaust to Zeus Meilichios; god's blessing follows)
- Aristoph. Clouds 408f.; 864 (family banquets, toy cart gift)
- Lukian Timon 7 (Timon's hecatomb banquets at the Diasia)
- Schol. Aristoph. Clouds 408; Schol. Knights 445
- Schol. Lukian Ikarom. 24 S. 107 Rabe; Schol. Lukian Timon 7 S. 110 (σκυθρωπός character)
- Poll. 1,37; Suid. Διάσια; Etym. m. 270,14
- [pseudo-Lys.] 6,53 (φαρμακός metaphor)
- Charidemos 1 (disputed agon)
- Novel of Eustathios Hysmine (late echo of nocturnal private sacrifice at Diasia; Deubner cautions)
**Notes**:
- The festival was neglected by the 2nd century CE: Lukian Ikarom. 24 has Zeus ask Menippos why the Athenians no longer celebrate the Diasia.
- Deubner interprets the dual character (gloomy public rite + private jollity) as reflecting awareness that the chthonic deity's demands have been met, and thus his grace is assured. Or it reflects the double nature of underworld gods (dreadful yet beneficent).
- Wilamowitz (Glaube d. Hell. 1, 227f.) identifies Zeus Meilichios with Zeus Maimaktes as originally the same weather-god; Deubner rejects this.
- Mommsen, Skias, Robert for sanctuary location near Ilissos.
**Deubner**: printed p. 155–157 / PDF p. 168–170

### Pompaia
**Month/Day**: Maimakterion, last third of the month (φθίνοντος Μαιμακτηριῶνος); related private calendar entry on Maimakterion 20 (IG 2a 1367,12)
**Deity**: Zeus Meilichios (connection described as secondary; the diòn kōdion is the primary element)
**Ritual**:
- A procession (πομπή) in which the following objects are carried by hand:
  1. The διόν κώδιον: a fleece from an animal sacrificed to Zeus Meilichios
  2. A κηρύκειον (herald's staff)
  3. Possibly a λέβης (cauldron) of Hermes
- The procession moved presumably around the newly plowed and sown fields as a magical circuit (περιχάρωσις / apotropaic circumambulation) to protect the buried grain
- Purificatory material (καθαρμοί) was thrown out at crossroads (ἐκβολαὶ καθαρμῶν εἰς τὰς τριόδους) — separate from the procession itself
- The name Pompaia derives from πομπός (the herald's staff carried in the procession)
**Aition**: None discussed by Deubner.
**Participants**: Civic (state) procession
**Sources**:
- Eust. Od. 22,481 S. 1935,5 (διόν κώδιον, kerykeion, cauldron; ἐκβολαί at crossroads; the name Pompaia)
- IG 2a 1367,12: private sacrifice calendar, Maimakterion — Zeus Γεωργός receives popanon (12-knopped, 1 choinix), nauσtos cake (with ox shape), panspermia, nephalia (wineless libation)
- Hes. Erga 465ff. (prayer to Zeus Chthonios at plowing; Zeus Meilichios closely related)
- De Waele, The Magic Staff 29ff. (kerykeion as apotropaic)
- Harrison, Proleg. 46 (fleece)
**Notes**:
- The fleece (κώδιον) was originally a general magical/purificatory object; the association with Zeus Meilichios is secondary.
- The Pompaia belongs to a set of rituals connected with the critical period of early winter sowing.
- Deubner rejects De Waele's explanation of the Pompaia as the ceremonies of "sending away" (the kerykeion as a "sending-rod").
- Panspermia in the private calendar entry: Deubner treats it as an offering, not a magical rite (against Nilsson, Griech. Feste 115).
- Not connected to the Maimakteria (festival of Zeus Maimaktes; see below).
**Deubner**: printed p. 157–158 / PDF p. 170–171

### Dipolieia (and Buphonia)
**Month/Day**: Skirophorion 14
**Deity**: Zeus Polieus; Athena Polias also receives a sacrifice (attested in the Aristophanes scholion; parallels on Kos, Rhodes, Telos)
**Ritual** (authentic version per Pausanias, which Deubner regards as the reliable core):
1. A mixture of barley and wheat grains is laid on the altar of Zeus Polieus on the Akropolis, without guard
2. A pre-prepared (cleansed and adorned) ox is led near the altar; when it eats from the grain it has committed a transgression (violated the sacred offering)
3. A βουφόνος ("ox-slayer," from the Thaulonidai gens) is called; he strikes the ox, then immediately throws down the axe and flees
4. The slaughterer is unknown (by fiction); the axe is put on trial (in the Prytaneion, as was customary for inanimate objects)
5. The ox is slaughtered, skinned, and eaten (the festival involved a sacrificial meal)
6. The ox-hide is stuffed with straw and set back on its feet, yoked to a plough, as if still alive
7. A formal trial is convened in which the various participants shift the blame down a chain: water-bearers (ὑδροφόροι) → sharpeners → axe-passer → slaughterer → knife; the knife is "convicted" of the killing
8. The knife is thrown into the sea
**Aition**: Deubner explicitly discusses the aition(s):
- *Primary (authentic) legend*: At the festival, an ox ate from the grain or the sacred cake on the altar. Thaulon (eponymous ancestor of the Thaulonidai) struck it and fled. His flight is the origin of the ritualized flight of the βουφόνος. (Sources: Aristoph. schol. Clouds 985; Androtion's Atthis cited there)
- *Secondary (Porphyrios, first version)*: Diomos, priest of Zeus Polieus, slew an ox that ate from the sacred cake; he is named instead of the βουτύπος, a clear error or secondary variant. Deubner judges this version as having no documentary value.
- *Sopatros legend (Porphyrios, second version, ultimately from Theophrastos)*: A foreign farmer named Sopatros slays an ox that ate from the offerings, flees to Crete; Delphi instructs Athens to punish the killer, revive the dead animal symbolically, and eat it. Sopatros agrees if the polis shares the deed collectively. The elaborate ritual results. Deubner extensively analyzes this version as a later literary construction (not genuine cult), with numerous internal inconsistencies (the Diomos confusion, the missing condemnation of the πατάξας, the malfitting oracle-instruction about punishment, the fabricated three priest-families). The Kultangaben in this version are in Deubner's view not factual but invented on the basis of the new legend.
**Participants**:
- Thaulonidai (noble gens; attested: Hesych; IG l² 839; they supply the βουτύπος)
- Kentriadai (a true genos; Phot.: πατριὰ Κηρύκων; attested in connection with Dipolieia heralds: IG l² 843, c. 420 BCE; Deubner: their driving of the oxen gave rise to the name)
- Daítroi (carvers/distributors; a cult title, not a genos per Deubner)
- IG 2² 2291a, 2128, 2129: hiereus bouttypos in imperial-period inscriptions
- A δῆμος Σκαμβωνίδαι may have held a meat-distribution at its agora (IG; uncertain)
**Sources**:
- Paus. 1,24,4 (core ritual); 1,28,10 (axe trial in Prytaneion; institution under Erechtheus)
- Aristoph. Clouds 984 (the Dipolieia as archaic, τετ-τίγων ἀνάμεστα)
- Schol. Aristoph. Clouds 985; Schol. Peace 419 (= Hes. Διιπόλεια); Suid. Βουφόνια, Θαύλων; Hesych Βουφόνια
- Etym. m. 210,30 (Buphonia, date Skirophorion 14; many oxen)
- Bekker Anecd. 1,238,21ff. (variant: 16th Skirophorion — Deubner rejects)
- Porphyr. de abst. 2,10 (Diomos version); 2,28 = S. 158,11–160,21 Nauck (Sopatros legend and cult)
- Ael. v.h. 8,3 (trial; axe or knife condemned; uses both festival-names)
- Androtion's Atthis (Thaulon cited in Aristoph. schol.)
- Hesych Θαυλωνίδαι; Hesych βουτύπος; Hesych βουτηές (confused with βουτύπος)
- IG l² 839 (archaic boustrophedon inscription: βουτύπος and Dipolieia)
- IG l² 843 (κῆρυ[ξ] at Dipolieia, c. 420 BCE)
- Scholia T to Il. 18,483 (Thaulon)
- Wackernagel, Rh. Mus. 45 (1890) 480ff. (correct name-form Dipolieia, from dative Αἱ Πολιεί)
**Notes**:
- The form Διώπολια (Wilamowitz) is a mistake; Dipolia is also wrong (Wackernagel).
- Deubner extensively reviews and rejects competing interpretations:
  - Totemism (Robertson Smith, Farnell): no valid totemistic elements in Greece.
  - Vegetation-daemon theory (Mannhardt, Frazer, Nilsson, Rostagni, Gebhard, Bickermann, Schweitzer): the vegetation-daemon is never killed with reluctance; no compelling analogies.
  - Human-sacrifice substitute (Prott): unsubstantiated.
  - Laum's elaboration: original slaughter of a Taurosmyste (speculative).
  - Schwenn's hunter-society parallel: the festival is agricultural; the ox is not a hunt animal; Nilsson rightly rejects it.
  - Stengel's "apology for converting unbloody cult to bloody": Deubner says the grain on the altar is the ox-lure within the rite, not the residue of an earlier unbloody cult.
- Deubner's own explanation (following K. Fr. Hermann and Wilamowitz): the rite reflects the plowman's guilt at killing his fellow-laborer, the plow-ox. Parallels: ancient law (Buzygean curse) prohibited slaughtering a plow-ox. The dramatic ceremony externalizes and distributes the guilt.
- The Dipolieia cannot date to the Mycenaean period: Zeus Polieus on the Akropolis is secondary to Athena; the cult cannot predate the archaic period.
- Possible meat distribution to the deme Skambonidai discussed but not firmly established.
**Deubner**: printed p. 158–173 / PDF p. 171–186

### Diisoteria
**Month/Day**: Skirophorion, before the 20th (decrees honoring festival officials are dated 20 and 28 Skirophorion, so the festival preceded them; appears after Bendideia of Thargelion 19 in the hide-sale accounts: IG 2² 1496)
**Deity**: Zeus Soter (primary) and Athena Soteira (joint); also offerings to Asklepios, Hygieia, and their circle
**Location**: Piraeus (main celebration at the Diosoteriον / Διασωτήριον)
**Ritual** (Piraeus festival):
1. Procession (πομπή) from the city to the Piraeus; the Agoranomoi of the Piraeus were required to prepare the roads for it
2. Epheboi participate in the procession and lead a sacrificial bull (IG 2² 1030,23)
3. A kanephore (basket-bearer) included with great probability (IG 2, 1387)
4. Rich animal sacrifices at the sanctuary; high hide-income in state accounts (IG 2² 1496 A a 88f., b 118f.)
5. Sacrifices also to Asklepios, Hygieia, and other related deities (IG 2² 783,5)
6. A lekisternion (reclining feast for images of the gods) — at least in one documented extraordinary celebration (IG 2² 676)
7. The festival concludes with an ephebe regatta (IG 2² 1006,30)
- Administration: Archon Eponymos (Aristot. Ath. Pol. 56,5); Epimeletes appointed for sacrifice arrangements (IG 2, 1358 VII; IG 2² 676; 783)
**City festival on Skirophorion 30** (distinct from the Diisoteria):
- A modest state sacrifice by the priest of Zeus Soter on the Agora before the Stoa of Zeus
- Sacrifices to Zeus Soter and Athena Soteira "for the health of the Boule" (IG 2a 689; 690)
- Called εἰσιτήρια (entering-offerings), purpose: to place the coming new year under the protection of the divine Savior
- No sanctuary of Zeus Soter in Athens proper; only an altar near the Stoa
- Never called "Diisoteria"
**Sources**:
- Aristot. Ath. Pol. 56,5 (archon responsible)
- IG 2² 1006,30; 1008,21 (epheboi at Diisoteria)
- IG 2² 1030,23 (sacrificial bull)
- IG 2, 1387 (kanephore, supplemented)
- IG 2² 783,5 (epimeletes, offerings to Asklepios etc.)
- IG 2² 676 (epimeletes, lekisternion)
- IG 2² 1496 A a 88f., b 118f. (hide accounts: high yield from Zeus Soter sacrifice)
- Bekker Anecd. 1,91,6 (sanctuary name Διασωτήριον)
- Lys. 26,6ff. (state expenditure; Demosthenes incident)
- [Plut.] v. X orat. 846b; Plut. Demosth. 27,8 (Demosthenes incident)
- IG 2a 689 (262/1 BCE); 690 (εἰσιτήρια)
- Pfuhl, De pompis 96 (procession route to Asklepieion)
**Notes**:
- The name "Diisoteria" is a later form; the expected older form would be Διασωτήρια (analogous to Dipolieia ← Dative Αἱ Πολιεί). The attested form first appears in documents from 122 BCE onward (IG 2² 1006; 2, 1358 VII).
- The Piraeus Diisoteria belong structurally to the state in the same way as the piräischen Dionysia.
- The rich Rindsopfer (cattle sacrifices) of the Diisoteria likely contributed to the false lexicographical explanation of the Buphonia name (Prott).
- The two festivals (Piraeus Diisoteria and Athens εἰσιτήρια) are entirely separate; they share only the deity.
**Deubner**: printed p. 173–176 / PDF p. 186–189

## Andere Feste (Other festivals in the Kronos–Zeus–Hera chapter)

### Maimakteria
**Month/Day**: Maimakterion (month named after the festival; no day attested)
**Deity**: Zeus Μαιμάκτης ("the raging/stormy one," from μαιμάω/μαιμάσσω)
**Ritual**: Unknown. Deubner supposes the festival was concerned with averting damage from the onset of winter storms.
**Sources**:
- Month-name inference: Meursius (Graecia feriata 6, Gronov. Thea. 7,820e)
- Lysimachides ap. Harpokr. (Suid.) s.v. Μαιμακτηρίων
- Plut. de coh. ira 458c (epithet Μαιμάκτης)
- Hesych μαίμακτ· ταραχώδης (meaning of epithet)
**Notes**:
- Deubner: the festival must once have been important enough to give its name to the month; almost nothing is preserved.
- Hesych's equation of Μαιμάκτης with Μειλίχιος and καθαρσιός is an incorrect identification of Zeus Maimaktes with Zeus Meilichios (Deubner, and contra Wilamowitz Glaube d. Hell. 1,227,2 who reads Plutarch as supporting that view; Deubner rejects this).
- Not connected to the Pompaia (above).
**Deubner**: printed p. 176 / PDF p. 189

### Pandia
**Month/Day**: A few days after the great Dionysia (Elaphebolion); Wilamowitz suggests fullmoon day (Glaube d. Hell. 1,258,2)
**Deity**: Zeus (name Pandia = "all-festival of Zeus")
**Ritual**:
- The day after the Pandia: a mandatory popular assembly in the theater of Dionysos at which complaints about the preceding festival could be lodged (Demosth. 21,8)
- The phyle Pandionis celebrated specially (IG 2² 1140); a decree of that phyle honoring the priest of hero Pandion was passed in the assembly after the Pandia
- The demos Plotheia also celebrated the Pandia (IG 2² 1172,9)
**Sources**:
- Phot. Πάνδια; Etym. m. 651,22ff.; Poll. 1,37
- Demosth. 21,8 (assembly after Pandia)
- IG 2² 1140 (phyle Pandionis decree)
- IG 2² 1172,9 (demos Plotheia)
- IG 2² 1496 A a 82f., b 113f. (hide accounts: Olympieia, not Pandia specifically, but sequence given)
**Notes**:
- The name Pandion of the Attic king is probably derived from the festival name, not vice versa (Deubner).
- The festival's significance faded early, overshadowed by the immediately preceding Dionysia.
- Wilamowitz earlier (Kydathen 133) regarded the festival as aristocratic; later revised to democratic (Berl. Sitz.-Ber. 1925, 237).
**Deubner**: printed p. 176–177 / PDF p. 189–190

### Olympieia
**Month/Day**: Munichion 19 (Plut. Phok. 37,1: cavalry escort the procession on the 19th of Munichion; confirmed by position in hide-accounts IG 2² 1496 between Elaphebolion Dionysia and Thargelion Bendideia)
**Deity**: Zeus Olympios
**Ritual**:
- A πομπή in which the cavalry (ἱππεῖς) participated
- A horse/cavalry agon (ἱππικὸς ἀγών) attested
- Agonistic Olympia attested in inscriptions of the imperial period (none older than Hadrian)
**Sources**:
- Plut. Phok. 37,1 (cavalry procession, Munichion 19)
- IG 2² 1496 A a 82f., b 113f. (hide accounts: Olympieia between Dionysia and Bendideia)
- Judeich, Topogr. v. Athen² 382f. (temple foundation)
**Notes**:
- The Pisistratids laid the foundations for the great Olympieion in place of an older simpler sanctuary; Hadrian completed the temple. The agonistic Olympia of the imperial period probably represent a renewal of the festival on the occasion of the temple's completion.
**Deubner**: printed p. 176–177 / PDF p. 189–190

### Theogamia / Gamelia (Hieros Gamos)
**Month/Day**: Gamelion, ca. 24th (Menander frg. 320, CAF 3,92 refers to the 24th of a month as the day of the Hieros Gamos, combined with the scholion to Hesiod which places Theogamia toward the end of a month)
**Deity**: Zeus and Hera (their sacred marriage, ἱερὸς γάμος)
**Ritual**:
- A sacrifice to Zeus Ἥραιος on the Akropolis (IG l² 840,20: "Διὶ Ἡραίῳ κοῦρος" — a piglet), recorded in an old, probably public, sacrifice calendar under Gamelion; Deubner regards this singular epithet as connected with the Hieros Gamos
- Athenians chose the "days approaching the new moon" as favorable for marriage, and performed the Theogamia then (Schol. Hesiod Erga 780)
- Month Gamelion named from Gamelia (a feast), which Deubner identifies with the Theogamia
**Sources**:
- Schol. Hesiod Erga 780 (Theogamia at end of month, favorable for marriages)
- Phot. ἱερὸς γάμος; Hesych ἱερὸς γάμος; Etym. m. 468,56f. (Zeus and Hera's sacred marriage called so)
- Hesych Γαμηλιών· ὁ τῶν μηνῶν τῆς Ἥρας ἱερός
- Nilsson, Entstehung d. gr. Kalenders 52 (month from feast)
- IG l² 840,20 (Gamelion: Διὶ Ἡραίῳ κοῦρος)
- Menander frg. 320, CAF 3,92 (24th as day of Hieros Gamos)
- Prott, Fasti sacri S. 4 (Zeus Heraios and the Hieros Gamos)
- Schmidt-Rühl, Handb. d. Chronol. 523ff.; Scheuer, De Iunone Attica 20f.
**Notes**:
- Deubner identifies Theogamia and Gamelia as the same festival.
- The singular epithet Zeus Ἥραιος ("Zeus in relation to Hera") is explained by the Hieros Gamos context.
- The festival is attested very indirectly; the date of Gamelion 24 is Deubner's reconstruction from Menander and the scholion.
**Deubner**: printed p. 177–178 / PDF p. 190–191
