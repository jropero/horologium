### Haloa

**Month/Day**: Posideon 26 (Photios: Ποσειδεῶνος πέμπτῃ φθίνοντος = 26th). Confirmed by: Philochoros; the accounting inscription IG² 1672 (Prytanies consistent with Posideon); Himerios or. 8,3 (winter solstice, when Demeter and Bakchos "meet"). Nilsson had narrowed the date to the second half of the month; Koehler and Pfuhl argued for month end.
**Deity**: Demeter and Kore (primary); a τελετή to them specifically at Eleusis. Dionysos named as co-lord in the Lukian scholion (279,24f.) and by the Atticist Pausanias at Eustathios — but Deubner considers this dubious: the inscriptions of the 3rd–2nd c. BCE (IG² 1299; 949) name only Demeter, Kore, and "the other gods to whom it is traditional" (τοῖς ἄλλοις οἷς πάτριον ἦν). Wine-drinking and phallos use led later to association with Dionysos; the Ikarosaition in the scholion is a secondary, erroneous transfer from the rural Dionysia.
**Ritual**:
- Exclusively a women's festival; men explicitly excluded
- At Eleusis a τελετή (mystery rite) was performed; this is the locus of all that the Lukian scholion describes (280,12–13)
- Terracotta phalloi were erected (and venerated) in the fields as a fertility charm stuck into the sprouting seed (red-figure pelike in British Museum, pl. 3,3; also vessels and Berlin krater pl. 4,2 and Castellani cup pl. 4,1)
- Women wore models of male and female genitalia as pendants and handled similar backware on the table
- Widespread αἰσχρολογία (obscene speech) and display of obscene objects
- Priestesses secretly approached the women and whispered incitement to illicit love as "secret advice" (280,16–17)
- A great banquet: tables covered with everything the earth and sea could provide — except forbidden items (see below). Wine in abundance
- Forbidden foods at the τελετή (280,22–24): pomegranate (fallen kernels), apple, domestic fowl, eggs, and from the sea: mullet (τρίγλη), erythinoi (μελάνουρος), γαλεός (a shark), κάραβος (crayfish)
- Hetaerae participated heavily: they attended the nocturnal festival (παννυχίς), reclined with lovers, received festival money from patrons; they even had access to the Eleusinian sanctuary, while being excluded from the Thesmophorion
- A pompé for Poseidon was also connected with the festival (Eustathios / Pausanias)
- Officials (ἄρχοντες) arranged the banquet but had to withdraw after; the meal for the πολῖται could be held outside the sanctuary
- The only inscriptionally attested animal sacrifice at Eleusis: Hierophant Archias illegally sacrificed (for the hetaira Sinope) — normally only women sacrificed and the Ackergöttin refused blood sacrifice ([Demosth.] 59,116)
**Aition**: The Lukian scholion (280,3–11) attaches the Ikarosaition (Dionysos / Ikarios / phallos-offerings by shepherds) to explain the terracotta phalloi. Deubner explicitly rejects this as secondary: the Ikarosaition originally belonged to the rural Dionysia of Ikaria, not to the Demeter festival; it was wrongly transferred because both featured clay phalloi.
**Participants**: Women only (for the τελετή and banquet); hetaerae prominently; men strictly excluded from the rite proper; officials and the general citizenry (πολῖται) invited to the sacrificial meal which could take place outside the precinct
**Sources**:
- Philochoros (month: Posideon; etymology: ἁλωά = "cultivated land"): cited in Harpokration (᾽Αλῶα); Bekker Anecd. 1,381,14ff.
- Photios (s.v. Ἁλῶα): Posideon 26; text via Reitzenstein
- IG² 1672 (great accounts inscription): lines 124, 143 (access ramp, tribüne for Haloa expenses)
- Lukian scholion (S. 279–281 Rabe), published by Rohde — main source for ritual content
- IG² 1299 (time of Demetrios II, 239–229 BCE): Strategos Aristophanes' offerings at the Haloa to Demeter and Kore
- IG² 949 (c. 165/4 BCE): Eleusinian demarch's offerings at the Haloa
- IG 3,895 (fragmentary Eleusinian inscription): Eumolpidai and Eleusis connected with the Haloa
- Himerios or. 8,3 (winter solstice, Demeter and Bakchos)
- Eustathios Il. 9,530 S.772,26 (Pausanias the Atticist on Dionysos at the Haloa — doubted by Deubner)
- [Demosthenes] 59,116 (the Archias incident: illegal sacrifice in the ἀυλή at Eleusis)
- Menander's Glykera (Alkiphr. 4,18,4): hetaira in Athens for the Haloa
- Alkiphron 4,6,3 (pannychis at the Haloa)
- Lukian dial. mer. 1,1; 7,4 (hetaerae and the Haloa)
- Red-figure pelike British Museum E 819 (pl. 3,3): fertility magic with phalloi in sprouting grain
- Berlin fragment cup pl. 3,2; Castellani cup (Villa Giulia Inv. 50404, pl. 4,1)
- Nilsson, *De Dionys. att.* 40; *Griech. Feste* 329
- Rubensohn, *Mysterienheiligtümer* 117
**Notes**:
- Deubner stresses that the Dionysiac connection is spurious at the historical level: wine and phalloi were superficially similar to Dionysiac festivals, and the Haloa was calendrically adjacent to the rural Dionysia. Only in later tradition (grammatici) did Dionysos enter
- The Lukian scholion's date (cutting of vines / new wine) is chronologically wrong (spring events cannot fall in Posideon/winter); likewise Pausanias' date (harvest) is wrong. Both error independently — taken together they confirm the calendar puzzle but not the festival's date
- The name Haloa: ancient majority etymologised from ἅλως (threshing floor); Deubner follows Nilsson and F. Solmsen: ἁλωά = ἅλως in the sense of "cultivated field, tilled land" (cf. Cretan ἁλῳά, Cypriot ἁλουά = κῆποι)
- Purpose: "to protect the newly sprouted seed against harmful influences and promote its growth" (Nilsson)
**Deubner**: printed p. 60–67 / PDF p. 59–66 (approximately; content confirmed from bpo15yg2o.txt and bgyhnwzt2.txt)

### Chloia

**Month/Day**: Disputed. Pringsheim: Anthesterion (from the Marathonian sacrificial calendar, IG² 1358,49, which lists an offering to Demeter Chloe in Anthesterion). Deubner notes counter-evidence: Philochoros places an offering to Demeter Chloe on Thargelion 6 on the Acropolis (schol. Aristoph. Lys. 835; schol. Soph. O.C. 1600). An Eleusinian inscription (IG² 949) lists Chloia after the Haloa. No definitive decision is possible.
**Deity**: Demeter Chloe and Kore
**Ritual**:
- Joyful character (Cornutus 28: μετὰ παιδιᾶς καὶ…)
- A sacrifice by the Eleusinian demarch (IG² 949,7)
- Celebrated at Eleusis and in the spring when the young grain is green
**Participants**: General, including the demarch officiating
**Sources**: IG² 949,7; IG² 1358,49 (Marathonian calendar); Cornutus 28 S.55,13 Lang; Philochoros in schol. Aristoph. Lys. 835; schol. Soph. O.C. 1600
**Notes**: Deubner cannot fix the month precisely. A ram was also sacrificed to Demeter Achaia in Marathon in the Thargelion (IG² 1358,27).
**Deubner**: printed p. 67 / PDF p. 66 (briefly)

### Kalamaia

**Month/Day**: Spring or early summer — when grain stalks (κάλαμοι) had reached a specific stage of growth. Precise chronological fixing is not possible. The Ionian month Kalamaion (attested for Miletos, Kyzikos, Olbia) cannot with full certainty be placed on the Attic calendar.
**Deity**: Demeter (connected to the same group as the Haloa from the Piraeus inscription)
**Ritual**:
- Sacrifice (θυσία) in the old manner
- A pompé, organized together with the Hierophant and the priestesses (IG² 1177,6: same Piraeus inscription)
- Also celebrated at Piraeus
**Participants**: Hierophant, priestesses, demarch
**Sources**: IG² 1177,6 (Piraeus inscription, listing Thesmophoria, Plerosia, Kalamaia, Skira and other days); IG² 949 (Eleusinian accounting with Chloia and Kalamaia)
**Notes**: Deubner cannot date it precisely. The festival is otherwise poorly attested.
**Deubner**: printed p. 67–68 / PDF p. 66–67

### Plerosia

**Month/Day**: 5th of a month, probably Posideon (IG² 1183,32: "am 19. Posideon" — text damaged, date uncertain; Gjerstad ARW 27,1929,212). Sequence in the Piraeus inscription (IG² 1177) places it in the autumn–winter range, between Thesmophoria and Kalamaia/Skira.
**Deity**: In Demos Myrrinus: Zeus (IG² 1183); but listed among Demeter festivals in the Piraeus inscription
**Ritual**:
- A sacrifice (θυσία) overseen by the demarch
**Participants**: Demarch officiating
**Sources**: IG² 1183,32 (Myrrinos demarch's Plerosia inscription); IG² 1177,6 (Piraeus inscription listing Plerosia among Demeter festivals)
**Notes**: Deubner rejects the identification of Plerosia with the Proërosia (linguistically doubtful, calendrically impossible: Posideon vs. Pyanopsion)
**Deubner**: printed p. 68 / PDF p. 67

### Proërosia

**Month/Day**: 5 Pyanopsion (Suid. s.v. Προηρόσια: "the sacrifices before the plowing"; Hesych; IG² 1363 from Eleusis). Alternative name: Proarkturia (Hesych: "the people call them Proarkturia" — a farmer's term linked to astronomical reckoning, but Deubner denies this inaugurates a new sacred calendar use of star-reckoning).
**Deity**: Demeter and Kore (aition: Aithra comes to the altars of Demeter and Kore at Eleusis to offer ὑπὲρ χθονὸς ἀρότου = "on behalf of the plowing of the earth," Schol. Aristoph. Plut. 1054; cf. Euripides Hiketides beginning)
**Ritual**:
1. Hierophant and herald solemnly announce (προαγορεύουσιν) the festival — probably in Athens (IG² 1363)
2. Public ox-sacrifices (βοῦς παντοκτός? — IG² 1028: the epheboi lift and carry the oxen to the altar — "ἄρεσθαι" = to lift, not to select, cf. Ziehen, Hermes 66,1931,227ff.)
3. Epheboi participate in the public ox-sacrifices (IG² 1028,28: "and they carried [ἤγαγον] oxen to the Proërosia")
4. Uninstructed private cult: non-blood offerings (Maximus Tyrius 13 S.292,17 Hob.)
5. The sacred plowing on the Rarian Plain (ἱερὰ ἄροσις) is inseparable from the Proërosia and probably belongs to the festival itself as an introductory act
6. A folk song verse (Carm. pop. 50, Diehl: "Kore, come, cross the bridge, it is not yet time to plow") seems to invite Kore to participate in the plowing
**Participants**: Hierophant, herald, epheboi (for the ox-sacrifice), the general public (for private offering), farmers
**Sources**: Suid. (Προηρόσια); Hesych (Προηρόσια; Προαρκτουρία); IG² 1363 (Eleusinian inscription with Hierophant and herald announcement); IG² 1028 (epheboi); IG² 1358b,9 (pregnant cow sacrificed to earth goddess in Marathon, Posideon — possible parallel); Maximus Tyrius 13; Schol. Aristoph. Plut. 1054 (aition — Aithra); Euripides Hiket. init.; Carm. pop. 50; Epiktetos 3,21,12; Dittenberger, Syll.³ 1038 note 2; Nilsson, *Griech. Feste*; Pringsheim, *Beitr.* 213
**Notes**: Deubner notes the sacred plowing on the Rarian Plain is intimately connected to this festival. The festival concerns "the sacrifices before the plowing" in the sense of ensuring a good crop before autumn sowing.
**Deubner**: printed p. 68–69 / PDF p. 67–68

### Balkytis (Βαλλητύς)

**Month/Day**: Not specified by Deubner. Celebrated at Eleusis annually.
**Deity**: Demeter (in honor of Demophon, son of Keleos, per the Homeric Hymn to Demeter)
**Ritual**:
- A throwing ceremony — what was thrown is not transmitted; possibly stones
- Annual mock-battles (πόλεμον) among the boys of Eleusis in honor of Demophon, as Demeter proclaims at Hymn. Hom. in Cer. 265
- An agrarian meaning seems likely
**Sources**: Athen. 406d (᾽Ελευσῖνι γάρ... οἶδά τινα πανήγυριν ἀγομένην καὶ καλουμένην Βαλλητύν); Hesych (Βαλλητύς: ἑορτὴ ᾽Αθήνησιν ἐπὶ Δημοφῶντι τῷ Κελεοῦ ἀγομένη); Homer Hymn. Hom. in Cer. 265
**Notes**: Deubner considers the meaning agrarian, aligning it with the general fertility context of the Eleusinian cult. The content of the throwing rite is unattested (perhaps stones, by analogy).
**Deubner**: printed p. 69 / PDF p. 68

### Kleine Mysterien (Agrai)

**Month/Day**: Anthesterion
**Deity**: Demeter and Kore; the connection to Dionysos is uncertain (Steph. Byz.: their content "mimics the things about Dionysos")
**Ritual**:
1. The obligatory prerequisite for admission to the Great Mysteries (Plato Gorg. 497c with schol.: "it was necessary to be initiated in the small first, then the great")
2. Purification bath of the μύσται in the Ilissos river at Agrai (Polyaen 5,17,1)
3. Sacrifice by the Epimeletes (IG² 661,9)
4. Pantomimic performances (pantomimische Aufführungen) with Dionysiac content — but Deubner notes it cannot even be said with certainty whether the Dionysos of the small Mysteries is the same as the Iakchos of the great ones
**Participants**: Initiates (μύσται); Epimeletes officiating
**Sources**: Plato Gorg. 497c with schol. (p. 319 Hermann); Schol. Aristoph. Plut. 845; Plut. Demetr. 26,1; Clemens Strom. 4,3,1 S.249,8 Stähl.; 5,71,1 S.374,1ff.; Polyaen 5,17,1; IG² 847,22 (celebrated twice in Olympic years that also had the Eleusinia); IG² 661,9 (Epimeletes sacrifice); Steph. Byz. (Ἄγρα / Ἄγραι); Himerios or. 3,4 (Ilissos in spring); Bekker Anecd. 1,326,24
**Notes**: Originally an autonomous sanctuary at Agrai with independent significance; only after Eleusis was incorporated into the Attic state did it become a preliminary to the Great Mysteries. Deubner notes: whether this Dionysos = the Iakchos of the Great Mysteries remains open.
**Deubner**: printed p. 69–70 / PDF p. 68–69

### Eleusinische Mysterien (Great Mysteries)

**Month/Day**: Boëdromion (preparation begins 13 Boëdromion; the great procession is 19 Boëdromion). The mysteries themselves take place at Eleusis after the procession arrives.
**Deity**: Demeter and Kore; Iakchos (personification of the ἰαχχός-cry, not = Dionysos at the origin); Plutos as the fruit of the sacred marriage
**Ritual**:

**Preparatory days in Athens:**
1. 13 Boëdromion: Epheboi go to Eleusis and escort the ἱερά (sacred objects stored in cists) back to Athens; in Hellenistic times only met them at the "Echo" shrine partway; deposited in the urban Eleusinion below the Acropolis (IG² 1078,11ff.)
2. 14 Boëdromion: ἱερά arrive in the urban Eleusinion
3. 15 Boëdromion (first day of the Mysteries): ἀγυρμός — the assembly of the mysten (Hesych: "the first day of the Mysteries"); πρόρρησις of the Hierophant and Daduch in the Stoa Poikile — foreigners (βάρβαροι) and murderers (ἀνδροφόνοι) formally excluded (Isokr. 4,157; Schol. Aristoph. Frö. 369)
4. 16 Boëdromion: ἅλαδε μύσται ("to the sea, initiates!") — sea bath of mysten and their sacrificial pigs in the Kantharos harbour (Plut. Phok. 28,6); the pigs are subsequently sacrificed (purifying function debated by Deubner: he doubts the blood itself was a purification; the careful washing of the animals suggests otherwise)
5. 17 Boëdromion: Sacrifice for Demeter and Kore (attested in a private Attic calendar, IG² 1367,6); public sacrifice at the urban Eleusinion
6. 18 Boëdromion: Epidauria — festival of Asklepios inserted when his cult was admitted; pompé for Asklepios organized by the Basileus (Aristot. Ath. Pol. 56,4); mysten stay home (protection against pollution). Nachtfeier, Arrephorie, and special offerings at the Asklepieion (IG² 974,11). Volksversammlungen could still take place (IG² 657,3; 787,3)
7. 19 Boëdromion: Great procession on the Sacred Road to Eleusis. The ἱερά are retrieved from the Eleusinion; the Iakchos image (from his Athenian temple) joins the procession. Sacrifices, libations, hymns, and dances at the sacred stations along the road. The γεφυρισμοί (jokes at the Kephisos bridge) have no religious significance (Foucart, followed by Deubner). Mysten arrive at Eleusis after sunset (= 20th begins)
8. Night of 19/20 (arrival): Night festival at Eleusis — Iakchos received solemnly by the Epimeletes (IG² 847,21); torch-lit dances and songs at the Kallichoron well (τὸ καλλίχορον φρέαρ, mentioned in Homer Hymn 273); the well is where the oldest Eleusinian women danced and sang in honor of Demeter

**Purifications and ritual protections:**
- The Hierophant must become sexually inactive by consuming hemlock (Hippolytos ref. 5,8,40)
- Myrtle crowns worn by priests and mysten (Schol. Soph. O.C. 681; Aristoph. Frö. 330)
- βάκχοι: bundles of branches (twigs), protective talismans
- Headbands (στρόφιον): worn by Hierophant and Daduch; placed on the mysten by the Hierophantin at the start of the initiation (Epikt. 3,21,16; Plut. Aristid. 5,7; Eph. arch. 1885 no. 26,13)
- κρόκαι (threads bound around right hand and left foot of mysten — χρόκη δεσμός): apotropaic; performed by the clan of the Krokonidai, whose eponymous ancestor Krokon lived at the Eleusinian border. Originally bound at the frontier, later in Athens as numbers grew (Bekker Anecd. 1,273,25)
- Washing in the water-basin outside the great Propylaea; handwashing at the entrance of the urban Eleusinion (from the ἱερὰ χέρνιψ: [Lys.] 6,52)
- Purification in the Rheutoi streams at Eleusis: the northern one belongs to the elder goddess (Demeter), the southern to the younger (Kore) — used for the bathing of the mystai (Hesych s.v. Ῥεῖτοί)
- Possibly a "baptism" (water poured over the initiate) shown on a relief (pl. 6,3) — Deubner notes this is uncertain
- Food restrictions throughout the preparation period and a complete fast on the day before the τελετή itself
- The clothes worn at the Mysteries were dedicated to the goddesses afterwards (or used as swaddling clothes for children to transmit the accumulated sacred power)

**The Myesis (initiation before the τελετή proper):**
- Could happen monthly (before 460 BCE) in the sanctuary courtyard or in the urban Eleusinion (IG^1 6,125)
- Depicted on the Torre Nova sarcophagus (pl. 7,1), the Lovatelli urn (pl. 7,2), and related monuments — showing the initiation of Herakles as the generic type
- Main act (from the monuments): Initiate sits on a ram's fleece (perhaps = the Δῖον ᾠδίον, also associated with the Daduch's own usage — Suidas); veiled; the Hierophantin or priest stands to one side; a liknon (winnowing fan/basket) is used to scatter something (a blessing-charm) over the initiate; Kore stands with lowered torches beside the initiate (symbolically burning away impurity); the initiate's feet are bare (widespread religious custom)
- The ram's fleece: Deubner suggests a kathartic meaning (cf. Δῖον ᾠδίον for those burdened with a curse); unsure if it should be called a Δῖον ᾠδίον, since Suidas attests that term for the Daduch, not for the Eumolpidai / Kerykes in general
- Initiation possible also at the Epidauria (Philostr. v. Apoll. 4,18 — with reservations about the romance)

**The Telety (the mystery ceremony itself at Eleusis):**
- Preceded by a complete fast on a day of natural light; the fast ends at nightfall with the drinking of the kykeon
- Because of the enormous number of mysten, multiple nocturnal performances were necessary; each group that was admitted had to fast the preceding daylight
- The σύνθημα (password/formula) of the Eleusinian Mysteries as quoted by Clemens Protr. 21,2: "ἐνήστευσα, ἔπιον τὸν κυκεῶνα, ἔλαβον ἐκ κίστης, ἐργασάμενος ἀπεθέμην εἰς κάλαθον καὶ ἐκ καλάθου εἰς κίστην" ("I fasted, I drank the kykeon, I took from the ciste, I worked [with it], I put it in the kalathos and from the kalathos into the ciste")
  - Deubner's interpretation: This formula refers to a ceremony that preceded the seeing of the dromena and deiknymena and was distinct from the earlier myesis; it was performed within the festival at Eleusis. The formula refers to (a) the kykeon-drink (sacred barley-water: sacramental significance, since Demeter herself drank it first after her fast — Hymn 208ff.), then (b) a handling of something taken from and returned to the ciste. The precise nature of the object remains unknown
  - Deubner explicitly rejects Dieterich's interpretation (a phallos → sacred intercourse with the goddess) and Koerte's interpretation (a κτείς taken from the ciste → ritual rebirth by touching it → becoming a child of god → assurance of eternal bliss). Three reasons: (1) mere touching of a cunnus would not constitute a "rebirth" scene — a dramatic ceremony like Diodoros' adoption rite would be needed; (2) ἐργάζεσθαι is a strange word for mere touching; (3) Theodoret, who mentions the κτείς at Eleusis, also mentions it at the Thesmophoria — the two were often confused in later sources. The Clemens passage's obscene closing words refer not to the σύνθημα but to the Baubo episode that precedes it. The ciste's contents (from Clemens Protr. S.17,4ff.) were: various types of cakes (πόποι), salt, snakes, pomegranates, onions, narthex stalks, ivy branches — and only at the end of a loosely attached separate symbol-list does a κτείς appear, not specifically linked to the ciste content. Deubner concludes: the nature of the ciste-object is genuinely unknown and the central significance of the Mysteries remains an unsolved riddle

**The Dromena (sacred enactments):**
1. Pantomimic representation of the Koremythos: abduction, Demeter's wandering, grief, and the recovery of Kore. Evidence:
   - Isokrates 4,28 (deeds done for Demeter that only the initiated may hear)
   - Apollodoros of Athens (scholion to Theokritos 2,35/36b = FGrHist 2,1074,16): the Hierophant struck a gong (ἦχεῖον) when Demeter's call for Kore rang out
   - Aristides 19 S.422 Dind.: the temple as the scene of Demeter's search
   - Proklos (on Plato's Republic, 1,125,20 Kroll): the threnoi (laments) of the goddesses are transmitted in the τελεταί as secrets
   - Laktanz, Inst. Ep. 18,7: Proserpina sought with torches by night; the finding ends the ceremony with rejoicing and torch-swinging
   - Clemens Protr. 12,2: "Deo and Kore became the subject of a mystical drama" (Ἁρπαγή, πλάνη, πένθος / wandering, grief)
   - Gregorios of Nazianzos or. 39,4: the abduction, wandering, Keleos, Triptolemos, dragons, the fire; the things done and the things suffered

2. Ἱερὸς γάμος (Sacred Marriage) of Zeus and Demeter, performed by the Hierophant and the Hierophantin, in darkness. Evidence:
   - Schol. Plato Gorg. 497c: "the great mysteries were performed for Deo and Kore, because Plutos abducted the one and Zeus lay with Deo (ἐμίγη)"
   - Clemens Protr. 2,15 S.13,2: "the mysteries of Agra: the love intercourse of Zeus with his mother Demeter (ἀφροδίτιοι συμπλοκαί)"
   - Asterios hom. 10 (Band 40 S.324b Migne): "does not the priest... descend into the dark underground room with the priestess alone..."
   - Lukian Alex. 38f. (the pseudo-Mysteries of Alexander of Abonuteichos), which modeled their γάμοι on Eleusinian precedent
   - Deubner accepts the ἱερὸς γάμος as genuine but cautiously

3. Proclamation of the birth: After the sacred marriage, the Hierophant cried out (Hippolytos ref. 5,8 S.96,16 Wendl.): "ἱερὸν ἔτεκε πότνια κοῦρον, Βριμὼ Βρίμον" = "The Lady (Brimo/Demeter) has borne a sacred son, the Strong (Brimo) the Strong (Brimos)" — i.e., Plutos, the mythic embodiment of harvest abundance. Deubner follows Foucart and Kern in identifying the child as Plutos (Hymn. Hom. 489f.; Hesiod Theog. 969; Odyssey 5,125ff.), not the individual myste (contra Koerte)

4. The "Ue Küe" formula (Rain — conceive): spoken by the Epoptai looking up at the sky ("rain") and down at the earth ("conceive"), as a pure fertility spell (Proklos, Tim. 293a, Bd.3 S.176,28 Di.; Hippolytos ref. 5,7,34 S.87,6). Deubner: this is a pre-Greek, non-mythological layer of the ritual — a simple magic formula for agricultural fertility, contrasted with the mythological overlay of the Hieros Gamos. Together they form two strata: (a) an archaic agrarian spell (formula + the reaped ear); (b) the Greek mythological elaboration (Hieros Gamos + birth of Plutos)

5. The reaped sacred ear of grain shown to the Epoptai: "ἐν σιωπῇ τεθερισμένον στάχυν" (Hippolytos ref. 5,8,39 S.96,10): "the great, wonderful, most perfect epoptic mystery — a silently reaped ear of grain." Deubner: this silently reaped ear is the same as Plutos — the harvest symbol, the concrete natural substrate of the mythic Plutos. The ear was probably left standing on the Rarian Plain and now ritually mown and displayed. It stands for the inexhaustible fertility of the earth

**The Epopteia (second grade, higher initiation):**
- Only those who had participated in the Great Mysteries at least a year before were admitted (Plut. Demetr. 26,2)
- The dromena, legomena, and deiknymena were presumably distributed between the two grades — but how remains uncertain
- Deubner's reconstruction: The first grade probably involved the mythological Dromena (Koremythos, Hieros Gamos); the second grade (Epopteia) included the sacred ear and the "ue küe" formula — the older, agrarian stratum
- The precise source of the assurance of eternal bliss remains "an unsolved riddle": the ancient evidence says "blessed is he who has seen (ὄπωπεν)" the rites, suggesting the *seeing* (Epopteia) as the climax, but the exact mechanism is unknown

**Closing rite of the Mysteries:**
- On the last day: the πλημοχόαι ceremony — two funnel-shaped earthenware vessels (πλημοχόη) filled with an unknown liquid, then overturned (one toward the East, one toward the West) while standing and reciting a mystical formula (Athen. 496a). Deubner interprets this most plausibly as a rain-charm (Regenzauber), fitting the agrarian character of the festival. The verse from the Peirithoos that Athenaeus also cites ("where we shall reverently pour these πλημοχόαι into the chthonian [soil]...") refers to a different rite

**Participants**: All free people (citizens, non-Greeks initially excluded but later admitted); barbarians and murderers formally excluded; the priesthoods of Eumolpidai (Hierophant) and Kerykes (Daduch, Keryx, altar-priest); Epimeletes; Basileus; Epheboi (for the escort and ox-sacrifices); the Krokonidai (binding of threads); a child "from the hearth" (ἀφ' ἑστίας) who performed preliminary rites on behalf of all mysten; the Hierophantin

**Sources (major)**:

**Notes**:
- Deubner's key positions: (1) The Great Mysteries are pre-Greek in origin, belonging to a pre-Hellenic Aegean stratum that preserved itself only at Eleusis. (2) He rejects a Cretan origin (contra Persson, Picard). (3) He is skeptical of Koerte's rebirth theory and keeps the central meaning of the Mysteries genuinely open. (4) He distinguishes two ritual strata: an archaic pre-Greek fertility spell layer (formula, reaped ear) and a Greek mythological layer (Hieros Gamos, Plutos birth, Koremythos drama). (5) The assurance of eternal bliss remains an "unsolved riddle" — probably connected to the Epopteia/seeing, but the mechanism is unknown.

### Eleusinia (agonistic festival)

**Month/Day**: Probably Metageitnion (Deubner: this is the most likely; the beginning of Boëdromion also comes in question, per van der Loeff and Pringsheim). The precise date within the month is not specified.
**Deity**: Demeter and Kore (the agon is in their honor: "the agon of Kore and Demeter, called Eleusinia" — Schol. Pind. Ol. 9,150b)
**Ritual**:
1. A pompé in which a kanephore (basket-carrier) participated (IG² 930,8; IG 3,916; P. Foucart, Rev. ét. gr. 32,1919,201)
2. Sacrifices divided into προτέλεια (preliminary) and main sacrifice (τὸ ἐν τῇ ἑορτῇ = main offering); established by the important inscription IG^1 25 (c. 500 BCE) from Eleusis:
   - Proteleia offered by the hierapoioi of the Eleusinians: to Hermes Enagonios + the Charites (a goat), to Poseidon (a ram), to Artemis (a goat), to Triptolemos (a ram)
   - Main sacrifice: to Plutos, Chthonios (the Two Goddesses), a triple bovine offering (τρίτταν βόαρκον) ἐν τῇ ἑορτῇ
3. Competitions (agon): Athletic/musical contests; the festival had a Panegyris character
**Frequency**: Both pentaeteric (every four years = Great Eleusinia, held in the second year of each Olympiad, IG² 1304,25) and trieteric (every two years, in the 1st and 3rd years of the Olympiad). The small Mysteries at Agrai were celebrated twice in years when the Eleusinia also fell (IG² 847,22)
**Prize**: A quantity of sacred barley from the Rarian Plain (Schol. Pind. Ol. 9,150b: "the prize consists of barley" — κριθαί; IG² 1672,252ff. with specific quantities 258ff.). Deubner: the same logic as the sacred olive oil at the Panathenaia (p. 34) — the sacred origin of the prize reflects the divine blessing it carries
**Participants**: Athletes/competitors from the Greek world (given the Panegyris character); the kanephore in the pompé; hierapoioi performing the sacrifices; officials of Eleusis
**Sources**:
- Schol. Pind. Ol. 9,150b (agon of Kore and Demeter; barley prize)
- IG² 1672,252ff. (great accounts inscription: barley prizes for Eleusinia)
- IG² 1304,25 (pentaeteric character)
- IG² 847,22 (connection with double small Mysteries in Eleusinia years)
- IG² 930,8 and IG 3,916 (kanephore)
- IG^1 25 (proteleia inscription, c. 500 BCE, from Eleusis)
- P. Foucart, Rev. ét. gr. 32,1919,201
- van der Loeff, *De ludis Eleus.* 74ff., 89ff.
- Boesch, Berl. philol. Wochenschr. 1917,155ff.
- Pringsheim, *Beitr.* 102
**Notes**: Deubner explicitly separates the Eleusinia from the Eleusinian Mysteries: "The Eleusinia are to be distinguished from the Mysteries also celebrated at Eleusis; they were a great agonistic festival." They are an entirely distinct institution — an athletic/competitive Panegyris — not a mystery ceremony.
