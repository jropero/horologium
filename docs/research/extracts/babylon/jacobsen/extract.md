# Jacobsen — The Treasures of Darkness (1976): Festival Extract

Source: Thorkild Jacobsen, *The Treasures of Darkness: A History of Mesopotamian Religion*, Yale University Press, 1976.

**Scope note**: This book is a literary and religious history of Mesopotamian religion organized around metaphors (provider, ruler, personal god). It is **not** a festival calendar handbook. Jacobsen discusses the Dumuzi cult, sacred marriage, and related ritual cycles in terms of their myth and meaning, **without giving specific month/day numbers** for the ritual events. The day-specific data in the app (e.g., day 26–28 of Dumuzu) comes from Neo-Assyrian hemerologies and cultic calendars (Cohen 1993, Livingstone, etc.) — sources outside Jacobsen's scope. This extract captures everything calendar-adjacent that Jacobsen does state.

---

## Dumuzi / Tammuz Lament Cycle

### What Jacobsen says

**Seasonal context (p. 47):**
> "Spring does not last long, and its fecund power wanes and is lost as the dry hot summer sets in. Mythopoeically experienced this means that Dumuzi dies, a victim of the powers of death and the netherworld."

The death is thus firmly located at the transition from spring to summer — consistent with month 4 (Duʾūzu) being the month of lament. No specific month number is stated.

**Form of death — multiple versions (p. 47–48):**
- Set upon by highwaymen in his fold, by a posse of evil deputies from Hades, killed, taken captive, or perishing in flight.
- In many versions the death is assumed to have occurred at an earlier time; the mourners (widow Inanna, sister Geshtinanna, mother Duttur) come to his raided camp to bewail him.

**"Dumuzi's Dream" (pp. 48–49):**
Dumuzi has premonitory dreams: rushes rise against him (highwaymen), lone reed and two reeds removed (Duttur and Geshtinanna taken), his churn removed, cup gone from its peg, sheep and goats lying in dust. He sends Geshtinanna as lookout. When the attackers (deputies from four cities) arrive, his sister keeps her promise but his comrade betrays him. Multiple escapes as a gazelle (Utu transforms him), capture at the sheepfold: "he himself has fallen **at the festival of the young men** as the text says, using an old kenning for battle." (p. 49)

**"The Most Bitter Cry" (pp. 49–52):**
Inanna weeps; seven evil deputies enter Dumuzi's fold by night. The seventh deputy rouses the chief shepherd who lies asleep. Dumuzi is stripped of his crown, robe, and staff, taken away. He escapes again, reaches the river in flood, is swept past Duttur and Inanna, carried to the underworld. No day numbers given.

**Laments — mourners and content (pp. 52–62):**
- Geshtinanna: dirge "Before a Young Wife" (p. 52).
- Inanna: "The Wild Bull Who Has Lain Down" — she finds him dead in the desert, fold raided (pp. 53–55).
- Duttur (mother): "My Heart Plays a Reed Pipe" — she and Geshtinanna and Ninsuna search the desert (pp. 54–55).
- The Descent of Inanna: Inanna goes to the netherworld for unstated reasons; is killed; revived by Enki's kurgaru and kalaturru with water of life and grass of life; must provide a substitute — delivers Dumuzi (pp. 55–62).

**Resolution — the half-year alternation (p. 61):**
> "You half a year, your sister half a year: while you are walking around (alive), she will lie prostrate; while your sister is walking around (alive), you will lie prostrate."

Dumuzi in the netherworld half the year; Geshtinanna substitutes the other half. Jacobsen interprets this in terms of grain (Dumuzi) going underground in spring/early summer and grape/wine (Geshtinanna) going underground in autumn (pp. 62–63).

**Deities of the lament cycle:** Dumuzi/Tammuz, Inanna, Geshtinanna (sister), Duttur/Ninsuna (mother), Utu (sun god, brother-in-law), Ereshkigal (queen of netherworld), Enlil, Nanna, Enki.

**General check on month placement:**

## Sacred Marriage (Hieros Gamos)

### What Jacobsen says

**Core description (pp. 32–47; summary pp. 47):**
The orchardman's sacred marriage rite (Dumuzi-Amaushumgalanna/Inanna) is a date-harvest ritual — Inanna (storehouse of Eanna) is wed to Amaushumgalanna (the power of the date palm's annual budding), capturing the yield of the date harvest into the storehouse community. The herder's rite is a spring fertility celebration: the sacred mating of all nature at the vernal season.

**When the rite took place:**
- The Idin-Dagan Text states (p. 38): "has it (i.e., the nation) **at New Year, the day of the ritual**, set up a bed for milady." — Spring New Year (= Nisannu region).
- For the Nanna cult at Ur (p. 126): "One may guess that the sacred marriage was celebrated at the **Akiti festival of Nanna in the twelfth month** [Addaru], when offerings connected with 'setting up the bed' are recorded."

**Who performs the rite:**
"In this rite the king assumed the identity of the god while a high priestess seems to have embodied the goddess." (p. 125)
King = Dumuzi (or named king, e.g., Iddin-Dagan of Isin). Goddess = Inanna, embodied by queen or high priestess.

**Deities:** Dumuzi/Amaushumgalanna and Inanna; in Nanna context, Nanna and Ningal.

**No mention of:**
- Month 11 (Šabāṭu) in connection with any hieros gamos.
- Bēl (Marduk) and Beltiya as a sacred marriage pair.
- Nabû and Tašmētu as a sacred marriage pair.
These are Neo-Babylonian traditions (from Babylonian cultic calendars, 1st-millennium BCE) outside Jacobsen's focus, which is pre-Babylonian (3rd–2nd millennium Sumerian tradition).

### Discrepancies vs. app Šabāṭu data

**Day 7 — "Matrimonio sagrado: Bēl weds Beltiya + Nabû weds Tašmētu"**
- NOT MENTIONED in Jacobsen. Jacobsen's hieros gamos is Dumuzi/Inanna (spring, New Year or Addaru 12th month), not Bēl/Beltiya or Nabû/Tašmētu in Šabāṭu.
- Status: CANNOT CONFIRM from Jacobsen — but this is NOT a discrepancy. Jacobsen covers the 3rd–2nd millennium Sumerian tradition. The Šabāṭu marriages of Bēl and Nabû are 1st-millennium Neo-Babylonian cultic calendar practice (sourced from Babylonian ritual texts, Cohen 1993, etc.). Jacobsen is the wrong source to verify this specific entry. No contradiction exists — these are different traditions.

**Broader observation:** Jacobsen's placement of hieros gamos at Nisannu (New Year/spring) rather than Šabāṭu is irrelevant to the app's Neo-Babylonian data, which refers to entirely different deity pairs in a different city context.

## Akītu Festival

### What Jacobsen says

**Index reference (p. 261):** "Akiti: of Nanna, 126. See also New Year Festival."

**Nanna's Akiti (p. 124, 126):**
- "Lustrations to keep the bright moon free of defilement appear indeed to have been a fairly general feature of the cult and to have taken place not only at eclipses but annually at New Year (zag-mu.ak), which in Ur probably fell at the beginning of the month Mashdagu, and at the 'Great Festival' (Ezen-mah) in the ninth month which took its name from it." (p. 124)
- Nanna's Akiti festival in the **twelfth month**: offerings for "setting up the bed" are recorded there (p. 126) — Jacobsen connects this to the sacred marriage of Nanna.

**New Year Festival at Babylon (p. 232):**
Referenced briefly in the Epilogue in connection with Marduk's role in the first millennium. Jacobsen writes that the divine assembly "read at New Year" the fates (p. 231). No day-by-day detail of the Babylon Akitu is provided in this book.

**Pallis reference (p. 14, fn):**
"Pallis, who in his study of the Akitu festival in Babylon compares it with similar dramas elsewhere." — Jacobsen cites Pallis (1926) as the specialist source; he does not himself provide the day-by-day Babylonian Akitu reconstruction.

**Day-by-day Akitu data:**
Jacobsen does NOT provide the day 1/4/8/11 breakdown of the Babylonian Akitu. The app's Nisannu dayEvents (days 1, 4, 8, 11) come from the Babylonian ritual texts, not from Jacobsen.

### Discrepancies vs. app Nisannu data

- CANNOT CONFIRM from Jacobsen: the specific day sequence (day 1 procession, day 4 Enuma Eliš, day 8 reinvestiture, day 11 fate-fixing). These are correct per Babylonian ritual sources but not verified by this book.
- No contradiction with Jacobsen's general statements about the Akitu and New Year.

## Other Calendar / Festival Data

### Monthly festivals (p. 122)
> "Corresponding to the phases of the moon, festivals called **eš e š** (i.e., 'all-temple' or 'general' festivals) were celebrated on the **first, seventh, and fifteenth** of the month during the Third Dynasty of Ur."

Special offerings on the day the moon was invisible: "ud-nu-a, 'the day of lying down' — the day Nanna went to the netherworld to judge and make administrative decisions." (p. 122)

This confirms the general importance of days 1, 7, 15 (and the dark-of-moon) across all months. The app's Kislīmu Kinunu festival on days 1, 2, 6, 7, 14, 15 and the general sapatu day 15 are consistent with this lunar rhythm.

### "Festival of the young men" (p. 49)
Dumuzi falls "at the festival of the young men" — an old kenning for battle in the Sumerian cult texts. Jacobsen does not identify this with a specific calendar date or month. It could be a general designation, or might relate to the Abu games (month 5), but Jacobsen does not make this connection explicit.

### Ancestor cult / funerary offerings (p. 122)
The special rites on the dark-of-moon day suggest ancestor contact themes. This provides background context for the Abu month 5 ab/pum ancestor rite, though Jacobsen does not discuss Abu specifically.

### Šabāṭu / hardships of winter (p. 238)
Jacobsen quotes Esarhaddon's annals: "the month of Shabat, the hardships of winter." This is not festival data but confirms the month's seasonal character (midwinter).

### Calendar institution (index, p. 263): "Calendar: instituted, 179"
Page 179 refers to the Nippur calendar ("The Nippur calendar was then instituted" — institutional calendar as part of Early Dynastic period administration). This is historical context about calendar origins, not festival data.

**No data in the app is directly contradicted by Jacobsen.** The app's day-specific entries derive from Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian cultic calendars (Cohen 1993, Assyrian hemerologies, Livingstone, etc.) which are appropriate primary sources for those entries and outside Jacobsen's scope.

**What Jacobsen positively supports:**
1. Dumuzi's death at onset of hot summer → month 4 (Duʾūzu) correctly named and placed (pp. 47, 26).
2. The lament involving Inanna, Geshtinanna (sister), Duttur/Ninsuna (mother) — consistent with the app's characterization of the lament month (pp. 48–55).
3. The "half-year" alternation of Dumuzi and Geshtinanna in the netherworld (p. 61) — mythological background to the cyclical nature of the lament.
4. Sacred marriage at New Year / spring as the basic tradition (pp. 38, 125), with Nanna's variant in month 12 (p. 126).
5. Days 1, 7, 15 as generally important festival days throughout all months (p. 122).
6. Šabāṭu characterized by "hardships of winter" (p. 238) — consistent with its midwinter position.

**Jacobsen is not the primary source for:** specific day numbers of Dumuzi lament events, the ab/pum ancestor rite, the Gilgameš games, the Maqlu exorcism, the Neo-Babylonian hieros gamos of Bēl and Nabû, or the Bīt Dagan lamentations. Those entries should be verified against Cohen (1993), Livingstone (CUSAS 25), and Assyrian hemerological texts.
