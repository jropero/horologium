# Mensis Februarius — Fowler (1899)
**PDF pages used**: 310–342 (printed 299–331)

## Introduction: the month of purification
Fowler opens with a careful warning: "februum" (instrument of purification, Sabine = purgamentum) does not mean general lustration of the people nor a Christian-style penitential period. It covers involuntary ritual errors and aversion of hostile unseen influences, not moral sin. The Parentalia and Lupercalia are the characteristic rites. The month marks a transition from joyful winter gatherings to duties toward dead ancestors and a mysterious piacular rite. Spring begins agriculturally on Feb. 7 (Favonius). Pages 299–302.

## Junoni Sospitae (Kalends of February)
- **Day**: 1 (Kalends)
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Juno Sospita (the great Lanuvian goddess)
- **Ritual**: Dedication day of her temple in the Forum olitorium; vowed 197 BC by consul Cornelius Cethegus; had decayed by Ovid's time.
- **Page**: p. 302

## Fauno in Insula (Ides of February)
- **Day**: 13 (Ides, but dated to this entry because placed by Fowler before the Fornacalia discussion)
- **Fasti status**: IP (C.I.L. vi. 2302)
- **Deity**: Faunus
- **Ritual**: Temple of Faunus on the Tiber island; vowed in 296 BC by plebeian aediles with money from fines on holders of ager publicus who had not paid rents. Cross-reference to Dec. 5 (Faunalia rustica).
- **Page**: p. 302

## Fornacalia
- **Day**: Variable (conceptivae); ending on the Quirinalia (Feb. 17); the day for each individual curia was announced by its curio at varying times
- **Fasti status**: Not in calendars (sacrum publicum pro curiis, not pro populo)
- **Deity**: Fornax (spirit of the oven); Juno Curis honoured in the curiae (tables = mensae in quibus immolabatur Iunoni)
- **Ritual**: In each private house within each curia: an offering of far (roasted emmer wheat meal), made into cakes in the household oven (pistrina). Then each curia met as a body for communal sacra; each curia had a designated space in the Forum marked by a placard on the Quirinalia day. Anyone who had forgotten or did not know which day their own curia met could make up the rite on the Quirinalia (Feb. 17) at the general assembly of all thirty curiae — hence "stultorum feriae" (feast of the fools). Pliny connects it with boundary-beating of each curia's land. Fowler thinks the household cakes were a contribution to a communal religious meal; the "stultorum feriae" tag is of late origin as the curiate organisation declined with the admission of plebeians.
- **Page**: p. 302–306

## Parentalia (and Feralia)
- **Day**: Feb. 13–21 (begins sixth hour of Feb. 13; public festival = Feralia, Feb. 21)
- **Fasti status**: Dies parentales not individually marked N in calendars (per Mommsen, showing they are later than the Lemuria, which are N). Feralia appears in three calendars (Caer. Maff. Farn.) in large letters, and is partly F/FP.
- **Deity**: Di Manes (dead ancestors); no specific deity
- **Ritual**: From Feb. 13 to 21 all temples were closed, marriages forbidden, magistrates appeared without insignia. Families visited the family tomb; offerings of water, wine, milk, honey, oil, and blood of black victims; flowers placed on the tomb; solemn greeting "Salve, sancte parens"; a meal shared with the dead; petition for good fortune. The Vestal Virgin performed a parentatio at the tomb of Tarpeia on Feb. 13 (Philocalus). Feralia (Feb. 21) was the only public festival in the sequence and the oldest known day for ancestor worship; it was in part a dies fastus. On or around Feralia, Ovid describes a folk-witchcraft rite: an old woman works spells for Tacita (binding enemies' mouths), using incense at a mouse-hole, woollen threads, black beans, a pitch-sealed fish; Fowler regards this as a savage spell for counteracting hostile spirits, with only a doubtful connection to the Feralia itself.
- **Aition**: The Parentalia is described by Fowler as "a yearly renewal of the rite of burial" — a systematic civic cult of the dead organized by the state and pontifices.
- **Page**: p. 306–310

## Caristia / Cara Cognatio
- **Day**: 22
- **Fasti status**: Mentioned in Philocalus, Silvius, and rustic calendars only; Valerius Maximus is the next writer after Ovid to mention it
- **Deity**: Lares of the family (who shared in the sacred meal, "incincti")
- **Ritual**: Family reunion immediately after duties to the dead. Living members of the family assembled, quarrels were to be laid aside, guilty or cruel members excluded. A love-feast centred on the family Lares; Ovid (Fasti 2.617 ff.) describes it as a reunion of surviving blood-relations after visiting the tombs: "Postque tot amissos quicquid de sanguine restat, / Aspicere, et generis dinumerare gradus." Fowler sees it as a glimpse of the gentler side of Roman family life.
- **Page**: p. 309–310

## Lupercalia
- **Day**: 15 (XV KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (Caer. Maff. Farn. IP; Philoc. Silv. and all rustic calendars)
- **Deity**: Uncertain. Ovid attributes it to Faunus ("Cornipedi Fauno caesa de more capella," Fasti 2.361); Livy mentions Inuus (a mysterious deity otherwise hardly known); Liber is suggested by Servius; Juno has been suggested because the thongs were called "Iunonis amiculum." Fowler concludes the Romans of the literary age did not know who the god was; no deity called Lupercus existed.
- **Ritual**: Celebrants gathered at the Lupercal cave at the foot of the south-western Palatine. First, goats and a dog were sacrificed (exceptional piacula: both goat and dog were unusual victims); sacred cakes made by the Vestals from the first ears of last year's harvest were also offered (last use of the year's mola salsa). Two youths of high rank, one from each of the two collegia of Luperci (Fabiani and Quinctiales/Quintiliales), had their foreheads smeared with the bloody knife, then wiped with wool dipped in milk, then laughed. They girt themselves with the goat-skins and feasted. Then, naked but for the goat-skin girdles and with strips of skin (februa = "Iunonis amiculum") cut from the hides, they ran round the base of the Palatine in two companies, following the ancient pomoerium, striking at women who came near them or offered themselves. The striking with the februa was intended to produce fertility. The course was a lustratio: Varro, "Lupercis nudis lustratur antiquum oppidum Palatinum gregibus humanis cinctum." Caesar rebuilt the decayed Lupercal; the feast survived until 494 AD, when Pope Gelasius I changed Feb. 15 to the Purification of the Virgin Mary.
- **Aition**: Fowler discusses several theories: (a) purification from blood-guilt of the sacrificers; (b) Mannhardt's vegetation-spirit death-and-revival; (c) the lustratio of the Palatine community. Fowler prefers the piacular explanation combined with a pastoral origin. He dismisses the notion that the runners represent wolves personating a vegetation-spirit, and rejects any deity named Lupercus.
- **Page**: p. 310–321

## Quirinalia
- **Day**: 17 (XXII KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Caer. Maff. Farn. Philoc.); Quirino in colle (Farn. Caer.)
- **Deity**: Quirinus (on the Quirinal hill; Fowler regards him as another form of Mars, the equivalent cult-expression of the same religious conception in two allied communities)
- **Ritual**: Festival of Quirinus; also the final day of the Fornacalia ("stultorum feriae"). Curio Maximus announced the Fornacalia rites on this day; the general assembly of all thirty curiae took place in the Forum, each curia assigned a space with a placard. Fowler cannot explain why the festival of Quirinus coincided with the last day of the curiae's sacra; he thinks the conjunction was accidental. Of the actual rites of Quirinus on this day he knows nothing.
- **Page**: p. 322–324

## Terminalia
- **Day**: 23 (VII KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Caer. Maff. Rust. Philoc. Silv.)
- **Deity**: Terminus (god of boundaries; eventually connected with Jupiter, who shared his Capitoline temple with a terminus-stone under an open hole in the roof)
- **Ritual**: In the country: the two landowners each garlanded their own side of the boundary-stone with double offerings; an altar was built; the farmer's wife brought fire from the hearth; a daughter thrice scattered fruits into the fire with honey-cakes; neighbours stood by in silence in white garments; a lamb and sucking-pig were slain and the stone sprinkled with blood; a feast and songs in praise of Terminus followed. The rite was a yearly renewal of the original stone-fixing ceremony (described by gromatic writer Siculus Flaccus: fruits, bones, ashes, and blood of a victim buried under the stone). Roman scholars thought the Terminalia was the last festival of the year (Varro, Ovid), but Fowler notes this was a misunderstanding arising from the coincidence of the date with the point before intercalation; Terminus is a god of land boundaries, not time. At the sixth milestone on the via Laurentina there was a public terminus-stone where sacrifices were made.
- **Page**: p. 324–327

## Regifugium
- **Day**: 24 (VI KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: N (Caer. Maff. Philoc.); Silvius adds "Tarquinius Superbus fertur ab urbe expulsus"
- **Deity**: Unknown (Rex sacrorum as celebrant); connection with Jupiter suggested by some later sources but rejected
- **Ritual**: Fowler argues firmly that the historical explanation (flight of Tarquin, as in Silvius and Ovid) is a misapprehension. The real meaning, supported by Mommsen's completion of a mutilated Festus gloss and by Plutarch (Q.R. 63), was: the Rex sacrorum performed a sacrifice in the Comitium and then fled from the spot. Parallels include the Athenian Bouphonia ("ox-murder") and the sacrifice of a bull-calf to Dionysus at Tenedos where the priest had to flee. Fowler concludes that the flight followed the sacrifice of a sacrosanct victim, possibly a scapegoat; the word "Regifugium" attaches only to Feb. 24.
- **Page**: p. 327–330

## Equirria
- **Day**: 27 (III KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Maff. Caer.); Varro L.L. 6.13
- **Deity**: Mars (horse-race in the Campus Martius, said to have been established by Romulus in honour of Mars)
- **Ritual**: Horse-race in the Campus Martius. A second race falls on March 14. No explanation for the doubling is known; Huschke's conjectures are dismissed by Fowler as worthless. Mannhardt suggested the races originated in a desire to catch the spirit of vegetation in an animal form; spring races occur widely in Europe. Fowler notes that the Lupercalia, Quirinalia, and Equirria may all represent foreshadowings of the March Mars-cult, beginning even before March 1, at the time the first swallows arrive. Ovid celebrates this as the end of the Roman year.
- **Page**: p. 330–331

## Summary
Fowler presents February as a transitional month: the joyful mid-winter series ends and gives way to duties toward the dead (Parentalia, Feralia) and a mysterious piacular rite (Lupercalia). The Fornacalia and its conclusion at the Quirinalia are carefully linked to the old curiate organisation. The Lupercalia receives by far the most extensive treatment of any February festival, with a full survey of competing theories (Schwegler's human sacrifice hypothesis, Mannhardt's vegetation-spirit, Robertson Smith's sacrosanct-victim framework). Fowler is consistently cautious and identifies multiple genuine open questions. The month ends with the Equirria as a prologue to the Mars-festivals of March, and the Regifugium preserves (Fowler argues) an ancient sacerdotal flight whose original meaning was already lost by the Republic.
