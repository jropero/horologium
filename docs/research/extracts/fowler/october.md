**October span**: printed pp. 239–251 (Tigillum Sororium discussion begins p. 238 in the September/October transition)

## Tigillum Sororium

- **Day**: 1 (Kal. Oct.) — not formally headed as KAL. OCT. by Fowler; discussed in the transition from September
- **Fasti status**: not stated in this passage
- **Deity**: Janus Curiatius; Juno Sororia
- **Ritual**: A beam (tigillum) let into the opposite walls of a street leading from the Carinae to the Vicus Cyprius. Renewed from time to time; attested until at least the 4th c. AD. Two altars stood beneath it — one to Janus Curiatius, one to Juno Sororia — apparently in the hereditary care of the gens Horatia ("quibusdam piacularibus sacrificiis factis, quae deinde genti Horatiae tradita sunt," Livy 1.26). Fowler treats the standard antiquarian explanation (a primitive Janus-arch) with scepticism, noting the tigillum appears always in the singular.
- **Aition**: Roman antiquaries connected it with the expiation of the legendary Horatius after the murder of his sister — acquitted by popular appeal, he erected the altars and passed under a yoke, thereafter commemorated by the tigillum. Fowler judges this "really inexplicable" as a historical aition; he prefers Roscher's suggestion that the germ lies in the practice of creeping through a split tree to remove a spell or disease. He also speculates the legend of Horatius may itself have arisen from a pre-existing gentile cult of two deities whose cult-titles were "Curiatius" and "Sororia."
- **Page**: pp. 238–239
- **Notes**: Fowler does not assign this a formal calendar-header entry for Oct. 1; the discussion appears at the end of the September section and flows into the MENSIS OCTOBER heading. Sources cited: Livy 1.26; Dion. Hal. 3.22; Festus 297 and Paul. 307; Aur. Vict. 4.9; Schol. Bob. ad Cic. p. 277 Orelli; Lydus *de Mensibus* 4.1.

## Iovi Fulguri, Iunoni Curitis in Campo

- **Day**: 7 (Non. Oct.)
- **Fasti status**: F (stated in text as "F.")
- **Deity**: Iuppiter Fulgur; Iuno Curitis
- **Ritual**: Fowler states flatly: "Of these worships in Rome nothing else is known." The entry is attested in the Arval and Pauline calendars (text cites "AEV. PAUL." — Arvales, Paulus Diaconus).
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 239
- **Notes**: Iuno Curitis is identified as the goddess of Falerii; Fowler had discussed a supposed *hieros gamos* of hers earlier in the book (p. 223). The calendar variant reads "Curriti Arv[ales]" vs. "Q[uiriti] Paul[us]." No temple or altar location specified beyond "in Campo."

## Meditrinalia

- **Day**: 11 (V Id. Oct.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (stated; calendars: Sab., Maff., Amit.)
- **Deity**: Meditrina (nominal; Fowler doubts her existence as a real deity); Jupiter (Fowler follows Mommsen: Jupiter is the real deity, as with the Vinalia)
- **Ritual**: The day on which new wine (mustum) was first tasted. Formula recited: *"Vetus novum vinum bibo, veteri novo morbo medeor"* (old and new wine drunk, old and new disease cured). Both Varro (*L.L.* 6.21) and Festus/Paulus (Paul. 123) give this account. The Flamen Martialis reported (via Varro) that new and old wine were poured and tasted *medicamenti causa* on this day.
- **Aition**: The name Meditrinalia was said to derive from *medendo* (healing), and the goddess Meditrina was invented from the formula. Fowler accepts this folk-etymology origin for the name but denies real divine status to Meditrina.
- **Page**: pp. 239–240
- **Notes**: Parallel cited with Fratres Arvales in May tasting *fruges aridas et virides* (old and new grain). Plutarch (*Quaest. Conv.* 7.1) discussed the belief that new wine (*mustum*) was wholesome and non-inebriating. Mommsen (*CIL* i² 332) notes Jupiter as the real deity (see Vinalia, p. 86 in Fowler). No sanctuary or location given. Feriae Iovi is the secondary calendar entry (Amit.).

## Fontinalia

- **Day**: 13 (III Id. Oct.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (stated; calendars: Sab., Maff., Amit., Min., IX [i.e., Maffeian, Amiterni, and others])
- **Deity**: Fons (also called Fontus)
- **Ritual**: Garlands were thrown into springs and wells were garlanded. Varro (*L.L.* 6.22): *"Fontinalia a Fonte, quod is dies feriae eius; ab eo tunc et in fontes coronas iaciunt et puteos coronant."* Fowler comments the original object of garlanding was probably to secure abundant water; the holiness of wells and springs is taken as self-evident.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Page**: pp. 241 (under the heading "III ID. OCT.")
- **Notes**: Evidence for a god Fons or Fontus: a *delubrum Fontis* (Festus 85), an *ara Fonti* on the Janiculum (Cicero, *N.D.* 3.20; Preller 1.176), and a *porta Fontinalis* in the Campus Martius. Fons appears alongside Flora, Mater Larum, Summanus, etc. in Fratres Arvales ritual (Henzen, *Acta Fr. Arv.* 146). Fowler notes this is "a case in which multiplicity passes into a quasi-unity" — Fons did not survive long as a unified deity. Very ancient festival.

## Equus October (October Horse)

- **Day**: 15 (Id. Oct.)
- **Fasti status**: M (Philocalus' late calendar only; not in the Republican calendars — Fowler devotes a paragraph to explaining this absence: the Ides were sacred to Jupiter, and secondary rites on Ides were liable to be omitted from popular-use fasti)
- **Deity**: Mars
- **Ritual**: A two-horse chariot race was held in the Campus Martius. The near (left) horse of the winning pair was sacrificed to Mars — killed with a spear (Timaeus ap. Polyb. 12.4b). The tail was cut off immediately and carried at speed to the Regia so that warm blood dripped onto the sacred hearth (*focus*) there. The head was decked with cakes (*panes*). A fierce contest then took place between men of the Via Sacra quarter and the Subura for possession of the head: if the Via Sacra men won, the head was fixed to the wall of the Regia; if the Subura men won, it went to the *turris Mamilia*. The site of the sacrifice is given in Philocalus as "ad nixas" (*ciconiae nixae*, three carved storks with interlocking bills near the Tiber), but Fowler notes this was non-existent under the Republic; the actual site must have been an old *ara Martis* in the Campus.
- **Aition**: Timaeus connected it (after the Greek fashion) with the legend of Troy and the wooden horse; Fowler dismisses this as characteristically Timaean mythology. The real meaning Fowler follows Mannhardt in tracing to a primitive agricultural harvest festival: (1) it is the last of the harvest-festival series (after Ambarvalia, Vestalia, Consualia, etc.); (2) the horse's head decked with cakes parallels the draught-animals at Vestalia and Consualia — Paulus states the rite was "ob frugum eventum" (on account of the past harvest); (3) the Corn-spirit theory of Mannhardt — the sacrificed horse represents the Corn-spirit, whose head fixed prominently brings fertility and wards off evil for the coming year; (4) the blood mixed with ashes of unborn calves (Fordicidia) and sulphur/bean-straw was distributed at the Parilia (April 21) as a purifying medicine. Fowler also accepts the Wissowa parallel with the arms-purification calendar: October 15 horse-race and October 19 Armilustrium mirror March 14 horse-race and March 19 Quinquatrus (lustratio armorum), marking the close of the war-season.
- **Page**: pp. 241–250
- **Notes**: Extended discussion (9 pages). Primary ancient sources: Festus 178 (Verrius Flaccus); Paul. 179 and 220; Philocalus calendar; Timaeus ap. Polybius 12.4b; Plutarch, *Quaest. Rom.* 97. Modern sources: Mannhardt, *Mythologische Forschungen* 156–201; Frazer, *Golden Bough* ii.64; Roscher, "Mars," *Myth. Lex.* 2416; Wissowa, *de Feriis* ix–x. Fowler explicitly notes that "probably, though not quite certain," the blood from the tail was used at the Parilia together with Fordicidia ashes. He gives a nuanced verdict: the rite originated as an agricultural harvest custom and later took on a martial character as Mars developed from a general deity of herds and crops into the god of warriors.

## Armilustrium

- **Day**: 19 (XIV Kal. Nov.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (stated; calendars: Arv., Sab., Maff., Amit., Ant.)
- **Deity**: Mars (implied; Salii perform the rite)
- **Ritual**: The Salii performed sacred rites in arms and sounded trumpets during the sacrifice (*"armati sacra faciebant ac dum sacrificarent tubis canebant"* — Festus 19). The arms (*arma*) and sacred shields (*ancilia*) were purified (*lustrata*) and put away for the winter. This was the last day of the Salii's active season; they did not appear again until the following spring. The place of the lustratio was on the Aventine *"ad circum maximum"* (Varro, *L.L.* 5.153) — Fowler finds no satisfactory explanation for this location (no Mars altar known there); he suggests it was probably the last station of a Salii procession.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Page**: pp. 250–251
- **Notes**: Name explained by Varro and Verrius: *"Armilustrium ab eo quod in armilustrio armati sacra faciunt... ab ludendo aut lustro, quod circumibant ludentes ancilibus armati"* (*L.L.* 6.62). Practical evidence that the Salii were active on this day: in 190 BCE a Roman army had to halt at the Hellespont because *"dies... quibus ancilia moventur, religiosi ad iter inciderant"* (Livy 37.33.7) — the commander Scipio was a Salius, and the army had left Italy in July (hence autumn). Fowler draws the symmetry explicitly: March 19 (Quinquatrus, lustratio armorum, season opens) ↔ October 19 (Armilustrium, lustratio armorum, season closes). After this day, no Mars-related festivals appear until March 1; Fowler compares this to Apollo's seasonal absence.

| 5 | Mundus patet | O | — | 239 |
| 7 | Iovi Fulguri, Iunoni Curitis in Campo | F | Iuppiter Fulgur; Iuno Curitis | 239 |

| 19 | Armilustrium | IP | Mars (Salii) | 250–251 |

**Not covered by Fowler in this section**: Ieiunium Cereris (Oct. 4, a late institution, 191 BCE per Livy 36.37; likely absent from Fowler's Republican-period scope or treated elsewhere).

**Thematic thread**: Fowler presents October as framed by the *close of the war season* (Oct. 15 horse-race; Oct. 19 Armilustrium) mirroring the March opening, and simultaneously as the *final harvest festival* — the October Horse being his most extended discussion, tracing the rite from primitive agricultural corn-spirit ritual through to its militarized Republican form. The Tigillum Sororium he leaves genuinely open, rejecting both the Horatius legend and the Janus-arch theory as unsatisfactory.
