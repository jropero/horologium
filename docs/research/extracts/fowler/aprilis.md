# Aprilis — Fowler (1899)

**Source**: W. Warde Fowler, *The Roman Festivals of the Period of the Republic* (1899)
**PDF pages used**: 77–109 (printed pp. 66–98; May chapter starts at printed p. 98)
**OCR note**: pdftotext reads "IP" in calendar-status positions throughout; from context and standard usage these are "NP" (nefas publicum). Reported as NP below. The letter "N" in the old typeface is rendered as "I" by the OCR.

---

## Veneralia
- **Day**: 1 (Kal. Apr.)
- **Fasti status**: F (Philocalus)
- **Deity**: Fortuna Virilis (older, popular stratum); Venus Verticordia (later, upper-class)
- **Ritual**: Women — including *humiliores* — bathed in men's public baths adorned with myrtle garlands, worshipping Fortuna Virilis for good fortune in relations with men. Women of rank (*honestiores*) supplicated Venus Verticordia. Fowler follows Mommsen in restoring the Praenestine note to read: "honestiores Veneri Verticordiae, humiliores etiam in balineis … Fortunae Virili." The two cults were distinct in Ovid but were amalgamated under the Empire.
- **Aition**: Temple of Venus Verticordia founded 114 BCE by Sibylline injunction after discovery of Vestal incest, "quo facilius virginum mulierumque mens a libidine ad pudicitiam converteretur."
- **Page**: pp. 67–69
- **Notes**: Fowler concludes the April 1 cult of Venus was introduced as a foreign element (Cyprian Aphrodite / Astarte via Sicily and Latium) onto an older Roman custom of Fortuna Virilis. Macrobius and Varro both assert Venus had no original share in the day. The name "Verticordia" is a Roman cult-title added to a foreign deity of questionable reputation.

## Megalesia (Ludi Megalesiaci)
- **Day**: 4 (Prid. Non. Apr.); ludi extended to 10 (Apr. 10, dies natalis of the temple)
- **Fasti status**: C (Maff.); Ludi attested from Apr. 4–10 inclusive (C.I.L. 314)
- **Deity**: Magna Mater Idaea (Cybele)
- **Ritual**: The sacred stone representing the goddess, brought from Pessinus in Phrygia in 204 BCE by order of a Sibylline oracle, was deposited in the temple of Victory on the Palatine on April 4. The day was made a festival, though normally no Roman festival falls between Kalends and Nones. Romans were forbidden by senatus consultum to participate in the cult service. The *nobilitas* gave dinner-parties (*mutationes cenarum*) on April 4; the plebs was excluded until the Cerealia. The temple itself was dedicated April 10. The festival name "Megalesia" is Greek (from *megale*, the goddess's title), as Cicero noted. Later and more extravagant cult developments came only under the Empire.
- **Aition**: A Sibylline oracle promised that the presence of this deity would drive the enemy (Hannibal) from Italy. The stone arrived in 204 BCE and was received with enthusiasm; a fine harvest followed, Hannibal evacuated Italy the next year.
- **Page**: pp. 69–71
- **Notes**: Fowler stresses that the cult was kept deliberately foreign: Greek name, Romans barred from service, no integration into traditional Roman religion. Attests the political skill of the Senate in enlisting Eastern cults at a critical moment.

## Fordicidia (also Hordicidia, Hordicalia, Fordicalia)
- **Day**: 15 (XVII Kal. Mai.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (Caer., Maff., Vat., Praen.)
- **Deity**: Tellus
- **Ritual**: Slaughter of pregnant cows (*hordae* or *fordae*): one on the Capitol for the state and one in each of the thirty *curiae* (thirty cows total for the curiae). The *virgo vestalis maxima* and her attendants tore the unborn calves from the wombs of the slaughtered mothers and burned them; the ashes were kept by the Vestals for use at the Parilia a few days later. This was the first festival of the year in which the Vestals took an active part, and the first of a series of acts connected with the growth, ripening, and harvesting of crops. Its purpose was to procure fertility of the corn growing "in the womb of mother earth" to whom the sacrifice was offered. Fowler calls it "beyond doubt one of the oldest sacrificial rites in the Roman religion."
- **Aition**: None given by Fowler explicitly. The connection with Tellus and the corn-fertility cycle is the functional explanation offered.
- **Page**: pp. 71–72
- **Notes**: The second of the two great festivals of the *curiae* (the other being Fornacalia, Feb. 17). The ashes link this rite directly to the Parilia (Apr. 21). Fowler draws a parallel with a Chinese spring festival described by Sir John Barrow (1804) involving a porcelain cow image.

## Fortuna Primigenia oracle at Praeneste
- **Day**: 11 (III Id. Apr.)
- **Fasti status**: N (Fasti Praen. fragment)
- **Deity**: Fortuna Primigenia
- **Ritual**: The oracle of the great temple of Fortuna Primigenia at Praeneste was open to suppliants on this day. Two duoviri sacrificed a calf. Not strictly a Roman festival, but noted by Fowler as the most renowned oracle in Italy.
- **Aition**: Not given.
- **Page**: p. 72
- **Notes**: Fowler explicitly says this is "not a Roman festival" but includes it for its fame throughout Italy. The cult of Fortuna is discussed more fully under June 24 and Sept. 13.

## Cerealia (Cerialia)
- **Day**: 19 (XIII Kal. Mai.); games (*ludi Ceriales*) ran from Apr. 12 to Apr. 19
- **Fasti status**: NP (Caer., Maff., Praen., Esq.) — Apr. 19 marked in large letters in the calendars
- **Deity**: Ceres, Liber, Libera (corresponding to Demeter, Dionysus, Persephone)
- **Ritual**: April 19 was the dies natalis of the temple of Ceres, Liber, and Libera at the foot of the Aventine (dedicated 493 BCE). Ludi Cereales ran from the 12th to the 19th. On the 19th the distinctive old Italian ritual survived: foxes had burning brands (*taedae*) fastened to their tails and were let loose to run in the Circus Maximus. The *ludi* proper (as fully organized games) cannot be proved earlier than 202 BCE (Livy 30.39), but the festival itself is older. The temple was under plebeian aediles; its rites and decorations were Greek from the beginning.
- **Aition**: Temple founded in consequence of a famine in 496 BCE, in obedience to a Sibylline oracle; dedicated 493 BCE. The fox-custom is explained by Ovid via a folktale from Carseoli (a boy who set a captive vixen on fire caused crop damage; hence a law against foxes and the ritual re-enactment), which Fowler dismisses as etiological myth. Preller interprets the foxes as a *sinnbildliche Erinnerung* of *robigo* (red fox / mildew). Mannhardt sees corn-spirits.
- **Page**: pp. 72–79
- **Notes**: Fowler devotes substantial discussion to whether Ceres was originally an Italian deity (connected etymologically with *cerus* = genius / *creare*) overlaid with Greek Demeter, or an importation. He concludes she was a genuine Italian earth-power, probably identical with the Dea Dia of the Fratres Arvales, and with Tellus, Bona Dea, and Ops. The plebeian connection of both temple and games is strong. Cerealia was the plebs' counterpart to the patrician Megalesia.

## Parilia (also Palilia)
- **Day**: 21 (XI Kal. Mai.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (Caer., Maff., Praen.); also marked ROMA CONDITA, NATALIS URBIS, CIRCENSES MISSUS XXIV (Caer., Philoc.)
- **Deity**: Pales (deity of both sexes or indeterminate sex; rustic shepherd deity)
- **Ritual**: Both public and private; urban and rustic. The rustic rite (from Ovid): (1) Sheep-folds decorated with green boughs and wreaths; (2) At earliest daybreak, the shepherd purified the sheep by sprinkling and sweeping the fold; fires were made of heaps of straw, olive-branches, and laurel (crackling gave good omen); shepherds and flocks leapt through and were driven through the flames; burning sulphur was also used; (3) Offerings to Pales of baskets of millet, millet cakes, pails of milk, followed by a shared meal; a prayer recited four times facing east, hands wetted with morning dew, asking Pales to avert disease and ensure fertile flocks; (4) Milk and *sapa* (heated wine) drunk until fumes were felt, then a leap over burning heaps. The urban festival: connected to the birthday of Rome; the Vestals supplied the *suffimen* — a mixture of ashes from the unborn calves of the Fordicidia plus blood from the head of the October Horse (kept since October), thrown on heaps of burning bean-straw; men and women leapt through the resulting smoke and flames on the Palatine Hill. Ovid himself participated and leapt three times through the flames. The *suffimen* components are called *februa casta* (holy purifying agents).
- **Aition**: None explicitly given by Fowler for the origin of the rite. The pastoral derivation (Sabine hill-country; Pales called *rusticola*, *pastoricia*, *silvicola*) is the structural explanation. The Palatine urban form became entangled with the legend of Rome's foundation (Romulus), so the day was also celebrated as the birthday of Rome. Fowler notes that "the sacrificing priest was originally the Rex Sacrorum" (Dionys. 1.88), suggesting extreme antiquity.
- **Page**: pp. 79–84
- **Notes**: Fowler calls the Parilia "at once one of the oldest and best attested festivals of the whole year." The triple linkage with Fordicidia and the October Horse sacrifice is central: all three rites form a coherent agricultural-purificatory system. Pales is described as "a common deity of Italian pasture land" surviving in Rome "as little more than a name from the oldest times." The prayer-to-east with dew is compared by Fowler to the Catholic shepherd of the Campagna. He also connects the leaping-through-fire to Easter and Midsummer fires across northern Europe (Mannhardt / Frazer).

## Vinalia Priora
- **Day**: 23 (IX Kal. Mai.)
- **Fasti status**: F (Caer.), also shown as FP (Caer., second entry — Fowler notes this peculiar double notation is shared with Aug. 19 Vinalia Rustica and Feb. 21 Feralia); NP (Maff., Praen.)
- **Deity**: Jupiter (original and correct, per Varro and Verrius); Venus secondarily from 181 BCE (dies natalis of temple of Venus Erycina)
- **Ritual**: The wine-skins were first opened and libations of new wine made to Jupiter ("Vinalia diem festum habebant, quo die vinum novum Iovi libabant" — Verrius ap. Festus 65 and 374). Wine was then tasted. A notice posted in vineyards at Tusculum (Varro) warned that new wine was not to be brought into the city until the Vinalia had been proclaimed on the Nones. The Flamen Dialis performed the *vindemiam auspicatus est* (plucked the first grapes after sacrifice). Libations were poured also "from the temple of Venus" (Plutarch Q.R. 45 — he calls this the Veneralia, confusing it with the Vinalia). From 181 BCE the day was also the *dies natalis* of the temple of Venus Erycina outside the Porta Collina.
- **Aition**: Praenestine calendar note: "Cum Latini bello permerentur a Rutulis, quia Mezentius rex Etruscorum paciscebatur, si subsidio venisset, omnium annorum vim fructum [i.e. the wine harvest would be vowed to him]" — Latins vowed it instead to Jupiter. Ovid (Fasti 4.877–900) gives this Mezentius legend. Fowler leaves the legend aside and accepts the simpler explanation: the day was Jupiter's, and Venus's temple happened to be founded on it.
- **Page**: pp. 85–88
- **Notes**: Fowler states emphatically: "The Vinalia, then, both in April and August, was really and originally sacred to Jupiter." The Venus connection is secondary, dating only from 181 BCE, when the temple of Venus Erycina was founded outside the Porta Collina. He also notes Venus's connection to gardens and vineyards (Varro R.R. 1.1) may have facilitated the popular association. The connection of the Flamen Dialis (priest of Jupiter, not of Liber) to the *auspicatio* of the vintage confirms Jupiter's ownership. Prostitutes also claimed this as their feast-day (alongside the Floralia).

## Robigalia
- **Day**: 25 (VII Kal. Mai.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (Caer., Esq., Maff., Praen.)
- **Deity**: Robigus (spirit of red rust / mildew; possibly a form or indigitation of Mars, per Tertullian: "Marti et Robigini Numa ludos instituit")
- **Ritual**: Procession to the grove of Robigus at the fifth milestone on the Via Claudia. The Flamen Quirinalis (Quirinus being a form of Mars) led the sacrifice: victims were a dog and a sheep. The officiant carried a *patera*, a *turis acerra* (incense-box), and a rough linen napkin. Ovid (who witnessed the ceremony) records the prayer addressed to Robigus: "Aspera Robigo, parcas Cerialibus herbis…" asking the deity not to harm the growing crops. Separately attested is a sacrifice of reddish sucking whelps (*rufae canes*) outside the Porta Catularia, connected either with this festival or an *augurium canarium* tied to the stage when ears begin to emerge from their sheaths.
- **Aition**: Not explicitly given by Fowler beyond the functional one: the dangerous mildew (*robigo*) attacks cereals as the ear is forming, precisely at the end of April in Italy. The dog sacrifice was explained by Ovid (and the priest) as substituting for the Dog-star (Sirius/Canis), but Fowler calls this certainly mistaken: Sirius disappears (does not rise) at sunset on Apr. 25. The real meaning of the dog victim was unknown to both priest and poet; Mannhardt and Festus suggest the rufous colour of the whelps was meant to resemble ripe corn ("Rufae canes immolabantur, ut fruges flavescentes ad maturitatem perducerentur").
- **Page**: pp. 88–91
- **Notes**: Fowler regards this as one of the most genuinely old Roman agricultural rites. The races (*ludi cursoribus maioribus minoribusque*) noted in the Praenestine calendar are a later accretion. By late antiquity the festival gave way to the Christian *Litania maior* (still on April 25, now St. Mark's Day), which had the same agricultural protective purpose. The grove was apparently at the junction of the Via Claudia with the Via Flaminia near the Milvian bridge (Mommsen's identification).

## Floralia (Ludi Florae)
- **Day**: 28 Apr. (IV Kal. Mai.) to 3 May (Non. Mai.); dies natalis of temple Apr. 28; formal *Florae* festival on May 3 (Non. Mai.) marked C (Ven.)
- **Fasti status**: NP for Apr. 28 (Maff., Praen.); C for May 3 Flora (Ven.); ludi on intervening days also attested
- **Deity**: Flora
- **Ritual**: Games established 238 BCE by plebeian aediles (the Publicii, using fines from defaulting holders of *ager publicus*) in consequence of a dearth, following consultation of the Sibylline Books. A temple was dedicated to Flora *ad circum maximum* on April 28 of that year. The games as eventually organized ran to May 3. Key features: (1) Indecency was licensed, especially on the original day (Apr. 28); drunkenness; the prostitutes of Rome claimed it as their feast-day; *mimae* would undress (Cato the Younger withdrew rather than watch); (2) Hares and goats were let loose in the Circus Maximus on the later days; (3) Vetches, beans, and lupines were scattered among the people in the Circus (*cicer*, etc., per Persius). The ludi did not become regular and annual until 173 BCE (Ovid Fasti 5.295). The ludi overlapped with the Bona Dea rite on May 1.
- **Aition**: Temple dedicated and games instituted because of crop failure (*propter sterilitatem frugum*, Praenestine note). Fowler notes Flora was almost certainly an old Italian deity (Flamen Floralis, appearance in Acta Fratrum Arvalium alongside Janus and Vesta), probably related to Ceres and Venus. The legend that Flora was a harlot whose legacy funded the games (Lactantius) Fowler dismisses as a late rationalization of the indecency of the festival.
- **Page**: pp. 91–95
- **Notes**: Not marked in the oldest calendars in the large letters indicating extreme antiquity, so Fowler classifies it as relatively late — later than Robigalia and Fordicidia. He sees it as a plebeian foundation parallel to Cerealia. The scattering of legumes he connects (via Mannhardt) to fertility symbolism widespread in marriage rites worldwide. The wearing of parti-coloured dress during the Floralia is noted (Ovid Fasti 5.355) in contrast to the white worn at Cerealia. Flora herself is identified as an ancient Italian deity with Sabine connections (Varro L.L. 5.74: she was Sabine; a Sabine month *Flusalis* = Floralis). Fowler suggests the characteristics of Flora resemble Aphrodite and that Greek influence may have shaped the Floralia's lasciviousness, especially after the Syrian war (the *ludi* became annual in 173 BCE, i.e. post-Syrian war).

## Feriae Latinae
- **Day**: No fixed date; normally held in April in historical times, announced by consuls on the Ides of March (before 153 BCE) or after January 1 (from 153 BCE)
- **Fasti status**: Not listed in Roman city fasti (held on the Alban Mount, not at Rome)
- **Deity**: Jupiter Latiaris
- **Ritual**: All magistrates or deputies of Latin cities assembled at the temple of Jupiter Latiaris on the Alban Mount. The Roman consul offered a libation of milk; deputies from other cities brought sheep, cheeses, or other pastoral offerings. Characteristic rite: slaughter of a pure white heifer that had never felt the yoke, by the consul on behalf of all cities. The flesh of the victim was divided and consumed by all deputies in a common meal (*sacramentum*). A city left out of the allotment or denied its portion had to be given one; failure required *instauratio fenarum*. General festivity followed; *oscilla* (small puppets) hung from branches of trees. A truce between all cities was in force. Both consuls had to be present (leaving a *praefectus urbi* at Rome); if both were absent a dictator *Feriarum Latinarum causa* could be appointed.
- **Aition**: Fowler: "perhaps no festival, Greek or Roman, carries us over such a vast period of time as this; its features betray its origin in the pastoral age." Foundation of the temple on the Alban Mount attributed to the Tarquinii (confirmed by the character of the foundations, resembling the Capitoline temple). The absence of wine (the offerings are milk, cheese, and sheep) proves the origin predates the introduction of the grape to Italy. Fowler interprets the common meal as a survival from the age when cattle were sacred animals slain only annually at clan renewal rituals (Robertson Smith).
- **Page**: pp. 95–97
- **Notes**: Fowler stresses this festival's incomparable antiquity among all Roman-Latin festivals. As Rome absorbed Latium, Jupiter Latiaris yielded to the Capitoline Jupiter, but the festival continued until at least the end of the 3rd century CE. The white victim may be a reminiscence of a primitive white breed of cattle. The *oscilla* are connected with Paganalia and Terminalia; Fowler believes they were apotropaic (averting evil influences), not the complex symbolic objects the later Romans imagined.

## Summary

Fowler covers ten April entries in detail (printed pp. 66–98):

| Day | Festival | Status | Deity |
|-----|----------|--------|-------|
| 1 | Veneralia | F (Philoc.) | Fortuna Virilis / Venus Verticordia |
| 4–10 | Megalesia (Ludi Megalesiaci) | C (Maff.) | Magna Mater Idaea |
| 11 | Fortuna Primigenia oracle (Praeneste; not Roman) | N | Fortuna Primigenia |
| 15 | Fordicidia | NP | Tellus |
| 12–19 | Ludi Ceriales; 19 = Cerealia | NP (Apr. 19) | Ceres, Liber, Libera |
| 21 | Parilia | NP | Pales |
| 23 | Vinalia Priora | F/NP (mixed) | Jupiter (Venus secondarily) |
| 25 | Robigalia | NP | Robigus (form of Mars) |
| 28–May 3 | Floralia (Ludi Florae) | NP (Apr. 28) | Flora |
| variable (usu. Apr.) | Feriae Latinae | (not in city fasti) | Jupiter Latiaris |

**Key Fowler emphases for April**:
- The oldest festivals (Fordicidia, Robigalia, Parilia) form an interlocking agricultural-purificatory system connected through the Vestal ashes.
- The Cerealia and Floralia are plebeian foundations, structurally parallel to each other.
- The Megalesia is foreign from start (Greek name, Romans banned from service, nobilitas only).
- The Vinalia is Jupiter's festival throughout; Venus's association is a late accretion (from 181 BCE).
- Female and gender-ambiguous deities dominate April (Pales, Ceres, Flora, Fortuna, Venus, Tellus): Fowler reads this as a mark of great antiquity.
- April has a large number of *dies nefasti* between the Nones and the 22nd.
