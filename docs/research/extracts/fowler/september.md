## Ludi Romani (Ludi Magni)

- **Days**: Sep 5–19 (in Cicero's time; Sep 4 probably added after Caesar's death per Mommsen, C.I.L. 338)
- **Fasti status**: Not stated as a unit; individual days are ludi days; the central day (Sep 13, Ides) has NP status
- **Deity**: Jupiter (Capitolinus)
- **Ritual**: Games extending on either side of the Ides of September. Fowler says their "real centre-point and original day" was the pompa from the Capitol to the Circus Maximus, probably Sep 15 (two days after the Epulum; Sep 14 was postridianus/unlucky and occupied by the equorum probatio). The equorum probatio (preliminary to the races) took place on Sep 14, the day after the Ides. The ludi originated in ludi votivi — vows made by kings or consuls at the opening of campaigns and paid to Jupiter after victory, the standard formula being preserved by Livy 36.2.3 (vow for the war against Antiochus). Mommsen's essay in Römische Forschungen vol. ii p. 42 ff. is the key source on their origin. The name "ludi Romani" is the fully organized form of these originally votive ludi; "ludi magni" (ludi magni votivi) is the older or more solemn designation.
- **Aition**: Vows made and discharged to Jupiter by Roman generals after the close of wars; the performance of consular vota at the end of the campaigning season. Fowler connects their spread around the Ides to the Ides' sacred status as dies Iovis and the foundation day of the Capitoline temple.
- **Page**: pp. 215–216, 217
- **Notes**: The length Sep 5–19 is attested for Cicero's time (Verr. i.10.31). The addition of Sep 4 after Caesar's death is Mommsen's inference (C.I.L. 338). Fowler does not catalogue the individual days of the ludi in detail; his main interest is the Epulum Iovis and the cult of Jupiter.

## Epulum Iovis

- **Day**: 13 (Ides of September)
- **Fasti status**: The Ides are NP (sacred to Jupiter); Fowler notes the Ides of September was further distinguished as the day of the Epulum Iovis and the dies natalis of the Capitoline temple
- **Deity**: Jupiter, Juno, Minerva (the Capitoline Triad)
- **Ritual**: A public sacrificial banquet held in the Capitoline temple in the presence of the images of the three Capitoline deities. The ceremony began with a sacrifice; the victim is not specified in the sources but Fowler judges it was "no doubt a heifer, and probably a white one." The images of the gods were decked out as for a feast; Jupiter's face was painted red with minium (like a triumphator). Jupiter had a couch (lectus); Juno and Minerva each had a sella. The meal proceeded in the visible presence of the gods' images, shared by the magistrates and senate. The collegium of epulones (established 196 BCE) had charge of this and other public feasts (Gellius 13.8.2; Liv. 38.57.5). Fowler regards the Epulum as originating in a form of thanksgiving to Jupiter for the preservation of the state from the perils of the war season; no better day could be chosen than the foundation day of the Capitoline temple (Aust, Myth. Lex. s.v. Iuppiter, 732).
- **Aition**: Thanksgiving to Jupiter for the state's preservation through the war season (Aust's explanation, endorsed by Fowler). The origin of the anthropomorphic iconic form — gods visibly present at the feast — is attributed by Fowler to Graeco-Etruscan influence introduced by the Etruscan dynasty that built the Capitoline temple, not to native Italian practice. Marquardt (Röm. Staatsverw. 348–349) attempted to trace it to the domestic rite of Jupiter Dapalis (Cato, R.R. 139), but Fowler does not find this parallel convincing.
- **Page**: pp. 217–220, 234
- **Notes**: Fowler reports (following Marq. 349; Mommsen, C.I.L. 329, 333) that some scholars believe the Epulum Iovis originally belonged to the Ides of November and the Ludi Plebeii, as Livy does not mention it in connexion with the Ludi Romani, and the first attestation in September is in the Augustan calendars. Fowler follows Aust in placing it as genuinely earlier than the Ludi Plebeii (received date 220 BCE) and holding it on Sep 13. The Vestals prepared the mola salsa on Sep 13 (Serv. Ecl. 8.32) — one of three days in the year when this was done; Fowler floats the conjecture that Vesta was the original deity of the rite, with Jupiter and wine-offerings later additions, but does not press it.

## Dies Natalis Templi Capitolini

- **Day**: 13 (Ides of September)
- **Fasti status**: Part of the NP Ides; two calendars (not named) attest this specifically
- **Deity**: Jupiter Capitolinus (the Capitoline Triad)
- **Ritual**: Anniversary of the dedication of the great Capitoline temple. Fowler notes that the coincidence of the dies natalis with the Ides of September and the Epulum Iovis creates a very close connexion between the Ludi Romani and the cult of Jupiter. Plutarch (Poplicola 14) and Aust (Lex. s.v. Iuppiter, 707) are cited for the dies natalis.
- **Aition**: Not stated; the dies natalis is simply the foundation day of the temple.
- **Page**: p. 217, 158–159 (in the text as transcribed)
- **Notes**: The nail-driving ceremony (clavus annalis) was also located on this day by Roman tradition; see separate entry below.

## Clavus Annalis (Nail-Driving Ceremony)

- **Day**: 13 (Ides of September)
- **Fasti status**: Not stated
- **Deity**: Connected to Jupiter's Capitoline temple; Fowler (following Mommsen) re-attributes the rite to Fortuna/Necessitas (Etruscan)
- **Ritual**: A nail (clavus) was driven into the wall of the cella Minervae in the Capitoline temple by the "Praetor maximus" (= dictator clavi figendi causa), in obedience to an old lex fixed on the wall of the adjacent cella. Sources: Livy 7.3 and Festus p. 55 (Verrius Flaccus). However, Mommsen's criticism (Röm. Chronologie p. 175 ff.) argues decisively that this was NOT an annual rite but occurred once, or very infrequently (Fowler says "once in a century"), to commemorate a vow made in 463 BCE during the great pestilence that killed both consuls. The dictatorship was created specifically for this purpose (dictator clavi figendi causa); when the dictatorship was abandoned after the Second Punic War, the ceremony fell into oblivion, and later Roman antiquarians mistakenly described it as an annual year-counting rite. The nail symbolizes Fortuna or Necessitas; the rite was Etruscan, celebrated also at Volsinii in the temple of the Etruscan deity of Fate, and transplanted to Rome.
- **Aition**: Etruscan origin; Fowler (following Mommsen and Horace, Od. 1.35 and 3.24) sees the nail as a symbol of Fortuna/saeva Necessitas/Atropos, not a year-marker. The specific Roman occasion: a vow in 463 BCE during the pestilence (Fowler, p. 235).
- **Page**: pp. 234–235
- **Notes**: Roman scholars believed it annual and connected it to counting of years. Mommsen's reinterpretation is accepted by Fowler. The lex was physically fixed on the wall of the adjacent cella. This is one of the most debated archaisms of the Roman religious calendar.

## Summary

Fowler's September chapter (printed pp. 215–235; PDF pp. 226–246) contains remarkably few discrete festival entries. The author opens by noting that "the Calendar of this month is almost a blank" — only the Kalends, Nones, and Ides are marked in the large letters of the public fasti, with no other festival assigned its own deity in the calendar proper. The usable data is:

| Day | Festival | Deity | Source |
|-----|----------|-------|--------|
| 5–19 | Ludi Romani (Ludi Magni) | Jupiter | p. 215–216 |
| 13 | Epulum Iovis | Jupiter, Juno, Minerva | pp. 217–220, 234 |
| 13 | Dies Natalis Templi Capitolini | Jupiter Capitolinus | p. 217 |
| 13 | Clavus Annalis (nail ceremony) | [Fortuna/Necessitas] | pp. 234–235 |

The bulk of the chapter (pp. 218–234) is a long scholarly excursus on the nature and development of the cult of Jupiter throughout Italy (Umbria, Sabellic peoples, Oscan, Etruscan, Latin, Praenestine, and finally Roman forms), provoked by the anthropomorphic Epulum. This discussion contains no additional datable September festivals. The September chapter has no Volturnalia entry — that festival (vi Kal. Sept. = Aug. 27) appears in the preceding August chapter and was extracted from the top of the same PDF pages but belongs to August.

**October begins**: printed p. 236 (PDF p. 247), confirmed by the text "MENSIS OCTOBER / IN the Italy of historical times, the one agricultural feature of this month was the vintage."

### Caveats and open items

1. Fowler does not list the Fasti day-letters (F/N/NP/EN/C) individually for the ludi days.
2. The Epulum Iovis date in September is accepted by Fowler but he notes a minority view placing it originally in November (with the Ludi Plebeii).
3. The Volturnalia (Aug. 27) appears in the text immediately before the MENSIS SEPTEMBER heading but is an August entry, not September.
4. The clavus ceremony is not securely annual; Fowler and Mommsen treat it as a one-off (or at most extremely infrequent) Etruscan-derived rite.
5. No aition is given for the Ludi Romani beyond the general vow-fulfillment rationale; no founding myth is cited.
