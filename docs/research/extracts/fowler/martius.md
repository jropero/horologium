**Source**: W. Warde Fowler, *The Roman Festivals of the Period of the Republic* (Macmillan, 1899)

## Kal. Mart. — Feriae Marti / Matronalia (March 1)

- **Day**: 1 (Kal. Mart.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (Feriae — the day is marked as Feriae in the Fasti; Philocalus also notes N = natalis Martis, which Fowler considers very doubtful)
- **Deity**: Mars (primary); Juno Lucina (temple dedication on this day, Esquiline)
- **Ritual**: The sacred fire of Vesta was renewed; fresh laurels were fixed on the Regia, the Curiae, and the houses of the flamens. The Aedes Vestae was also freshly decorated (Macrobius, Sat. 1.12.6; Ovid, Fasti 3.135 ff.). This was the New Year's day of the Roman religious calendar. The day was also called **Matronalia** (not a technical religious term): wives received presents from their husbands and entertained their slaves. It was also the first day on which the Salii 'moved' the ancilia: they took the sacred shields from the sacrarium Martis in the Regia and carried them through the city in procession, leaping, dancing, striking the shields, and singing the Salian hymns. Dionysius (2.70) describes these processions, which continued until the 24th.
- **Aition**: Fowler notes the legend that the original ancile fell from heaven (or was found in Numa's house) on this day, and that the first of the twelve copies was made by Mamurius (mentioned in the Carmen Saliare). He also records — but doubts — Philocalus' notation that this is the *natalis Martis*, noting no early Latin source corroborates a birth of Mars on this day. The temple of Juno Lucina was dedicated on this day (probably 375 BCE per Pliny, NH 16.235), and Fowler thinks this dedication-day may have suggested the motherhood of Juno and the birth of Mars.
- **Page**: pp. 36–39
- **Notes**: Fowler is explicitly sceptical of the *natalis Martis* being ancient (Philocalus is 4th century CE, "urban and not a rustic basis"). The 'Matronalia' label has no technical or religious significance according to Fowler. The Salian processions began today and ran continuously through March 24.

## Salii Processions (March 1–24, continuous)

- **Day**: 1–24 (throughout the month)
- **Fasti status**: not a single day entry; the processions span the month
- **Deity**: Mars
- **Ritual**: Twenty-four Salii (12 Palatini devoted to Mars Gradivus, 12 Collini/Agonales devoted to Quirinus) carried the twelve ancilia through Rome, leaping and dancing at sacred stations (mansiones Saliorum), singing the Carmen Saliare. Each evening the shields were hung up at a new station and the Salii feasted. Three days are specifically noted as major 'movings': March 1, March 9, and March 23 (Tubilustrium). The last active day of the Salii was March 24.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Page**: pp. 39–43
- **Notes**: Fowler draws on Frazer and Mannhardt to argue the rites functioned to protect the vegetation-spirit (Mars) from hostile demons at the opening of spring, and possibly to expel collected evil via a scapegoat (see March 14, Mamuralia). The Salii wore trabea and tunica picta, helmet, breastplate, and sword, carrying the ancile on the left arm and a staff to strike it. Membership required patrician birth and patrimi et matrimi status.

## Non. Mart. — Vediovis (March 7)

- **Day**: 7 (Non. Mart.)
- **Fasti status**: F (fastus — Fowler explicitly notes "dies fastus")
- **Deity**: Vediovis
- **Ritual**: Temple of Vediovis inter duos lucos. Fowler defers detailed discussion to May 21 (the dedication-day of the temple in arce), noting the cult has no obvious special connexion with other March rites.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 43
- **Notes**: The Fasti Praenestini note is partially damaged. Fowler reads the first word as [something + ]Vediovis; Mommsen suggests "[Aedis] Artis Vediovis" but concedes difficulty. Ovid (Fasti 3.429) confirms only a Vediovis reference for the Nones; it was not Feriae Martis. Fowler dismisses the day briefly here and treats the cult fully under May 21 and January.

## VII Id. Mart. — Arma Ancilia Movent (March 9)

- **Day**: 9 (VII Id. Mart.)
- **Fasti status**: C (comitialis — Fowler cites Philocalus)
- **Deity**: Mars (Salii)
- **Ritual**: Second principal day of 'moving' the ancilia. Fowler follows Marindin in suggesting the three major days of the Salian processions (March 1, 9, 23) correspond to the three most important mansiones Saliorum, two of which were probably the Curia Saliorum on the Palatine and the Sacrarium Martis in the Regia.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 43–44
- **Notes**: Lydus (4.42) gives the third major 'moving' as March 23 (Tubilustrium). The Salii were active throughout the month; these three days are the calendar-marked peaks.

## Prid. Id. Mart. — Equirria / Mamuralia (March 14)

- **Day**: 14 (Prid. Id. Mart.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (marked IP in Fasti Maffeiani and Vaticanus)
- **Deity**: Mars (Equirria); Mamurius Veturius (Mamuralia — 4th century and later)
- **Ritual**: **Equirria**: A horse-race (feriae Marti), the second Equirria of the year (the first was Feb. 27). Wissowa conjectures this was originally on the Ides (March 15) but moved back one day to allow attendance at both the Equirria and the very popular festival of Anna Perenna. **Mamuralia**: Philocalus (4th c. CE) and the rustic calendars record instead a 'Sacrum Mamurio / Mamuralia.' According to Lydus (de Mensibus 3.29 and 4.36 — 6th c. CE), a man clad in skins was led through the streets of Rome, beaten with long peeled rods, and driven out of the city amid shouts of 'Mamurius.'
- **Aition**: Lydus: the skin-clad man represented the legendary smith Mamurius Veturius who made the eleven copies of the ancile; he was beaten out of the city because the Romans suffered misfortune when they used the copies rather than the heaven-sent shield. Frazer (followed by Usener): the figure represented the old Mars (Mamurius Veturius = 'old Mars'), the spirit of vegetation of the past year, expelled at the new year so that the new Mars-spirit could thrive.
- **Page**: pp. 44–50
- **Notes**: Fowler is explicitly sceptical of the Mamurius-rite's antiquity. The older calendars (Maffeianus, Vaticanus, Caeretanus) record only the Equirria / feriae Marti; the Mamuralia appears only from Philocalus (354 CE) onwards. Fowler finds no certain Latin literary allusion to such a rite before that date — not in Ovid, Dionysius, Servius, or Plutarch. He concludes the Mamurius-legend grew out of the Carmen Saliare and that Lydus' account (which may conflate foreign or other Italian customs) is too uncertain to bear the weight Usener and Frazer built upon it. Servius (Aen. 7.188) mentions that 'pellem virgis caedunt,' and Minucius Felix (Octavius 24.3) notes the Salii 'pelles caedunt,' but these may refer to ritual use of skins by the Salii, not to a scapegoat rite. Mommsen holds that 'Mamuralia' was simply a popular nickname for the second Equirria among the vulgus, derived from the prominence of Mamurius in the Carmen Saliare.

## Id. Mart. — Anna Perenna (March 15)

- **Day**: 15 (Id. Mart.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (ISP — in speculum — i.e., the full Praenestini note; Fasti Maffeiani and Vaticanus mark the day only EID for the usual Ides sacrifice to Jupiter)
- **Deity**: Anna Perenna
- **Ritual**: The plebs streamed out to the Campus Martius near the Via Flaminia (ad lapidem primum, near the present Porta del Popolo), lying about on the grass in pairs of men and women, in the open or in improvised huts/tents. They spent the day in drinking and revelry, praying for as many years of life as they could swallow cups of wine, singing with dancing and gesticulation. They returned to the city drunk. Ovid says he witnessed this himself (Fasti 3.541). Macrobius (Sat. 1.12.6) records that sacrifice was offered in March to Anna Perenna 'ut annare perannareque commode liceat.' Lydus notes prayers for the salubrity of the coming year.
- **Aition**: Ovid tells two legends: (1) Anna (= Perenna) tricked Mars (who was in love with Minerva/Nerio) by presenting herself veiled as a bride; this explains obscene songs sung at the festival. (2) During the secession of the plebs to the Mons Sacer, an old woman of Bovillae named Anna supplied rustic cakes (liba) daily. Fowler regards both as secondary and untrustworthy: the first is a folk-tale of human beings later attached to the gods; the second he thinks grew out of the custom of old women selling liba on the Liberalia.
- **Page**: pp. 50–54
- **Notes**: Fowler concludes that Anna Perenna represented the circle/ring of the year (her name = feminine form of annus), propitiated at the first full moon of the new year (the Ides being the full moon in March). The festival had died out by the early Empire — absent from rustic calendars, Philocalus, and Silvius. The festival does not appear in the antiquissimi Fasti (not in the large letters of the Numan calendar). Fowler doubts she was a water-deity despite the Numicius legend, arguing that connexion arose from Aeneas already being associated with the Numicius.

## XVI Kal. Apr. — Liberalia / Agonalia (March 17)

- **Day**: 17 (XVI Kal. Apr.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (IP in the Fasti)
- **Deity**: Liber (primary); Jupiter (sacrifice on the Capitol); Iuventas (sacrifice by boys assuming the toga)
- **Ritual**: **Liberalia**: Old women (sacerdotes Liberi, crowned with ivy) sat throughout the city's streets with small portable altars (foculi) and offered cakes of oil and honey (liba) for sale; they sacrificed for the benefit of the buyer. The rustic population flocked into the city. **Agonalia**: A sacrifice by the Salii Collini (= Agonenses), known to priests only — the day's common name was Liberalia, not Agonalia. **Toga virilis**: This was the usual day for boys to assume the toga virilis (also called toga recta, pura, or libera). They sacrificed before leaving home and again on the Capitol (to Iuventas or Liber, or both). Ovid records army tirones were mustered on this day. **Argei circuit**: On this day and the previous day (March 16–17), some kind of procession 'went to the Argei' — i.e., made a circuit of the twenty-four Argeorum sacella in the four Servian regions of the city (Fowler defers full treatment to May 15).
- **Aition**: not stated (Fowler gives etymological discussions of 'Liber' as a creative/productive spirit akin to genius, but no founding myth for the festival)
- **Page**: pp. 54–57
- **Notes**: Fowler notes the conjunction of Liberalia and Agonalia on the same day is probably accidental (Wissowa, de Feriis xii); the day was universally known as Liberalia because Agonalia occurred four times a year and needed disambiguation. Liber is firmly distinguished from Dionysus — no connexion with Greek Dionysiac rites. The old women selling liba are called 'sacerdotes Liberi' by Varro (L.L. 6.14) 'by courtesy.' Fowler doubts this custom originally belonged to the Liberalia (see p. 53 footnote). The Fasti Caeretani explicitly list the Agonia: 'LIB. AG[ONIA]. LIBERO LIB.' The Fasti Vaticani list only the Agonia. Fasti Maffeiani and Farnese list only the Liberalia.

## KAL. XIV APR. — Quinquatrus (March 19)

- **Day**: 19 (XIV Kal. Apr. = 'fifth day after the Ides')
- **Fasti status**: NP from Caeretani and Vaticanus (IP); N from Maffeiani — Fowler does not resolve the discrepancy
- **Deity**: Mars (primary, original); Minerva (from the temple dedication, later association)
- **Ritual**: **Original**: Feriae Marti. The Salii danced in the Comitium (Varro, L.L. 5.85; Fasti Praenestini, completed by Mommsen) in the presence of pontifices and tribuni celerum. The ancilia were purified (lustrated), interpreted by Charisius as 'quod eo die arma ancilia lustrari sint solita' (though Fowler calls his etymology wrong, the ritual fact is accepted). This answered to the Armilustrium on Oct. 19. **Later addition**: The 19th became associated with Minerva because at least one of her temples (Caelian or Aventine, possibly both) was dedicated on this day (Fasti Praenestini: 'ARTIFICUM DIES [QUOD MINERVAE] AEDIS IN AVENTINO EO DIE EST [DEDICATA]'). Ovid confirms the Minerva connexion (Fasti 3.809 ff., 3.835 ff.). The five days 18th–23rd came to be permanently associated with Minerva and with artisans/guilds.
- **Aition**: The name 'Quinquatrus' = 'fifth day after the Ides' (Festus 254; this is the correct etymology for Fowler). The popular misunderstanding took it as a five-day period, leading the common people to apply the name to March 19–23.
- **Page**: pp. 57–62
- **Notes**: Fowler sees the Quinquatrus as the opening lustration of the army's arms for the season, paralleling the Armilustrium at season's end. The tribuni celerum present suggest the whole host (cavalry and infantry) were originally lustrated. Nerio, not Minerva, was originally associated with Mars at this season; Minerva supplanted her after the temple dedication. Fowler dismisses the elaborate myth of Mars's courtship of Nerio/Minerva as a Graecizing construction of the 3rd–2nd c. BCE. The 5-day popular 'Quinquatria' (March 19–23) was an extended festival for artisans particularly; Minerva as patron of crafts drove this extension.

## X Kal. Apr. — Tubilustrium (March 23)

- **Day**: 23 (X Kal. Apr.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (IP in Caeretani, Maffeiani, Vaticanus, Farnese, Min. III; Philocalus also marks it)
- **Deity**: Mars (Feriae Marti, per Fasti Praenestini: [FERIAE] MARTI); 'dea fortis' at the sacrifice — Fowler thinks this was later mistakenly taken for Minerva
- **Ritual**: The tubae (military trumpets) used in sacred ceremonies were purified in the Atrium Sutorium (location unknown), with a sacrifice of a lamb. This purified the instruments to be used at the comitia curiata the following day (March 24). Lydus (4.42) adds this was the last day on which the ancilia were 'moved' (third major movement), and that the Salii were also active on the 24th (Festus 278).
- **Aition**: Fasti Praenestini append a note: 'Lutatius quidem clavam eam ait esse in ruinis Pala[ti i]ncensi a Gallis repertam, qua Romulus urbem inauguraverit.' Fowler says this appears to confuse the lituus of Romulus (found on the Palatine, kept in the Curia Saliorum) with the lituus-shaped tuba, an error perhaps generated by the resemblance between the augur's baculum and the curved tuba.
- **Page**: pp. 62–64
- **Notes**: Fowler emphasizes the Tubilustrium was a Mars-festival; Minerva had no real connexion with it. The 'dea fortis' sacrifice at the Tubilustrium was incorrectly attributed to Minerva (as patron of trumpet-players) — a mistaken interpretation. The name means 'lustration of the tubae' (tuba = long straight brass tube with bell mouth, distinct from the curved lituus and the Italian tibia).

## IX Kal. Apr. — Q.R.C.F. / Quando Rex Comitiavit Fas (March 24)

- **Day**: 24 (IX Kal. Apr.)
- **Fasti status**: NP (IP in Vaticanus and Caeretani; Maffeiani and Praenestini give Q.REX.C.F.)
- **Deity**: no specific deity; the day is a procedural/constitutional one under the Rex sacrificulus
- **Ritual**: The comitia curiata met under the presidency of the Rex sacrificulus for the sanctioning (calatio) of wills, one of the two fixed days per year for this (the other being May 24). 'Q.R.C.F.' = *Quando Rex Comitiavit Fas*: judicial proceedings could only begin once the Rex had dissolved the assembly. The Salii were also active on this day (Festus 278).
- **Aition**: Fasti Praenestini record a popular but incorrect etymology: that the day was so named because the King fled the Comitium on this day (alluding to Tarquinius Superbus). Fowler follows Mommsen in rejecting this and favouring the procedural interpretation.
- **Page**: pp. 63–64
- **Notes**: This entry is taken in conjunction with May 23–24, which have identical calendar markings. The Tubilustrium on March 23 purified the tubae for use at the March 24 assembly. Fowler does not treat this as a religious festival in its own right, but as a constitutional event with religious overtones.
