# Mensis December — Fowler (1899)
**PDF pages used**: 267–287 (printed 256–276)

## Women's Sacrifice to the Bona Dea
- **Day**: 3/4 (night between Dec. 3 and 4, per Plutarch and Dio; date not fixed)
- **Fasti status**: Not in calendars
- **Deity**: Bona Dea (identified with Fauna, female form of Faunus)
- **Ritual**: Women-only rite held in the house of a consul or praetor ("in ea domo quae est in imperio"), attended by the Vestals; sacrifice of a young pig or pigs; performed "pro populo Romano" but not at public expense ("publico sumptu"); all men rigorously excluded. Originally a perfectly decorous rite; orgies described by Juvenal belong to the imperial period.
- **Aition**: Fowler characterises it as a survival from a time when the wife of the community chief, together with the daughters (represented by Vestals) and matrons, sacrificed to a deity of fertility.
- **Page**: p. 256

## Faunalia (Rustica)
- **Day**: 5 (Nones of December)
- **Fasti status**: F (Nones); but the rural Faunalia does not appear in Roman city calendars
- **Deity**: Faunus
- **Ritual**: Rural community (pagus) festival; a tender kid is sacrificed at an ancient altar (not a temple) with libations of wine and incense; the entire local farming community and their plough-oxen gather; after the sacrifice they dance in triple measure. Faunus was not introduced to the city of Rome until 196 BC (temple on Tiber island). The rite is known from Horace, Ode 3.18.
- **Page**: p. 257

## Agonalia (December) / Septimontium
- **Day**: 11 (H ID. DEC.)
- **Fasti status**: Appears as AG. IN. (Amiternian), AGON. (three other calendars). The Septimontiale sacrum may fall on the same day per Scaliger's conjecture from Festus.
- **Deity**: Unknown for Agonalia. Septimontium: sacrifice on the Palatium by the Flamen Palatualis (deity unknown, perhaps Pales or Palatua).
- **Ritual**: Agonalia: the Rex sacrorum may have sacrificed a ram in the Regia on all four Agonalia days (Jan. 9, Mar. 17, May 21, Dec. 11), though Dec. 11 is less certain. Septimontium: "feriae non populi sed montanorum modo" — rites pro montibus, not pro populo; no vehicles drawn by beasts of burden allowed in the city on this day (Plutarch, Q.R. 69). Fowler concludes the two were probably distinct ceremonies.
- **Aition**: None given.
- **Page**: p. 265–267

## Conso in Aventino
- **Day**: 12 (PRID. ID. DEC.)
- **Fasti status**: EN (Amiternian)
- **Deity**: Consus
- **Ritual**: A rite of Consus on the Aventine; the Amiternian calendar is the only source. Fowler merely cross-references the Consualia of Aug. 21 and notes this as the Aventine location.
- **Page**: p. 268

## Consualia (Winter)
- **Day**: 15 (XVIII K.IAN.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Maff. Praen. Amit. Ant.); Feriae Consoni (Praen. Amit.)
- **Deity**: Consus
- **Ritual**: Winter Consualia; see Fowler's discussion under Aug. 21. The Fasti Praenestini note that horses and mules were garlanded with flowers on this day ("equi et [muli floribus coronantur] quod in eius tu[tela]…"). Fowler suggests these winter rites may have originated in inspecting stored grain at mid-winter, and that the close sequence of Consualia, Opalia, and Saturnalia reflects a final review of the autumn harvest.
- **Page**: p. 268

## Saturnalia
- **Day**: 17 (XVI K.IAN., originally; the 19th became current through a calendar change that confused some people)
- **Fasti status**: IP (all major calendars: Maff. Amit. Guid. Rust. Philoc.); Feriae Saturno (Maff. Amit.)
- **Deity**: Saturnus (old agricultural god, possibly connected with serere/satio)
- **Ritual**: Public sacrifice at the temple of Saturn by the Forum; public feast following, at which feasters shouted "Io Saturnalia"; senators and equites wore toga for the sacrifice but laid it aside for the dinner. On the 18th and 19th: early bath, family sacrifice of a sucking pig, calls on friends, games, and exchange of gifts (cerei = wax candles, sigillaria = small paste/earthenware images). Slaves were waited on by their masters in equality. The festival extended in popular use to three (Augustus), later five, and sometimes seven days. Fowler notes it steadily gained in popularity and remained vigorous through Macrobius' time.
- **Aition**: Romans connected it with a legendary Golden Age under Saturn's reign; the lectisternium of 217 BC (Livy 22.1) introduced the public convivium.
- **Page**: p. 269–273

## Opalia
- **Day**: 19 (XIV K.IAN.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Maff. Amit.); Feriae Opi; Opi ad Forum (Amit.)
- **Deity**: Ops
- **Ritual**: Sacrifice to Ops "ad forum"; whether this was in a separate temple of Ops, in the Regia, or at the temple of Saturn is disputed (Mommsen, Wissowa, Aust differ). Fowler accepts that the "ad forum" location indicates a close functional connection between Saturnus and Ops, and places this among the mid-winter inspections of stored produce.
- **Page**: p. 273–274

## Divalia (Angeronalia)
- **Day**: 21 (XII K.IAN.)
- **Fasti status**: Feriae Divae Angeronae (Maff. Praen.)
- **Deity**: Diva Angerona; rite took place in the sacellum Volupiae (ara Volupiae)
- **Ritual**: Pontifices made sacrifice in the sacellum of Volupia. Angerona's statue had a gagged and sealed mouth. The Fasti Praenestini (mutilated text completed by Mommsen) mentions the goddess as averting angores (anxieties); some connected the day with the beginning of the new year. Fowler notes that the date coincides with the winter solstice centre, giving the strongest available clue to the festival's meaning.
- **Page**: p. 275

## Larentalia
- **Day**: 23 (X K.IAN.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Maff. Praen.); Feriae Iovi, Acca Larentiae (Praen. note)
- **Deity**: Acca Larentia (semi-deity); also Feriae Iovi (connection of Jupiter unexplained; Fowler calls it accidental)
- **Ritual**: Parentatio (rites for the dead) performed publicly at the tomb of Acca Larentia/Larentina in the Velabrum; Flamen Quirinalis and the Pontifices ("sacerdotes nostri publico parentant," Varro) took part. Fowler suggests this may be a survival of an originally December date for ancestor rites, preserved because the tomb of a semi-deity made it a public function. Compared with the February Parentalia.
- **Aition**: Praen.: Larentia called the wet-nurse of Romulus (some), or a wealthy courtesan who made the Roman people heir to a large fortune left her by her lover Tarutius.
- **Page**: p. 275–276

## Compitalia
- **Day**: Variable (conceptivae); between Saturnalia (Dec. 17) and Jan. 5; tendency to fall late in that period (Philocalus, Silvius)
- **Fasti status**: Not stativae until late Empire; announced by the praetor
- **Deity**: Lares Compitales (two shrines at each crossroads)
- **Ritual**: At street crossroads (compita) in the city, or at agricultural boundary-crossings in the country; sacella erected to Lares Compitales; magistri vicorum wore the toga praetexta; slaves and plebs participated (slaves included per Dionysius on Servius Tullius' institution). "Fracta iuga" (broken yokes) hung in the shrines, possibly as spells rather than simply as signs of finished work. Ludi Compitales accompanied the urban celebration (abolished 64 BC, revived 58 BC by Clodius, suppressed by Caesar, restored by Augustus).
- **Aition**: Ascribed to Servius Tullius or Tarquinius Superbus; Fowler regards it as organized in the Etruscan period from older rustic practice.
- **Page**: p. 279–281 (treated under January entry for Jan. 3–5)

## Summary
Fowler presents December as a "slack time" for agriculturists, filled with antique and obscure festivals nearly all traceable to husbandry and primitive country life. The sequence Consualia–Saturnalia–Opalia reflects a final mid-winter reckoning of the harvest; the Saturnalia is by far the most fully documented, with detailed treatment of its popular customs, slave equality, gift-giving (cerei, sigillaria), and connections to Christian Christmas traditions. The Faunalia and Bona Dea rites stand apart as non-calendar survivals. The Divalia, Larentalia, and Agonalia/Septimontium are frankly obscure even to Fowler, who is candid about the limits of available evidence. The Compitalia, though variable and possibly falling in early January, is treated here as the culmination of this winter series.
