## Kalendae Iuniae — Juno Moneta / Kalendae Fabariae / Carna

- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: N (nefastus)
- **Deity**: Juno Moneta (Kal. entry); Carna (primary for the day's ancient cult); also Mars (dies natalis of temple of Mars extra portam Capenam)
- **Ritual**: The day was the *dies natalis* of the temple of Juno Moneta on the arx, vowed by L. Furius Camillus Dictator in a war against the Aurunci and dedicated in 344 BC. Fowler notes all Kalends were sacred to Juno, so the day is not especially significant for her cult. The popular name *Kalendae Fabariae* ("because in this month ripe beans are employed in divine rites") reflects the ancient cult of Carna: worshippers ate bean-meal (*puls fabacia*) and lard, believed to protect digestion for the year. Macrobius records that prayers were offered to Carna for the preservation of liver, heart, and internal organs. Carna's ritual also included charm-recitations to protect infants from *striges* (vampire-owls): touching door-posts with arbutus branches, sprinkling with water, sacrificing a sucking-pig, placing a white-thorn wand at the window (Ovid, *Fasti* 6.155 ff.). Fowler strongly separates Carna from Cardea (goddess of door-hinges); he regards Ovid as deliberately conflating them. The dies natalis of the temple of Mars extra portam Capenam (on the Via Appia) also fell on this day, though Fowler notes the Venusian calendar does not mention it and suspects a re-dedication by Augustus.
- **Aition**: For Juno Moneta: temple vowed in Auruncian war (344 BC). The name *Moneta* is explained by the popular tradition that Juno's sacred geese warned Rome of the Gallic attack (390 BC); Fowler accepts this as possibly having a factual basis. Alternative aition (Macrobius, Sat. 1.12.22/31): M. Junius Brutus performed a *sacrum* on this day on the Caelian Hill after Tarquinius's flight — Fowler dismisses this as absurd.
- **Page**: pp. 129–134
- **Notes**: Fowler treats Carna as a genuine, extremely ancient Italian deity of the agricultural age, distinct from Cardea, whose cult was nearly extinct by the late Republic. He regards the Kalendae Fabariae as a survival of primitive bean-harvest customs, not a festival of the dead (contra Wissowa, who inferred a Parentalia-type nature from an inscription mentioning "rosas Carnar[iis]"). The Venusian calendar lists *Iunoni Monetae* and the Philocalus calendar has *Fabae ici circenses illussus*.

## Bellona in Circo Flaminio

- **Day**: 3 (III Non. Iun.)
- **Fasti status**: C (comitialis)
- **Deity**: Bellona
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of the temple of Bellona in the Circus Flaminius, vowed by the consul Ap. Claudius in an Etruscan war (296 BC); dedication date unknown. In front of the temple was the *columella* from which the *fetiales* threw the challenging spear to declare war against a foreign enemy (substituting this for enemy territory, since Pyrrhus held no land in Italy). The temple served as a Senate meeting-place outside the *pomerium* for business with generals and ambassadors.
- **Aition**: Not stated for the ritual itself. Temple vowed by Ap. Claudius Cos. in Etruscan war.
- **Page**: p. 135
- **Notes**: Fowler declines to identify Bellona with Nerio (contra Gellius and the Roscher Lexicon article). The *columella* / spear-throwing ritual is attested by Ovid (*Fasti* 6.205 ff.) and Paulus (p. 33).

## Hercules Magnus Custos

- **Day**: 4 (Prid. Non. Iun.)
- **Fasti status**: C (comitialis)
- **Deity**: Hercules Magnus Custos
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of the temple near the Circus Flaminius, founded by Sulla in 82 BC. The cult was Greek in character (ἡρακλῆς ἀλεξίκακος).
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Page**: p. 135
- **Notes**: Fowler notes this briefly; the Venusian and Rustic calendars have entries for this date.

## Dius Fidius (Semo Sancus)

- **Day**: 5 (Non. Iun. = Nones of June)
- **Fasti status**: N (nefastus)
- **Deity**: Dius Fidius / Semo Sancus (identified with each other and with Hercules by Roman scholars)
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of the temple on the Quirinal, said by Dionysius to have been vowed by Tarquinius Superbus and dedicated by Sp. Postumius in 466 BC. An earlier *sacellum* "ad portam Sanqualem" predated the formal temple. No specific public ritual is described; the deity was mainly known through the oath *me dius fidius* (pronounced outdoors, under open sky). His temple preserved the ancient treaty of Tarquinius with Gabii (on a wooden shield covered with ox-hide), and a statue identified as Gaia Caecilia / Tanaquil.
- **Aition**: Not stated for a specific ritual. Temple vow attributed to Tarquinius Superbus; Sp. Postumius dedicated 466 BC (Dionysius 9.60).
- **Page**: pp. 135–144
- **Notes**: Fowler devotes extensive discussion (pp. 135–144) to the theology: Dius Fidius = Semo Sancus = the religious concept of oath-binding fidelity; connected to Jupiter as sky-god and to lightning (via the *sacerdotes bidentales*, the open roof of the temple, the *sanqualis avis* bird, and the Iguvine Tables where *Fisus Sancius* appears alongside Iovius). Fowler argues Sancus was not distinctively Sabine in origin. Reifferscheid's theory of Dius Fidius = Genius Iovis is discussed cautiously; Fowler regards it as going too far.

## Mens in Capitolio

- **Day**: 8 (VI Id. Iun.)
- **Fasti status**: N (nefastus); Venusian, Maffeian, and (as VI minores) other calendars note it
- **Deity**: Mens
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of the temple of Mens on the Capitol, vowed by T. Otacilius (praetor) in 217 BC after the battle of Trasimenus, on account of neglect of *caerimoniae* and *auspicia*. Dedicated in 215 BC by the same man as *duumvir aedibus dedicandis*. The vow resulted from consulting the Sibylline books.
- **Aition**: Vowed after Trasimenus "propter neglegentiam caerimoniarum auspiciorumque" (Livy 22.9).
- **Page**: p. 145
- **Notes**: Fowler notes Preller's conjecture that Mens was another form of Venus (linked to Venus Erycina, whose temple was also dedicated the same year); this is treated as possible but unproven. The temple was restored by M. Aemilius Scaurus probably during the Cimbrian war.

## Vestalia (Vesta Aperit — Vesta Cluditur)

- **Day**: 7–15 (Vesta opens June 7; Vestalia main day June 9; Vesta closes June 15)
- **Fasti status**: N (nefastus) and *religiosi* for the whole period June 7 (or 5) to June 15
- **Deity**: Vesta
- **Ritual**: On June 7 (*Vesta aperit*), the *penus Vestae* — the innermost room of the aedes Vestae, closed all other days and accessible only to the *pontifex maximus* — was opened to all matrons. For seven days matrons visited barefoot to pray for household blessings. Vestals offered sacred cakes (*mola salsa*) made from the first ears of corn plucked in early May. Bakers and millers observed a holiday; mills were garlanded with flowers and donkeys decorated with wreaths and cakes. On June 15 (*Vesta cluditur*) the *penus* was swept and the sweepings thrown into the Tiber (Ovid, *Fasti* 6.713) or deposited at a specific spot. The day then became *fastus* once the last act of cleansing was done: *Quando stercus delatum fas* (Varro, *L.L.* 6.32). The Flaminica Dialis could not comb her hair, cut her nails, or have intercourse with her husband during the *nefasti* period; this restriction lifted when the *penus* was closed. Marriages were forbidden throughout.
- **Aition**: Origin traced (following Frazer, *J. Phil.* 1885, and Helbig) to the necessity of keeping one fire always alight in each settlement, tended by the chief's daughters. The *penus* preserves the ancient store-closet of the king's house; the whole Vestalia is interpreted as a pre-harvest purification of store-houses, barns, and domestic apparatus before the corn harvest (mid-Italy: latter half of June and July).
- **Page**: pp. 146–154
- **Notes**: Fowler gives an extensive anthropological interpretation linking the Vestalia to Creek Indian "Busk" (first-fruits festival), emphasizing four common features: general solemnity/fast, purification before first-fruits, renewal of sacred fire (cf. March 1), and reception of first-fruits (cf. May Vestals plucking ears). The Q.St.D.F. annotation on June 15 means *Quando Stercus Delatum [Fas]*. The Venusian calendar has *Vestalia* on June 9; the Philocalus calendar has *Vesta aperit* on June 7 and *Vesta cluditur* on June 15.

## Matralia

- **Day**: 11 (III Id. Iun.)
- **Fasti status**: N (nefastus); Tusc., Ven., Maff. calendars note *Matralia*; Ven. has *Matr[i] Matut[ae]*; Philocalus has *Matralia*
- **Deity**: Mater Matuta (primary); also Fortuna (temple in Foro Boario, same dies natalis per Ovid)
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of the temple in the Forum Boarium (near the Circus Maximus), vowed by L. Furius Camillus in the Veientine War (396 BC). Cult peculiarities: (1) Only the wife of a first marriage could deck the image of the goddess. (2) No female slaves admitted to the temple except one, who was driven out with a box on the ear each year as a memorial. (3) Sacred cakes were cooked in old-fashioned earthenware (*libum tostum*, cooked in pans). (4) Women prayed to the goddess for their nephews and nieces first, and for their own children only secondarily (Plutarch, *Q.R.* 16.1; Ovid, *Fasti* 6.559 ff.). A temple of Fortuna in the Forum Boarium shared the same dedication-day; a draped wooden statue in it was popularly identified with Servius Tullius, though its origin was probably Etruscan.
- **Aition**: Temple vowed by L. Furius Camillus in Veientine War (396 BC). Ovid links Mater Matuta to Ino/Leucothea (aition for the slave-exclusion ritual), but Fowler dismisses this Greek mythological identification as secondary and non-original.
- **Page**: pp. 154–157
- **Notes**: Fowler identifies Mater Matuta as an ancient Italian deity of the dawn and of childbirth, widely attested (Pisaurum inscriptions; Volsci; Campania; Praeneste; Satricum; Pyrgi). He separates her firmly from Leucothea, calling Ovid's identification a scholarly confusion. Best modern explanation (Preller, Wissowa): goddess of dawn light and childbirth, cognate with Juno Lucina. The draped Fortuna statue in the adjacent temple is left unexplained; Fowler notes it may be connected with Pudicitia or an Etruscan tradition.

## Feriae Iovi / Quinquatrus Minusculae

- **Day**: 13 (Id. Iun. = Ides of June)
- **Fasti status**: NP (the calendars note *Iovi* [Ven.] and *Iovi* [Tusc.]; Ovid confirms the Ides as *Feriae Iovi*; Fowler calls the fasti marking "NP" implicitly by analogy with other Ides)
- **Deity**: Jupiter (Ides as *Feriae Iovi*); Minerva (patron of the tibicines); the tibicines (guild of flute-players)
- **Ritual**: All Ides were sacred to Jupiter. On this day the *collegium* of tibicines feasted in the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus (Livy 9.30; Val. Max. 2.5.4). The festivity extended over three days. Tibicines wore masks (*personae*) and long robes (*stolae longae*), possibly the ancient dress of Etruscan pipe-players. They gathered first in Minerva's temple on the Aventine before a procession through the city. The day is called *Quinquatrus minusculae* (or *Idus Iuniae*) by popular usage, not because it truly falls five days after the Ides, but because the tibicines, as followers of Minerva, associated their feast with the greater Quinquatrus of March. Varro: *"Quinquatrus minusculae dictae Iuniae Idus ab similitudine maiorum"* (L.L. 6.17). A temple of Jupiter Invictus was dedicated on this day (possibly one of two temples dedicated on the Capitol in 192 BC, Livy — though Fowler notes an Ides dedication-day may suggest higher antiquity).
- **Aition**: The masks and robes were explained by the story (Livy 9.30; Ovid, *Fasti* 6.651 ff.; Plutarch, *Q.R.* 55) of how the tibicines fled to Tibur after the censor Ap. Claudius (311 BC) deprived them of their feast in the Capitol; they were then tricked, made drunk, and returned to Rome unconscious in a wagon. The compromise that secured their return was commemorated by the annual revelry.
- **Page**: pp. 157–159
- **Notes**: Fowler explains the tibia as probably indigenous to Italy (the word is purely Italian; contra Müller who argued Etruscan origin). The Law of the Twelve Tables limited funeral tibicines to ten players. A coin of the gens Plautia may depict the story.

## Summanus

- **Day**: 20 (XII Kal. [Iun. / Quinct.]; Fowler's heading reads "XII Kal. Iun." but contextually this is June 20)
- **Fasti status**: C (comitialis); Venusian, Esquiline, and Amiternum calendars note it
- **Deity**: Summanus (nocturnal Jupiter / deity of night-lightning)
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of the temple of Summanus at the Circus Maximus, founded ca. 278–275 BC after lightning destroyed a terracotta figure of Jupiter on the Capitol. Ritual cakes called *Summanalia* ("liba farinacea in modum rotae ficta" = flour cakes shaped like wheels) were offered or eaten on this day (Festus, p. 348). The wheel shape connects to sun-wheel symbolism (Gaidoz), though Fowler does not commit to a solar interpretation.
- **Aition**: The temple was founded as a result of the destruction by night lightning of a statue of Jupiter on the Capitol (Cicero, *Div.* 1.10); by implication the nocturnal bolt indicated a deity other than Jupiter himself — i.e., Summanus.
- **Page**: pp. 160–161
- **Notes**: Fowler argues the name Summanus = Submanus, "the god who sends lightning before dawn." By Ovid's time the deity was so obscure that Ovid writes *quisquis is est* ("whoever he is"). Augustine (*de Civ. Dei* 4.23) quotes the tradition that in early Rome Summanus was worshipped more than Jupiter; only when the great Capitoline temple was built did Summanus fall into oblivion. Varro classes him with Veiovis and Sabine deities. Fowler declines to accept Varro's Sabine or Müller's Etruscan derivation.

## Fors Fortuna

- **Day**: 24 (VIII Kal. Quinct.)
- **Fasti status**: C (comitialis); Amiternum calendar: *Forti Fortunae trans Tiberim ad milliarium primum et sextum*; Venusian and Philocalus: *Fortis Fortunae*; Rustic calendar: *Sacrum Fortis Fortunae*
- **Deity**: Fors Fortuna
- **Ritual**: A joyful popular festival held at temples of Fors Fortuna on the right bank of the Tiber beyond the city. Three temples were involved: (1) The ancient *fanum* of Servius Tullius (Varro, *L.L.* 6.17: "Dies Fortis Fortunae appellatus ab Servio Tullio rege, quod is fanum Fortis Fortunae secundum Tiberim extra urbem Romam dedicavit Iunio mense"); (2) the temple built by consul Sp. Carvilius in 293 BC near the first (Livy 10.46.17); (3) a later temple of Tiberius (17 AD, Ann. 2.41, which Fowler sets aside as post-Ovidian). The plebs went on foot or by boat (*cymba*), drinking wine even on the water; festive and somewhat licentious in character (Ovid, *Fasti* 6.775 ff.). Open to slaves as well as free citizens, because Servius Tullius was traditionally of servile birth and was the especial friend of the plebs. Columella alludes to the festival in the context of the grain harvest (*R.R.* 10.311 ff.).
- **Aition**: Institution attributed to Servius Tullius (Etruscan king / Mastarna), who built the first *fanum* on the Tiber. Fowler suggests the cult may have come from Etruria with Mastarna, equivalent to the Etruscan goddess Nortia of Volsinii (where a nail was driven annually in her temple). The date (near or at the summer solstice) may be significant: Gaidoz argues for a solar origin via the wheel symbol. Fowler treats both the solstice theory and the harvest-season theory as plausible; he declines to identify Fortuna as a dawn, moon, or sun goddess.
- **Page**: pp. 161–172
- **Notes**: Fowler reproduces H. Peter's analysis of the three temples, accepting Peter's suggestion that Carvilius's temple (293 BC) was later conflated with a Servian *fanum* because they shared the same dedication-day and location. He also provides an extensive critical discussion of Max Müller's attempt to derive Fortuna from Sanskrit *harab* ("to glow") and equate her with the dawn-goddess; Fowler rejects this derivation as unproven and prefers understanding Fortuna originally as an oracular deity of childbirth/luck, comparable to the Norse Norns, worshipped especially by women (Fortuna Primigenia at Praeneste; Fortuna Virilis, Muliebris, Mammosa, etc.).

## Summary

Fowler's *Mensis Iunius* chapter (printed pp. 129–173) covers twelve calendar entries. The most extensive treatments are given to:

1. **Kal. Iun. (June 1)** — The ancient goddess **Carna** (*Kalendae Fabariae*: bean-meal and lard ritual; anti-*strix* charm) alongside the Juno Moneta temple dedication. Fowler is careful to separate Carna from Cardea.
2. **Non. Iun. (June 5)** — **Dius Fidius / Semo Sancus**: a long theological excursus on the nature of this oath-deity, his relation to Jupiter, and Reifferscheid's identification with *Genius Iovis*.
3. **Vestalia (June 7–15)** — The most extensive entry, with a full anthropological analysis (using Frazer and the Creek Indian parallel) of the *penus* opening and closing, the *religiosi* days, and the pre-harvest purification.
4. **Matralia (June 11)** — **Mater Matuta** as an ancient Italian dawn/birth goddess, firmly separated from Leucothea.
5. **Feriae Iovi / Quinquatrus Minusculae (June 13)** — The tibicines' feast and the story of their secession to Tibur.
6. **Fors Fortuna (June 24)** — An extensive discussion of the cult's popular character, the three temples, the Servius Tullius tradition, and a critical engagement with Max Müller's solar/dawn theory of Fortuna.

Minor entries: **Bellona** (June 3), **Hercules Magnus Custos** (June 4), **Mens** (June 8), **Summanus** (June 20). Ludi Piscatorii and Jupiter Stator are absent from Fowler's chapter.
