# Mensis Ianuarius — Fowler (1899)
**PDF pages used**: 289–307 (printed 278–296)

## Kalends (Jan. 1) — Vediovis in Insula
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: F (Praen.)
- **Deity**: Vediovis; also Aesculapius (both on Tiber island)
- **Ritual**: Dedication day of the temple of Vediovis on the Tiber island (vowed by praetor L. Furius Purpureo 200 BC, dedicated six years later). Connection with Aesculapius on the same island is unexplained. Fowler also notes the custom of strenae (New Year presents) on Jan. 1 — word possibly derived from Sabine goddess Strenia, whose sacellum was at the head of the Via Sacra; sacred twigs were carried from her grove to the arx at year's beginning.
- **Page**: p. 278–279

## Compitalia (Ludi Compitales)
- **Day**: Variable (conceptivae); fell between Dec. 17 (Saturnalia) and Jan. 5; Philocalus assigns Ludi Compitales to Jan. 3–5
- **Fasti status**: Not stativae until late Empire
- **Deity**: Lares Compitales
- **Ritual**: See December entry. Festival of crossroads shrines; included slaves; magistri vicorum wore toga praetexta. Fowler treats this primarily under January (Jan. 3–5) because that is where Philocalus places the Ludi Compitales, though the rite could fall before January began.
- **Page**: p. 279–281

## Agonalia (January)
- **Day**: 9 (V ID. IAN.)
- **Fasti status**: IP? (Maff. Praen.); Praen. note gives the word Agonium
- **Deity**: Janus (implied; not certain). Rex sacrorum sacrificed a ram in the Regia; Ovid says Janus was "agonali luce piandus."
- **Ritual**: The Rex sacrorum offered a ram in the Regia. The meaning of the word AGON was unknown even to Roman scholars; Festus gives "agonia" as an old word for "hostia"; Varro uses the plural "agonales dies" for all four occurrences (Jan. 9, Mar. 17, May 21, Dec. 11), noting "rex in regia arietem immolat." Whether the victim was specifically for Janus is not certain, though a ram is attested as Janus' victim once in Fratres Arvales ritual. Fowler's long discussion of Janus follows: he concludes Janus originated as the numen of the house-door/entrance, the concrete starting-point of all undertakings, served by the Rex sacrorum as representative of the ancient paterfamilias.
- **Page**: p. 280–289

## Carmentalia (First Day)
- **Day**: 11 (III ID. IAN.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Praen. Maff.)
- **Deity**: Carmenta (also Juturna worshipped on this day per Ovid, Fasti 1.463, but the Juturnalia is a dedication festival only)
- **Ritual**: Festival of Carmenta at her temple near the Porta Carmentalis at the foot of the southern Capitol. Women were the chief or sole worshippers; they may have built a temple themselves. The rite is called "sacrum pontificale" (Ovid, Fasti 1.462), so pontifices also participated alongside the Flamen Carmentalis. All leather ("scortea") was banned from her shrine, since she is a birth-goddess and leather derives from dead animals. Her dual nature as goddess of prophecy (carmen = spell, prophecy) and childbirth is discussed at length. Ovid says Juturna was also worshipped on Jan. 11, but Fowler regards this as merely the dedication day of Augustus' temple, not a genuine ancient rite.
- **Aition**: Fowler notes the Praen. calendar says Carmenta "partus curat omniaque futura" — she oversees birth and foretells the future; the leather taboo is "ne violent puros exanimata focos." The two names Porrima and Postverta indicate the two positions in which a child may be born.
- **Page**: p. 290–293

## Carmentalia (Second Day)
- **Day**: 15 (XVIII K.FEB.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Praen. Maff. Phil. Caer.)
- **Deity**: Carmenta
- **Ritual**: Same goddess; same temple. The Romans did not consider the two Carmentalia a single festival in two parts, but two different festivals. The second day was said by some to have been added by a general who left Rome by the Porta Carmentalis; by others, as compensation to matrons deprived of the right to ride in carpenta (restored when the decree was withdrawn). Fowler finds no satisfactory explanation for the double festival; the suggestion that the two days represent the Roman and Sabine cities (as with two bodies of Salii and Luperci) is mentioned as unconfirmed.
- **Page**: p. 290–293

## Sementivae / Paganalia
- **Day**: Variable (conceptivae); Ovid dates ~Jan. 24–26; not fixed because the festival's timing was tied to agricultural conditions
- **Fasti status**: Not in calendars (feriae conceptivae)
- **Deity**: Ceres and Tellus ("consortes operis": Ceres provides the power of grain, Tellus provides the place/earth)
- **Ritual**: Offerings of cake (liba) and sacrifice of a pregnant sow; oxen that worked the ploughs were garlanded; prayers for protection of the seed from bird, beast, and disease. In the city the festival was called Sementivae or Sementinae (announced by the pontifices, Varro L.L. 6.26); in the country Paganalia (celebrated by the rural pagus). Oscilla were hung from trees as charms. The timing placed it after the autumn sowing had ended (Varro: equinox to winter solstice) and just before the spring work began around Feb. 7 (Favonius). Lydus mentions two days separated by seven (one for Tellus, one for Ceres, each on a market-day/nundinae). Fowler identifies this as the last of the mid-winter agricultural festivals.
- **Page**: p. 294–296

## Summary
January has only three fasti-attested festivals in large letters (Agonalia, and two Carmentalia), plus the major variable feriae (Compitalia and Paganalia). The Agonalia entry prompts Fowler's longest digression of any January section — a full treatise on Janus, concluding that he originated as the numen of the house-door and became the god of all beginnings by logical extension from that root. The Carmentalia receives careful discussion of the double-day problem and the goddess's dual role as prophetess and birth-deity. The Paganalia/Sementivae section connects agricultural timing with ritual: it closes the mid-winter feast series inaugurated by the Saturnalia, just before the spring sowing begins.
