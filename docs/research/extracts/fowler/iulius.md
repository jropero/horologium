# Iulius — Fowler (1899)
**PDF pages used**: 184–201 (printed pp. 173–190)
**Source**: W. Warde Fowler, *The Roman Festivals of the Period of the Republic* (1899)

---

## Poplifugia
- **Day**: 5 (Non. Quinct.)
- **Fasti status**: NP — marked in "large capitals" in Maff., Amit., Ant. calendars; also "FERIAE IOVI" in Amiternum
- **Deity**: Jupiter (Iovi)
- **Ritual**: The cult details had vanished even by Varro's time. Varro (*L.L.* 6.18) records only: *"Aliquot huius diei vestigia fugae in sacris apparent, de quibus rebus antiquitatum libri plura referunt"* — "traces of flight appear in the sacred rites of this day." In 42 BCE the Senate decreed Caesar's birthday be celebrated on this day, with those who refused to participate declared *sacer Iovi et Divo Iulio* (Dio 47.18). Fowler tentatively suggests it may have been an ancient lustration rite, in which priest and people fled after a sacrifice — analogous to the Athenian Bouphonia, which fell at nearly the same time of year. A scapegoat function (fleeing the victim to carry away pestilence) is also proposed, given that July was Rome's most dangerous season for disease.
- **Aition**: Two legendary explanations recorded but both treated as late rationalisations: (1) flight of Romans before the Fidenates after the Gallic retreat; (2) flight of the people after the disappearance of Romulus in an eclipse/tempest (Dionys. 2.56; Plut. *Rom.* 29). Fowler dismisses the first as incompatible with the festival's evident great antiquity (NP status predates the Gallic invasion). The second is noted only as a possible growth from ritual.
- **Page**: pp. 174–176
- **Notes**: The only festival in the entire year marked NP that falls before the Nones (Fowler, Introduction p. 7). Fowler stresses the meaning was "wholly vanished" when Romans began studying their own calendar. Connection to Jupiter is confirmed by the Amiternum note but not explained. Schwegler's confusion of this day with the Nonae Caprotinae is criticised; Varro (*L.L.* 6.18) distinguishes the two clearly.

## Nonae Caprotinae (Ancillarum Feriae)
- **Day**: 7 (Non. Quinct. = Nones of July)
- **Fasti status**: N — not in the old Republican fasti as an NP feast; recorded in the late calendar of Silvius as "Ancillarum Feriae" (Feast of Handmaids); Fowler notes this day does not appear as a festival in the old calendars
- **Deity**: Juno Caprotina; Consus (secondary, per Tertullian)
- **Ritual**: Female slaves (*ancillae*), dressed in their mistresses' robes, sacrificed to Juno Caprotina under a wild fig-tree (*caprificus*) in the Campus Martius. Free women also participated — Fowler reads this joint participation as proof that "Nonae Caprotinae" and "Ancillarum Feriae" were two names for the same observance (Macrobius 1.11.36). The fig's milky juice was used in the sacrifice instead of milk. Varro (*L.L.* 6.18) adds that a rod (*virga*) was also cut from the wild fig-tree, possibly used for the ritual beating described by Plutarch. The handmaids also had licence for "a game of romps" — beating each other, throwing stones, and mocking passers-by (Plut. *Camil.* 33). Tertullian (*de Spect.* 5) records a sacrifice to Consus at his underground altar in the Circus Maximus on this same day.
- **Aition**: Explicitly given (late, aetiological): the victorious Fidenates demanded Roman wives; a slave-girl named Philotis (or Tutula) advised substituting handmaids dressed in matrons' clothes; this ruse led to Roman victory. The story is in Macrobius 1.11.36 and Plutarch *Camil.* 33. Fowler treats it as a legend grown from harvest customs, not as historical record.
- **Page**: pp. 176–179
- **Notes**: Fowler connects the mockery of strangers with harvest customs documented by Mannhardt and Frazer — strangers intruding on harvest fields were ritually roughed up, tied with straw, etc. The wearing of matrons' dress is compared to the seasonal exchange of roles at the Saturnalia. The *caprificus* connection may link to the agricultural practice of *caprificatio* (introducing wild-fig branches among cultivated figs to aid ripening), which also occurred in July. Juno Caprotina was especially venerated at Falerii. The Consus connection links this day thematically to the Consualia (Aug. 21) and the rape of the Sabines legend.

## Vitulatio
- **Day**: 8 (VIII Id. Quinct.)
- **Fasti status**: N (so headed in Fowler's entry)
- **Deity**: Not explicitly named; Fowler notes the *vitulus* (heifer) was a special victim of Jupiter, and the day before (July 5) was "Feriae Iovi"
- **Ritual**: Macrobius (3.9.11 and 14) quotes Piso: *"Piso ait vitulam victoriam nominari, cuius rei hoc argumentum profert, quod postridie nonas Iulias re bene gesta, cum pridie populus a Tuscis in fugam versus sit (unde Populifugia vocantur), post victoriam certis sacrificiis fiat vitulatio."* Fowler notes that *vitulatio* was a word whose meaning Roman scholars had entirely lost, and that it should plausibly have been connected with *vitulus* (a heifer).
- **Aition**: None explicitly given; only the post-hoc legend linking it to a victory over the "Tusci" (a variant for Fidenates in Macrobius) the day after the Poplifugia flight.
- **Page**: p. 179
- **Notes**: This is not a named festival in the fasti; it is treated by Fowler as an observation about a rite (vitulatio) attested on this day. The heifer at Iguvium served as a scapegoat in the lustratio populi, which Fowler finds suggestive for the Poplifugia connection.

## Ludi Apollinares
- **Day**: 6–13 (Prid. Non. Quinct. – III Id. Quinct.)
- **Fasti status**: All days marked "ludi" in Maff., Amit., Ant. calendars; July 6 specifically marked "ludi Apoll[ini]"; July 13 "ludi in circo"
- **Deity**: Apollo (Apollo Medicus)
- **Ritual**: Games instituted in 212 BCE at the most dangerous moment of the Second Punic War, after Hannibal took Tarentum and invaded Campania. Recourse was had to the Sibylline books and to the oracles of the prophet Marcius, which commanded annual games to Apollo. Originally celebrated on July 13 alone, extended backwards over time until in the Julian calendar the ludi ran July 6–13. Made permanent by lex in 208 BCE, again due to pestilence (Liv. 27.23). Supervised by the *Decemviri sacris faciundis*; included scenic performances (Greek fashion) and chariot races. Apollo's oldest temple in Rome was vowed in 432 BCE during a pestilence; he bore the cult-title *Medicus*.
- **Aition**: Explicitly given: the oracle of Marcius commanded the games (*"Apollini vovendos censeo ludos, qui quotannis Apollini fiant"*, Liv. 25.12). The institution was a response to military crisis and summer pestilence, not to any myth. Fowler notes both functions (averting enemies and averting pestilence) were within Apollo's range; the 208 BCE renewal was explicitly connected to pestilence.
- **Page**: pp. 179–182
- **Notes**: Fowler discusses Apollo's cult as essentially a priestly/oligarchic import — "a new physician called in at perilous times." The cult had a Greek character from the first. Ennius produced his *Thyestes* at these ludi (Cic. *Brutus* 78). Caesar's real birthday was July 12, already occupied by the ludi (note 2, p. 180). Apollo's temple was outside the Porta Carmentalis near the Circus Flaminius; a second temple was not built until Augustus after Actium.

## Lucaria (I)
- **Day**: 19 (XIV Kal. Sext.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Maff., Amit.)
- **Deity**: Unnamed deities of woodland (*numina* of the grove); no specific deity recorded
- **Ritual**: Entirely lost — "wholly lost to us, as indeed it was to the Romans of Varro's time." Festus (*s.v. Lucaria*, 119) preserves the only relevant notice: the festival was celebrated in a grove of great size between the Via Salaria and the Tiber (probably on the Pincian hill), with the aetiological explanation that Romans fleeing from the Gauls hid there. Fowler interprets it as a propitiatory rite addressed to the deities inhabiting the woodland border of the cultivated Roman *ager*, at harvest-time when men and women were in the fields.
- **Aition**: Festus gives the legend of Romans hiding in the grove after flight from the Gauls — Fowler dismisses this as analogous to the aetiological stories for the Poplifugia, formed after the ritual's meaning had been forgotten. The battle of the Allia (July 18) preceded the first Lucaria and likely suggested the connection.
- **Page**: pp. 182–185
- **Notes**: Two days of Lucaria (July 19 and 21); Fowler suggests they may relate to the alternating pattern seen in Lemuria (three days), Lucaria, and other such groupings. The grove later became the site of the gardens of Lucullus, Pompeius, and Sallust. Cato (*R.R.* 139) preserves a prayer for propitiating unknown woodland deities before cutting trees, which Fowler uses to contextualise the festival's likely purpose.

---

## Lucaria (II)
- **Day**: 21 (XII Kal. Sext.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Maff., Amit.)
- **Deity**: Same as Lucaria I — unnamed woodland deities
- **Ritual**: Same grove and ritual context as Lucaria I (see above). Two separate days of the same observance; the two-day structure recurs elsewhere in the calendar (Lemuria, etc.).
- **Aition**: None separate from Lucaria I.
- **Page**: p. 182 (heading only; discussion covers both under the same entry)
- **Notes**: Fowler notes Wissowa tentatively connects Lucaria and Neptunalia as jointly averting summer heat and drought, but doubts this can be pressed far.

## Neptunalia
- **Day**: 23 (X Kal. Sext.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Pinc., Allif.); "FERIAE NEPTUNO" in Pure., Maff.
- **Deity**: Neptunus (and female consort Salacia)
- **Ritual**: Festival took place in the heat of summer. Booths or huts (*umbrae*) made of foliage of trees were erected to shade the worshippers (Festus 377: *"Umbrae vocantur Neptunalibus casae frondeae pro tabernaculis"*). Beyond this, the ritual is entirely unknown. Neptunus was paired with Salacia in ancient cult formulae (Gellius 13.23.2); Varro derives *Salacia* from *salum* (sea) but Fowler prefers derivation from *salax* ("lustful"), suggesting Neptunus as divine progenitor of a stock, like Mars. *Neptunus pater* is a cult title.
- **Aition**: None given; no mythological narrative attached.
- **Page**: pp. 185–187
- **Notes**: Fowler is cautious throughout: "we know that it took place in the heat of summer, and that booths of foliage were used — and that is all." No evidence connects Neptunus to the sea in his early Roman cult. His identification with Poseidon appears first at the lectisternium of 399 BCE (Liv. 5.13). The foliage-huts are compared to the *σκιάδες* of the Spartan Carneia (Athenaeus 4.141F). The connection of the Neptunalia's date (July 23) to the Lucaria dates (July 19, 21) is noted by Wissowa but viewed sceptically by Fowler.

## Furrinalia
- **Day**: 25 (VIII Kal. Sext.)
- **Fasti status**: IP (Pinc., Allif., Maff.); "FERIAE FURINAE" in Pinc., Allif.
- **Deity**: Furrina (or Furina) — a single, archaic goddess distinct from the Furiae (Furies), as proven by the long *u* indicated by the double *r* in the calendar spelling
- **Ritual**: Entirely unknown — "we know no single item in the cult." Varro (*L.L.* 5.84; 6.19) states explicitly that in his day barely a dozen Romans knew Furrina's name, yet she had been honoured *apud antiquos* with an annual festival and a flamen (*flamen Furinalis*). The flamen is taken as proof of her ancient importance and individuality as a deity.
- **Aition**: None; even Varro and Verrius Flaccus knew nothing of it.
- **Page**: pp. 187–188
- **Notes**: Fowler reviews the etymology: Preller connects the name with *fur*, *furnus*, *fuscus* (root of darkness/secrecy), suggesting a deity of the underworld or darkness. Bücheler (*Umbrica* p. 71) compares the Umbrian *furfare* = *februare* (to purify). Jordan (*in* Preller ii.121) speculates the festival averred dangerous summer heat. Huschke wrongly identifies Furrina with the Furiae. Fowler concludes: "it is as hopeless as it is useless to attempt to discover the nature of either goddess or festival by means of etymological reasoning." Gaius Gracchus was killed near her grove on the Janiculum (Plut. *G. Gracch.* 17), but this is historical accident, not cult information.

## Summary

Fowler covers seven festival occasions for Mensis Quinctilis (Iulius):

| Day | Festival | Deity | Fasti |
|-----|----------|-------|-------|
| 5 | Poplifugia | Jupiter (Iovi) | NP |
| 7 | Nonae Caprotinae / Ancillarum Feriae | Juno Caprotina; Consus | N |
| 8 | Vitulatio (rite, not named festival) | — | N |
| 6–13 | Ludi Apollinares | Apollo | ludi |
| 19 | Lucaria I | unnamed woodland deities | IP |
| 21 | Lucaria II | unnamed woodland deities | IP |
| 23 | Neptunalia | Neptunus (+ Salacia) | IP |
| 25 | Furrinalia | Furrina | IP |

**General character of the month (Fowler's framing)**: The July festivals are "so exceedingly obscure that it seems hopeless to try to connect them in any definite way with the operations either of nature or of man." The great NP festivals (Poplifugia, Lucaria, Neptunalia, Furrinalia) had already lost all meaning by Varro's time — decayed "from pure inanition." The Ludi Apollinares (212 BCE) were the only festival that retained popular interest throughout the Republic. No aitia are explicit for the archaic festivals; the legends attached to Poplifugia and Nonae Caprotinae are treated as late rationalisations.

**Festivals NOT covered by Fowler in these pages**:
- Victoria / Temple of Victoria dedication (Jul. 17?) — not mentioned
- Ludi Victoriae Caesaris (late July) — not mentioned (post-Republican)
- Any reference to Quirinalia or July Kalends rites

**Vitulatio caveat**: Fowler treats this as a rite documented on July 8, not as an independent named festival. It is included here for completeness but should not be entered in the app as a standalone festival without further attestation.
