# Augustus — Fowler (1899)
**Source**: W. Warde Fowler, *The Roman Festivals of the Period of the Republic* (London, 1899)
**PDF pages used**: 200–227 (printed pp. 189–216)
**Chapter heading**: MENSIS SEXTILIS (= Augustus)

Fowler opens with a framing observation: August in Italy is when the final harvest-operations (housing, storage) are completed; the cluster of late-August festivals reflects this, mostly gathering around the Aventine, Circus Maximus, and the Tiber bank — the area closest to the earliest Roman ager. Deities of fire and water are propitiated to protect the stored harvest. The rites are notably secret/chthonic (underground altar, Regia sacrarium) rather than public temple-centred.

---

## Nones Sacrifice to Salus (Aug. 5)
- **Day**: 5 (Nonae Sextiles)
- **Fasti status**: F (calendar MAFF./AMIT. mark NP; ANT. has F; contested)
- **Deity**: Salus
- **Ritual**: Public sacrifice on the Quirinal Hill (SALUTI IN COLLE QUIRINALE SACRIFICIUM PUBLICUM). The Philocalus calendar records this as NATALIS SALUTIS (birthday of Salus's temple).
- **Aition**: Temple founded 302 BC during the Samnite wars (Livy 10.1.9). The cult of Salus was older than the temple; the Augurium Salutis was an ancient performance at the new year or consular accession, ascertaining whether prayers would be acceptable and offering them on an auspicious day.
- **Page**: pp. 190–191
- **Notes**: Fowler notes Salus as an abstract impersonation of pontifical origin (later monarchy or early Republic), not a Greek import, though later assimilated to Greek Hygieia. By 180 BC she was associated with Apollo and Aesculapius in a supplicatio for pestilence (Livy 40.19).

## Sol Indiges on the Quirinal (Aug. 9)
- **Day**: 9 (VI Idus Sextiles)
- **Fasti status**: F (ALLIF.); also IP (AMIT. MAFF. ETC.)
- **Deity**: Sol Indiges
- **Ritual**: Public sacrifice to Sol Indiges on the Quirinal (SOLI INDIGITI IN COLLE QUIRINALE SACRIFICIUM PUBLICUM). There was a Solis pulvinar near the temple of Quirinus. The Gens Aurelia was said to have charge of the cult.
- **Aition**: Not explicitly given. Fowler reviews disputed etymologies of "Indiges" (= deity of a place, from indu + agere; or index, with Preller's conjecture that the epithet was added after Caesar's death). Wissowa concludes "Indiges" in the Julian calendar simply distinguished the indigenous Sun-god from foreign solar deities.
- **Page**: pp. 191–193
- **Notes**: The cult was believed to be of Sabine origin (Paulus, Quintilian, Varro). Fowler treats the etymology as genuinely uncertain; the pre-Julian calendar may have lacked the word "Indiges."

## Hercules Invictus at the Ara Maxima (Aug. 12)
- **Day**: 12 (Pridie Idus Sextiles)
- **Fasti status**: C (ALLIF. AMIT.); a second entry HERCULI MAGNO CUSTODI IN CIRCO FLAMINIO (VALL.) is generally taken as a confusion with June 4.
- **Deity**: Hercules (Invictus)
- **Ritual**: Yearly rites at the ara maxima (at the entrance of the Circus Maximus) and the aedes Herculis in the Forum Boarium. The praetor urbanus presided, wearing a laurel wreath and sacrificing with head uncovered (Greek fashion). The sacrifice was a heifer. A sacred meal followed on the remainder of the sacrifice; worshippers sat rather than reclined. Women were excluded. Decumae (tithes, one-tenth) of military booty, commercial gains, and windfalls were offered.
- **Aition**: The founding legend of the cult is the story of Hercules and Cacus. Two patrician families, the Potitii and Pinarii, originally served as priests; Appius Claudius (censor 312 BC) bribed the Potitii to hand over duties to public slaves (Dionys. 1.40). Fowler is sceptical about the Potitii (no inscriptional evidence; possibly a legendary invention).
- **Page**: pp. 193–197
- **Notes**: Fowler raises whether a form of Mars underlies the Hercules of the ara maxima (parallel with Cato's formula invoking Mars Silvanus, with similar exclusion of women and participation of slaves; Salii present in Virgil's description, Aen. 8.281). The tithe-giving practice (decumae) may be Semitic in origin, paralleled in the cult of Melcarth/Herakles; Fowler suggests the ara maxima may predate Rome's traditional foundation and its cult may have been established by foreign (Phoenician) adventurers. Participatory meal on remainder of sacrifice is paralleled by Italian practice.

## Ides of August — Diana, Vortumnus, and others (Aug. 13)
- **Day**: 13 (Idus Sextiles)
- **Fasti status**: NP (AMIT. VALL. ANT. ALLIF. for Diana; all Ides feriae Iovi)
- **Deity**: Jupiter (Ides); Diana (primary, Aventine); Vortumnus (Aventine); Hercules Invictus (ad portam Trigeminam); Castor and Pollux (in Circo Flaminio); Flora (ad Circum Maximum)
- **Ritual**:
  - *Feriae Iovi*: all Ides sacred to Jupiter.
  - *Diana in Aventino*: Best-known dedication of the day. Temple traditionally ascribed to Servius Tullius, who persuaded Latin chiefs to build it in common (Livy 1.45; Varro calls it "commune Latinorum Dianae templum"). The lex templi (ordinance for joint Roman-Latin worship) was written in Greek characters, seen by Dionysius. A xoanon (wooden statue) resembling Artemis at Massilia (itself derived from Ephesus) was housed in the temple. The day was a holiday for slaves. Roman women made a special point of washing their heads on this day. The horns of a cow hung in front of the temple gave rise to legends involving Sabines.
  - *Vortumnus in Aventino*: Temple dedicated 264 BC by consul M. Fulvius Flaccus. Propertius (4.2) describes Vortumnus as presiding over gardens and fruit; Ovid tells of his love for Pomona (Metam. 14.623). A bronze statue stood in the Vicus Tuscus; Varro thought him an Etruscan import (L.L. 5.46), but his name is Latin and may indicate the "turn" of the year at fruit-season.
  - *Hercules Invictus ad portam Trigeminam*, *Castor and Pollux in Circo Flaminio*, *Flora ad Circum Maximum*: Dedications noted in calendars; Fowler says nothing of special interest is known about these.
- **Aition**: Diana temple foundation legend (Servius Tullius, joint Latin-Roman cult). Slave holiday: Festus preserves a gloss of Verrius Flaccus linking the day to Servius Tullius's servile birth and Diana's protection of deer/fugitives.
- **Page**: pp. 198–202
- **Notes**: Fowler treats Diana as originally a spirit of holy trees/woods (like Silvanus), reclaimed into the Italian farmer's world. The Greek transformation (assimilation to Artemis) began very early; by 398 BC she was already paired with Apollo and Latona in the first lectisternium (Livy 5.13). The Aventine temple marked Rome's expansion from city-state to wider Italian influence: advance of Plebs, emancipation of slaves, Latin relations, Greek religious ideas.

## Portunalia (Aug. 17)
- **Day**: 17 (XVI Kal. Septembris)
- **Fasti status**: NP (MAFF. AMIT. VALL.); called Tiberinalia in the Philocalus calendar
- **Deity**: Portunus; also Janus (temple dedication: Iano ad theatrum Marcelli, VALL. ALLIF.)
- **Ritual**: Feriae Portuno; sacrifice at Pons Aemilius (PORTUNO AD PONTEM AEMILIUM). Varro (L.L. 6.19): "Portunalia et Portuno, quoi eo die aedes in portu Tiberino facta et feriae institutae." A mutilated Veronese commentary on Virgil records that on this day "apud veteres claves in focum add…" — Huschke conjectured "addere et infumare" (throwing/hardening keys in the fire), pointing to Portunus as a god of gates and keys securing storehouses; Verrius (Paulus 56) says Portunus "clavim manu tenere et deus esse portarum." Portunus had a special flamen (who could be a plebeian), whose only attested duty was "unguit arma Quirini" using a grease called persillum.
- **Aition**: Not explicitly given. Mommsen identified Portunus with Tiberinus (river-god) based on the Philocalus "Tiberinalia" name. Fowler prefers Huschke's interpretation of Portunus as a god of storehouses, keys, and (originally) any "safe place" (portus), appropriated to harbours only as civilization advanced. Jordan suggested the storehouses near the Tiber were under Portunus's care.
- **Page**: pp. 202–204
- **Notes**: Fowler finds the evidence too scanty for certainty. The Janus temple "ad theatrum Marcelli" was a late foundation (vowed by C. Duilius, First Punic War, 260 BC; rebuilt by Tiberius, dedication-day shifted to Oct. 18). Any close relation between Portunus and Janus (both gods of doorways/keys) remains conjectural.

## Vinalia Rustica (Aug. 19)
- **Day**: 19 (XIV Kal. Septembris)
- **Fasti status**: PP (ANT. ALLIF.); IP (MAFF. AMIT.); also feriae Iovi (ALLIF.)
- **Deity**: Jupiter (primary); Venus (temple dedication on this day: VENERI AD CIRCUM MAXIMUM, VALL.)
- **Ritual**: The Aedes Veneris ad Circum Maximum was dedicated 295 BC (Livy 10.31), built at the expense of matrons fined for adultery. On this day the Flamen Dialis reportedly performed the auspicatio vindemiae — plucking the first grapes and praying for the safety of the crop (Varro L.L. 6.16: "flamen Dialis auspicatur vindemiam, et ut iussit vinum legere, agna Iovi facit, inter quoius exta caesa et porrecta flamen primus vinum legit"). Alternatively (Festus, p.264): this day was a festival because new wine was first brought into the city. Varro (L.L. 6.20): growers kept holiday ("feriati olitores").
- **Aition**: No aition for the festival itself stated. For the Aedes Veneris: matrons' fine for adultery funded the building. Fowler notes the connexion with Venus is secondary; both Vinalia (April 23 and August 19) were dedication-days of Venus temples, but Venus's association with wine/vintage has no early proof.
- **Page**: pp. 204–206
- **Notes**: Fowler discusses the long debate between (a) the auspicatio vindemiae interpretation (Preller, Marquardt) — that this concerned the ripening grape-crop even before the September vintage — and (b) Mommsen's view that it concerned wine already stored (the custom of keeping wine a full year before opening it, roughly Oct. to Aug.), with the country growers now free to bring in their wine ("Rustica" = country people's festival). Fowler declines to decide but notes that August 19 with grapes "fully formed on the vines" makes anxiety-driven propitiation plausible. The Varro passage (L.L. 6.16) clearly refers to April 23 in context, complicating the attribution.

## Consualia (Aug. 21)
- **Day**: 21 (XII Kal. Septembris)
- **Fasti status**: IP (PINC. MAFF. VALL. ETC.)
- **Deity**: Consus
- **Ritual**: Sacrifice at Consus's underground altar in the Circus Maximus ("ad primas metas"), uncovered only on festival days (Dionysius calls it a τέμενος; Servius a "templum sub tecto"; Tertullian explicitly says "sub terra"). Tertullian (de Spect. 8): the sacrifice was performed by the Flamen Quirinalis in the presence of the Vestal Virgins. Horses and asses had a holiday and were decked with flowers (Plutarch, Qu. Rom. 40). Horse-races and mule-races were held in the Circus Maximus (Dionysius 2.31; Festus p.148 on mules as most ancient beasts of burden). A second Consualia fell on December 15.
- **Aition**: Romulus was said to have held the first Consualia and used it to attract and then abduct the Sabine women (Varro L.L. 6.20; Servius Aen. 8.636; Dionysius 2.31; Cicero Rep. 2.12). Fowler treats this as a reflection of the sexual license common at harvest-home gatherings (citing Frazer, Golden Bough). The false etymological inscription supposedly engraved on the altar ("Consus consilio, Mars duello, Lares coillo potentes") is post-antique according to Wissowa.
- **Page**: pp. 206–209
- **Notes**: Fowler derives Consus from "condere" — god of the stored-up harvest; the underground altar is a reminiscence of storing grain underground (Mommsen's suggestion, accepted by Wissowa). Alternatively, sacrifices to chthonic deities were sometimes buried, connecting underground altars with wealth (agricultural produce as earliest form of wealth). Consus had no flamen of his own despite antiquity, because his altar was subterranean and opened only once or twice a year. The Flamen Quirinalis (who sacrificed at this festival) also sacrificed a dog to Robigus on April 25 for the corn-crops — Fowler sees this as confirming the agricultural character of the Consualia. The Aventine temple of Consus was a comparatively late foundation (272 BC, Festus 209).

## Volcanalia (Aug. 23)
- **Day**: 23 (X Kal. Septembris)
- **Fasti status**: IP (PINC. MAFF. VALL. ETC.)
- **Deity**: Volcanus (Vulcan); also Ops Opifera and Nymphs (from a mutilated Arval Brothers fragment: "quib[ino] in colle, volk[ano] in comit[io] opi opifer[ae] in …, [nymp]his (?) in campo")
- **Ritual**: Heads of Roman families threw small scaled fish (from Tiber fishermen at the "area Volcani") into the fire. A Flamen Volcanalis officiated. After Nero's great fire, Domitian erected a new altar at which (and at all Volcanalia) on this day a red calf and a boar were offered (C.I.L. vi.826). Wissowa: the Nymphs' appearance indicates water used against conflagrations; Ops Opifera perhaps invoked to protect storehouses from fire.
- **Aition**: No founding aition stated. Fowler notes that Preller conjectured Volcanus was originally a nature-spirit of earth's warmth and fertilising power; later (under unknown influences) he became a hostile fire-god. The fish-sacrifice is unexplained (Festus p.310; Varro L.L. 6.20 uses the word "animalia" and does not specify fish). Fowler compares it to the fish offered to Tacita at the Parentalia for the ghost-world, seeing the fire-god as chthonic in a volcanic land.
- **Page**: pp. 209–211
- **Notes**: The aedes Volcani in circo Flaminio was dedicated before 215 BC (Livy 34.10.9); Vitruvius (1.7.1) explains its position outside the pomerium as keeping conflagrations from the city. But the principal area Volcani (Volcanal) was in the Comitium, inside the city. Fowler rejects Jevons's argument (from the extra-pomerial position) that the cult was foreign. At Ostia the cult was elaborate (aedes, pontifex Volcani, praetor sacris Volcani faciundis) because in August the storehouses were full of grain from Sicily, Africa, and Egypt.

## Mundus Patet (Aug. 24)
- **Day**: 24 (IX Kal. Septembris)
- **Fasti status**: Not in the main calendars; known from Festus (p.154, from Ateius Capito) and Macrobius (1.16.17)
- **Deity**: Dis Pater / Manes (underworld deities)
- **Ritual**: The mundus — a round pit on the Palatine, centre of Roma Quadrata — was opened on three days: Aug. 24, Oct. 5, and Nov. 8. It was normally closed by a lapis manalis. When the stone was removed, the underworld was thought to have free egress: Varro (ap. Macrob. 1.16.18) "Mundus cum patet, deorum tristium atque inferum ianua patet." These were dies religiosi (no battle, no levy, no marriage, no voyage on these days; Cato, ap. Festus 154).
- **Aition**: Not a standard aition. Plutarch (Rom. 11) and Ovid (Fasti 4.821) describe Romulus casting first-fruits of all necessities into a founding trench (the mundus). Fowler follows O. Müller: the mundus was originally the penus of the new city — a sacred grain-store — and was open on Aug. 24 because the harvest was being stored. The Graeco-Etruscan doctrine of the underworld was a later accretion on this simple original fact.
- **Page**: pp. 211–212
- **Notes**: The mundus is not a formal festival with a named deity in the old fasti; it is a dies religiosus affecting daily life. Fowler is tentative: "I am much inclined to see in this last idea a later Graeco-Etruscan accretion." The identification with "ianua Orci / faux Plutonis" belongs to later interpretation. The Müller interpretation (grain-store opened at harvest) fits the August context but is conjectural.

## Opiconsivia (Aug. 25)
- **Day**: 25 (VIII Kal. Septembris)
- **Fasti status**: IP (ALLIF. MAFF. VALL.); "OPICOID." (PINC.) is a cutter's error
- **Deity**: Ops Consiva (= Ops with the cognomen/epithet Consiva; attested by Varro L.L. 6.21 and Festus 187)
- **Ritual**: Varro (L.L. 6.21): "Opeconsiva dies ab dea Ope Consiva, quoius in Regia sacrarium, quod ideo actum ut eo praeter Virgines Vestales et sacerdotem publicum introeat nemo." The ritual took place in the sacrarium of Ops in the Regia (the ancient king's house); only the Vestal Virgins and a publicus sacerdos (the rex sacrorum or a pontifex) could enter. The calendar entry "OPI CONSIV. IN REGIA" (from Feriale Cumanum, AEV.) likely refers to this day (Mommsen notes the last four words may belong to Aug. 26).
- **Aition**: No explicit aition. Fowler interprets the Regia sacrarium as the sacred penus of the household — the treasury of earth's fruits on which the family subsisted — with the Vestals representing the daughters of the house in perpetual guardianship. The interval of three days between Consualia (Aug. 21) and Opiconsivia (Aug. 25) is the same as between the December Consualia (Dec. 15) and Opalia (Dec. 19), which led Wissowa to suggest Ops was Consus's wife; Fowler disputes this (Ops and Consus are obviously closely related but the marital tie is not required; he cites Lua Saturni, Moles Martis, Maia Volcani as analogous adjectival/genitive forms indicating functional affinity, not marriage).
- **Page**: pp. 212–214
- **Notes**: Wissowa treats Ops as a deity of "messis condendae" (harvest storage) and Consus as representing "opima frugum copiaque quae horreis conditur." Fowler accepts the close relation but not the conjugal interpretation. He cites Columella (12.4) on ritual purity required of those who handled the household stores, as a parallel for the exclusion of the sexually active from the Regia sacrarium. The second Opalia fell on Dec. 19.

## Volturnalia (Aug. 27)
- **Day**: 27 (VI Kal. Septembris)
- **Fasti status**: IP (ALLIF. MAFF. VALL.); noted in the Arval Brothers as "inter addita posteriora" (a later addition)
- **Deity**: Volturnus (river god)
- **Ritual**: Feriae Volturno; sacrifice to the river Volturnus (VOLTURNO FLUMINI SACRIFICIUM, VALL.). A Flamen Volturnalis is attested by Varro, but nothing is recorded of his duties. The later Latin scholars seem to have known nothing about the rite.
- **Aition**: None stated. Fowler interprets Volturnus as an old name for the Tiber (= the winding, snake-like river), distinct from Portunus (which he takes as a god of storehouses, not the river). The name Volturnus also appears for a river in Campania (Lucretius 5.745 pairs Volturnus with Auster as a wind, which Fowler notes but rejects as the primary meaning here).
- **Page**: pp. 214–215
- **Notes**: Mommsen's view that Portunus = Tiberinus creates a problem: the same river-god could hardly have had two flamines. Fowler sides with Mommsen's critics (Jordan on Preller ii.133; Marquardt 338). He sees Volturnus as the oldest attested name for the Tiber; Servius (on Aen. 8.330) says "Tiberinus a pontificibus indigitari solet," implying the name "Tiberinus" is pontifical coinage. Jordan thought Volturnus might be a whirlwind-god (Preller ii.142, Lucretius), which Fowler notes but does not adopt.

## Summary

Fowler covers eleven named entries for August, which he frames as a cluster of harvest-completion and storage-protection festivals:

| Day | Festival | Deity | Fasti status |
|-----|----------|-------|--------------|
| 5 | Sacrifice to Salus (Nones) | Salus | F (NP in some) |
| 9 | Sol Indiges on Quirinal | Sol Indiges | F / IP |
| 12 | Hercules Invictus at Ara Maxima | Hercules | C |
| 13 | Ides: Diana, Vortumnus, etc. | Diana (primary); Vortumnus; Jupiter; Hercules; Castor & Pollux; Flora | NP |
| 17 | Portunalia | Portunus (+Janus dedication) | NP |
| 19 | Vinalia Rustica | Jupiter (primary); Venus (temple) | PP / IP |
| 21 | Consualia | Consus | IP |
| 23 | Volcanalia | Volcanus (+Ops Opifera, Nymphs) | IP |
| 24 | Mundus Patet | Manes / Dis Pater | dies religiosus (not in fasti) |
| 25 | Opiconsivia | Ops Consiva | IP |
| 27 | Volturnalia | Volturnus | IP |

**Fowler's key interpretive themes for August:**
1. The late-August run (Consualia → Volcanalia → Opiconsivia → Volturnalia, each separated by two days) forms a coherent harvest-storage complex protecting corn from fire, water, and underworld dangers.
2. Rites cluster geographically around the Aventine/Circus Maximus/Tiber bank — the area of earliest Roman cultivation and storage.
3. Several rites are deliberately secret or chthonic (underground altar of Consus, Regia sacrarium of Ops, Mundus pit on Palatine).
4. Fowler is cautious about aitia: the Hercules/Cacus legend, the Romulus/Sabine rape legend at Consualia, and the Diana-as-Servius-Tullius foundation are all treated with historical scepticism.
5. Portunus, Volturnus, and Sol Indiges are marked as genuinely obscure; Fowler explicitly notes that the evidence is "too few and too doubtful" for Portunus.
