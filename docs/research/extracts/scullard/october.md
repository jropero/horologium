## Fidei in Capitolio
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Fides (Bona Fides / Fides Publica)
- **Ritual**: Annual worship of Fides established by Numa. Flamines traveled to the shrine in a two-horse hooded carriage; before sacrificing they wrapped their hands to the fingers as a sign that faith must be kept. The covering also protected the shrine from human pollution. The temple (Fides Publica) was built by A. Atilius Calatinus (consul 258 and 254 BC) and dedicated on 1 October on the Capitol, probably within the area Capitolina. It occasionally hosted Senate meetings; international treaties were affixed to its walls.
- **Aition**: Attributed to King Numa by Livy, though Scullard considers the cult of a personified abstract probably post-Numa; Fides was originally conceived as the gods' reliability toward men (divine side of the pax deorum relationship). The temple dedication on 1 October is the likely origin of the calendar entry.
- **Participants**: Flamines (here used loosely as sacerdotes); Senate used the temple for meetings.
- **Page**: p. 189–190

## Tigillo Sororio ad Compitum Acili
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: not stated (appears in Arval record for 1 October)
- **Deity**: Juno Sororia; Janus Curiatius
- **Ritual**: Annual sacrifice at the tigillum (a beam spanning a street near the Compitum Acili on the south-west slope of the Oppian hill) and at the two altars of Juno Sororia and Janus Curiatius erected beside it. Participants passed under the beam in a rite of purification. The beam was kept in repair and still stood in the fourth century AD.
- **Aition**: Scullard identifies the attached legend — Horatius killing his sister and his father erecting the beam as expiation — as a later aetiological overlay. The true function was passage rites: Juno Sororia presided over female puberty (sororiare), Janus Curiatius over the passage to manhood of the boys of the curiae. The rites were thus primitive "rites of passage," with the legend of Horatius/Horatii and Curiatii grafted on because of the name Curiatius.
- **Participants**: Pontiffs (who ordered the altars per Dionysius); those undergoing passage rite.
- **Page**: p. 190

## Ieiunium Cereris (Fast of Ceres)
- **Day**: 4
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Ceres
- **Ritual**: A fast (ieiunium) in honour of Ceres, held every five years from its institution in 191 BC (decemviri ordered to consult the Sibylline Books); by Augustus' day it was annual on 4 October.
- **Aition**: Scullard notes likely Greek influence: the second day of the Athenian Thesmophoria (a festival of Demeter/Ceres in October) included a fast (nesteia). The Roman fast follows this model.
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 190–191

## Iovi Fulguri, Iunoni Quiriti (Iunoni Curriti in Campo)
- **Day**: 7
- **Fasti status**: F
- **Deity**: Jupiter Fulgur; Juno Curitis
- **Ritual**: Sacrifice at a shrine in the Campus Martius, open to the sky. Jupiter Fulgur presided over daytime thunderbolts (Summanus over nocturnal ones). Juno Curitis had her principal cult at Falerii; her introduction to Rome may have come via evocatio when the Romans captured Falerii in 241 BC, or her origin may be older if the epithet connects with the curiae. Her campus Martius temple has been tentatively identified with Temple A in the Largo Argentina.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 191

- **Fasti status**: C

Pompey had built a temple to Venus Victrix (see 12 August).

## Meditrinalia
- **Day**: 11
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Jupiter (presiding deity per the calendars; a goddess "Meditrina" is a late invention from the festival name)
- **Ritual**: A flamen Martialis performed a libation of new and old wine and tasted it with a ritual formula: "wine new and old I drink, of illness new and old I am cured" (vinum novum vetus bibo, novo veteri morbo medeor). Festus records a similar formula at the first tasting of must. The emphasis was on formal, ceremonial tasting (possibly libo, "to libate," rather than bibo, "to drink") at a time too early for actual drinking; real tasting and drinking occurred at the Vinalia of 23 April. An important ceremony in early agricultural Rome.
- **Aition**: Varro derives the name from mederi ("to be healed"). Scullard also notes the possibility that "Meditrinalia" derives from a non-Indo-European word for the place of the ceremony (the wine-press). The curative formula is the aition as transmitted.
- **Participants**: Flamen Martialis (officiating); country people still performed the toast in Varro's day.
- **Page**: p. 192

## Fontinalia
- **Day**: 13
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Fons (god of Springs)
- **Ritual**: Garlands were thrown into springs and placed around the tops of wells in honour of Fons. A shrine (delubrum) was dedicated to Fons outside the Porta Fontinalis by C. Papirius Maso (consul 231 BC), whose army in Corsica had been saved by the discovery of a water-supply; its site is not known.
- **Aition**: Scullard gives the dedication by Maso as the origin of the shrine; the votive motive (army saved by water in Corsica) is the explicit aition for the temple.
- **Participants**: not stated (general population implied by the custom of throwing garlands)
- **Page**: p. 192–193

## Equus October (October Horse)
- **Day**: 15
- **Fasti status**: NP (calendar entry is Feriae Iovi; the October Horse appears only in the late Calendar of Philocalus as "Equus ad Nixas fit")
- **Deity**: Mars (sacrifice); Jupiter (Ides dedication)
- **Ritual**: A two-horse chariot-race in the Campus Martius. The right-hand horse of the victorious pair was sacrificed by the flamen Martialis on an altar to Mars in the Campus Martius; killed with a spear (Timaeus). The horse's head was cut off and decorated with cakes. Inhabitants of the Via Sacra then fought those of the Suburra for possession of the head: if the Via Sacra faction won, the head was nailed to a wall of the Regia; if the Suburra won, it was fixed to the Turris Mamilia. The horse's tail (or genitals), still dripping blood, was rushed to the Regia where the blood fell on the sacred hearth. The Vestal Virgins probably kept some congealed blood for use at the Parilia (21 April).
- **Aition**: Scullard surveys two competing interpretations — agricultural (horse as Corn-spirit; Festus: id sacrificium fiebat ob frugum eventum, "that sacrifice was made for the success of the crops") and military (purification of the army returning from the summer campaign, linked with the Armilustrium on 19 October). He allows both may contain truth: an agricultural rite could have developed into a military one, especially given Mars' possible origin as an agricultural deity. By the later Republic the military interpretation prevailed; Timaeus and Polybius both set it in a military context.
- **Participants**: Flamen Martialis (sacrifice); inhabitants of Via Sacra vs. Suburra (struggle for the head); Vestal Virgins (blood preservation).
- **Page**: p. 193–195

## Ludi Capitolini
- **Day**: 15 October (approximate; not in the official calendars)
- **Fasti status**: not stated (private Games by the College of Capitolini, not public)
- **Deity**: Jupiter Feretrius (older tradition: Tertullian, Ennius); Jupiter Optimus Maximus (Livy's attribution)
- **Ritual**: Games given by the College of Capitolini, still active in Cicero's time. Athletic contests (gauntlet fighting with caestus, running). A proclamation "Sardians are for sale" (Sardi venales) was made and an old man wearing a child's bulla round his neck was led about in mockery (Plutarch). Little else is known of the programme.
- **Aition**: Attributed either to Romulus (for founding) or to Camillus — either to celebrate saving the Capitol from the Gauls (Livy) or to celebrate the conquest of Veii (Plutarch, Festus). Scullard rejects Plutarch's explanation of the "Sardians" as Etruscans from Sardis in Lydia; the reference must be to Sardinians. If pre-Republican, the Games originally honoured Jupiter Feretrius (whose ancient temple housed the spolia opima).
- **Participants**: College of Capitolini (organizers); equestrians (members); competitors (athletes).
- **Page**: p. 194–195

## Armilustrium
- **Day**: 19
- **Fasti status**: NP (Dies religiosus)
- **Deity**: Mars
- **Ritual**: Festival in honour of Mars marking the end of the campaigning season. The Salian priests probably danced and sang through the streets; during the sacrifices tubae (war-trumpets) were sounded; the arma (weapons) and ancilia (sacred shields) were purified and put away until the following year. The lustratio was performed on the Aventine, in an open space called Armilustrium south of the church of S. Sabina (per Plutarch and Varro), possibly the final station of the Salian procession. A note in the Praenestine calendar (possibly for 20 October but possibly a gloss to the 19th) records that worshippers "frequently used to taste blood before" (sanguinem gustare antea frequenter solebant), referring to blood from the sacrifice — a practice apparently discontinued by Augustus' time.
- **Aition**: Scullard frames the Armilustrium explicitly as the autumnal counterpart to the spring military ceremonies (Salii, Equirria, Quinquatrus, Tubilustrium in March), both bracketing the campaigning season. The October Horse on the 15th and the Armilustrium on the 19th together form the army's autumn purification.
- **Participants**: Salian priests; army (purification); unspecified sacrificants (blood tasting, formerly).
- **Page**: p. 195–196

## Ludi Victoriae Sullanae
- **Day**: 26 October – 1 November
- **Fasti status**: not stated (not in the Republican calendars; a later addition)
- **Deity**: Victoria (Sulla's)
- **Ritual**: Multi-day games established by Sulla in 81 BC to celebrate his victory over the Samnites at the Colline Gate. Culminated in Circenses on 1 November (the date of the battle). Staged by the praetors. First recorded on a denarius of 59 BC: "Sex. Nonius praetor (in 81 BC) ludos Victoriae primus fecit."
- **Aition**: Sulla's victory at the Colline Gate (82 BC, battle dated to 1 November). The games were subsequently called Victoriae Sullanae to distinguish them from Caesar's later Victory Games.
- **Participants**: Praetors (organizers); public (spectators).
- **Page**: p. 196

## Summary

October in Scullard's treatment is described as "a lean month for festivals." The chapter covers twelve named events, of which two — the **October Horse** (15 Oct) and the **Armilustrium** (19 Oct) — are explicitly paired as the army's autumn purification bracket, mirroring the spring military ceremonies in March. The **Meditrinalia** (11 Oct) and **Fontinalia** (13 Oct) are the month's agricultural/natural rites. The opening of the **mundus** on the 5th is one of three annual openings. The **Ludi Capitolini** (around 15 Oct) are private games not in the official calendars. The **Ludi Victoriae Sullanae** (26 Oct–1 Nov) are a late Republican, non-Republican-calendar addition. Minor rites include dedications on the Capitol (9 Oct), a Juno Moneta commemoration (10 Oct), the Penates on the Velia (14 Oct), and cult days for Fides (1 Oct) and for Juno Sororia/Janus Curiatius at the Tigillum (1 Oct). The **Ieiunium Cereris** (4 Oct) reflects strong Greek influence (Thesmophoria parallel). Scullard notes that no certain date can be fixed for a Liber/vintage rite in October despite later evidence suggesting 15 October.

**Festivals with NP status**: Meditrinalia (11), Fontinalia (13), Feriae Iovi/October Horse (15), Armilustrium (19).
**Festivals with C status**: Ieiunium Cereris (4), Iovi Fulguri/Iunoni Quiriti (7), Genio Publico etc. (9), Iunoni Monetae (10).
**Festivals with EN status**: Penatibus in Velia (14).
**Festivals with N status**: Fidei in Capitolio (1).
**Dies religiosi**: Mundus patet (5); Armilustrium (19).
