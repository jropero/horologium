# December — Scullard (1981)
**PDF pages used**: 199–215

General context: No public Games were held in December; six large-lettered festivals appear in the calendar. The month was under Vesta's protection. Rustic calendars note manuring of vineyards, sowing of beans, and gathering of olives.

---

## Neptuno et Pietati — dies natales

- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: N (KAL. DEC.)
- **Deity**: Neptune; Pietas
- **Ritual**: Commemoration of temple restorations. The temple of Neptune (dies natalis originally 23 July) was restored, probably by Cn. Domitius (consul 32 BC). Its famous sculptural group by Scopas represented Neptune, Thetis and others. The temple of Pietas stood by the Circus Flaminius (distinct from the one in the Forum Holitorium). Lydus adds worship of Aphrodite (Venilia?), Amphitrite (Salacia?), Tyche Ephore (Fortuna Respiciens?), Sophrosyne (Prudentia?), and Eros (Cupid?), but this may reflect later development.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 199

---

## Bona Dea (women's night festival)

- **Day**: 3 (not fixed; attested on this date in 63 and 62 BC; broadly "sometime in December")
- **Fasti status**: not in official calendars (category between private and public ceremonies, applicable only to women)
- **Deity**: Bona Dea
- **Ritual**: Held at night in the house of a sitting consul or praetor; men were entirely excluded. The room was decorated with vine-leaves; a pig was sacrificed; wine (called "milk") was offered to the goddess; there was music and dance. Vestal Virgins attended; the ceremony was held pro populo Romano. Myrtle was reportedly excluded from the ritual at home (Plutarch) and from the temple (Macrobius), though its exceptional use in December is debated. Cicero called the rites incredibilis caeremonia. Plutarch compared them to Orphic rites with paidia and much music. The festival was distinct from the annual sacrifice at Bona Dea's temple on 1 May.
- **Aition**: not stated (possible Tarentine origin suggested speculatively: the cult may have arrived from Tarentum, whence Dania = Bona Dea is said to have come)
- **Participants**: Women only; wife and female relatives of the host magistrate presided; Vestal Virgins attended
- **Page**: pp. 199–201

---

## Faunalia (rural Nones festival)

- **Day**: 5
- **Fasti status**: F (NON. DEC.) — not registered in the city calendars; held in the pagi
- **Deity**: Faunus
- **Ritual**: Annual rural festival in the pagi (country districts), not in Rome itself, celebrated by countryfolk in the fields. An offering of wine and a tender kid was made on an ancient altar with incense. Sheep gambolled; farmers danced on their land. Horace Odes 3.18 is the primary description. Faunus' city cult was introduced in the second century BC but never became popular with the urban population.
- **Aition**: not stated (Faunus' name probably derives from favere, "the kindly one," with euphemistic connotation)
- **Participants**: Rural population (pagani)
- **Page**: p. 202

---

## Tiberino et Gaiae — dies natalis

- **Day**: 8
- **Fasti status**: C (VI ID. DEC.)
- **Deity**: Tiberinus; Gaia
- **Ritual**: Festival at the temple of Tiberinus on the Tiber Island (dies natalis of the temple). In drought, prayers were offered with the formula adesto Tiberine cum tuis undis. Tiberinus was invoked by the pontiffs (Servius) and augurs (Cicero); the pontiffs maintained the bridge over the Tiber and performed non mediocri ritu on both sides of the river. Gaia: two legendary women named Gaia were linked to the Tiber Island; a theriomorphic origin (gaius = magpie) has been postulated. The temple site on the Tiber Island is not precisely known.
- **Aition**: Varronian tradition in Augustine attributes the cult of Tiberinus to Romulus
- **Participants**: Pontiffs; augurs
- **Page**: pp. 202–203

---

## Tribuni plebis magistratum ineunt

- **Day**: 10
- **Fasti status**: C (IV ID. DEC.) — noted in Praenestine Fasti
- **Deity**: none
- **Ritual**: The tribunes of the plebs assumed office on this date, not on 1 January. A civic/administrative entry, not a religious festival in the proper sense.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: Tribunes of the plebs
- **Page**: p. 203

---

## Agonalia (Agoni in Indigeti / Sol Indiges)

- **Day**: 11
- **Fasti status**: NP (III ID. DEC.) — confirmed by Ostian Fasti
- **Deity**: Sol Indiges (Indiges); also ascribed to the Sun by Lydus
- **Ritual**: Fourth and final Agonalia of the year (see also 9 January, 17 March, 21 May). The December celebration was linked to the end of the sowing season (Columella's septimontialis satio). The sun's warming rays were invoked to bring newly sown crops to fruition. An offering was presumably made at the altar of Sol Indiges on the Quirinal (cf. 9 August).
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: Rex sacrorum (as for other Agonalia)
- **Page**: p. 203

---

## Septimontium

- **Day**: 11 (same day as Agonalia, but a separate and distinct festival)
- **Fasti status**: not in early calendars (only in late calendars, e.g. Silvius); feriae non populi sed montanorum modo (Varro LL 6.24)
- **Deity**: Diva Palatua (flamen Palatualis officiated on the Palatine); deities of the other hills unspecified
- **Ritual**: Festival of the seven ancient hills of Rome's earliest settlement (Palatium, Velia, Fagutal, Cermalus, Caelius, Oppius, Cispius — not the later canonical Seven Hills). Sacrifices were offered on each of the seven hills; the flamen Palatualis sacrificed to Palatua on the Palatine. The festival was restricted to the montani (hill inhabitants), not the whole people. No carts or vehicles drawn by beasts of burden were allowed in the city on this day. Festus attests sacrifices specifically at the Palatine (to Palatua) and the Velia. Emperor Domitian celebrated it with an epulum where senators and knights received better food than the plebs. The ceremony may have involved a lustration of the districts and a common meal (Scullard notes this is hypothesis).
- **Aition**: Goes back to the seventh century BC when settlers of seven hills first united; reflects Rome's earliest phase of expansion from the Palatine
- **Participants**: Montani of the seven hills; flamen Palatualis; magistri et flamines of individual hills (attested for Mons Oppius in an inscription)
- **Page**: pp. 203–204

## Consualia (December I) — dies natalis of Aventine temple

- **Day**: 12
- **Fasti status**: EN (PR. ID. DEC.)
- **Deity**: Consus
- **Ritual**: The temple of Consus on the Aventine had its dies natalis on either 21 August or 12 December; a Augustan restoration on 12 December remains uncertain. Consus was also honoured with Games three days later (15 December Consualia proper).
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 204

---

## Telluri et Cereri — dies natalis Telluris, lectisternium Cereris

- **Day**: 13
- **Fasti status**: NP (ID. DEC.) — confirmed by Ostian Fasti; Praenestine Fasti also mentions a lectisternium
- **Deity**: Tellus (primary); Ceres (lectisternium)
- **Ritual**: Festival at the temple of Tellus in Carinae (a district at the south-west end of the Esquiline). The temple was vowed by P. Sempronius Sophus in 268 BC during an earthquake in battle against the Picentes, on the site of the destroyed house of Sp. Cassius. Ceres was closely linked with Tellus (e.g. the porca praecidanea was offered to both). A lectisternium of Ceres is attested by Arnobius and the Praenestine Fasti (the only annual public lectisternium recorded in the Fasti). The Senate sometimes met in the temple; its walls bore a map of Italy.
- **Aition**: Temple vowed in 268 BC during an earthquake; on the site of Sp. Cassius' demolished house (destroyed 495 BC)
- **Participants**: Aediles conducted the lectisternium (Praenestine Fasti: aediles … lectisternium … faciunt)
- **Page**: pp. 204–205

---

## Consualia (December II)

- **Day**: 15
- **Fasti status**: EN (Ant. mai.) — but Scullard notes it was probably NP; XVI (Julian XVIII) KAL. IAN.
- **Deity**: Consus
- **Ritual**: Second festival of Consus in December (Feriae Conso per Praenestine Fasti). The Praenestine note records that horses and mules were crowned with flowers "because in his protection (…). And so the king on a horse (…)." Probably repeated the horse-and-chariot-racing and garlanding of horses that marked the first Consualia on 21 August. Linked in the December sequence with Saturnalia (17th) and Opalia (19th).
- **Aition**: not stated explicitly
- **Participants**: Rex (mentioned in Praenestine note riding on a horse); horses and mules
- **Page**: p. 205

## Saturnalia

- **Day**: 17–23 (strictly 17 December religiously; extended to 7 days in practice by Cicero's time; fluctuated between 3, 5 and 7 days under the Empire)
- **Fasti status**: NP implied; XIV (Julian XVI) – VIII (Julian X) KAL. IAN. — Saturno ad Forum (Amit.), Feriae Saturno (Ant. mai.), Feriae servorum (Silv.)
- **Deity**: Saturn
- **Ritual**: The festival opened at Saturn's temple in the Forum with a great public sacrifice at which senators and knights wore togas, followed by a public banquet (convivium publicum) open to all — this was established or formalized in 217 BC (Livy). At the banquet informal clothes (synthesis) and soft felt caps (pilei) were worn; it ended with the shout Io Saturnalia. Shops, law-courts and schools were shut; public gambling was permitted. Saturn's statue (filled with oil, normally bound in woollen bonds) had its bonds loosed on the festival day. In households: masters waited on their slaves at table (slaves briefly treated as equals). A Saturnalicius princeps (mock king / Master of Revels) was chosen within the family. Gifts: pottery dolls (sigillaria) for children, wax candles (cerei) for friends. Slaves received extra wine rations (Cato: 3½ congii).
- **Aition**: Saturn's temple dedicated 17 December, probably in the early Republic. Saturn assimilated to Greek Kronos / lord of the Golden Age. The loosing of the statue's bonds explained by Macrobius as symbolizing seed bursting from the womb; Scullard suggests an agricultural analogy (seed stored, now released). Festival held near the winter solstice as a season of rest and merrymaking.
- **Participants**: All Roman citizens; senators and knights at the opening sacrifice; slaves at household feasts; the Saturnalicius princeps within each family
- **Page**: pp. 205–208

## Opalia

- **Day**: 19
- **Fasti status**: N (Ant. mai.) / NP (Amit.); XII (Julian XIV) KAL. IAN.
- **Deity**: Ops (Feriae Opi, Opi ad Forum per Amit.)
- **Ritual**: Second festival of Ops in December (cf. Opiconsivia 25 August). The cult-centre ad Forum is debated: Mommsen suggested a sacellum attached to Saturn's temple; more probably Ops had a separate temple (possibly that dedicated by L. Metellus as Ops Opifera, site unknown). The December sequence — Consualia (15th), Saturnalia (17th), Opalia (19th) — reflects a cluster of related agricultural/abundance festivals.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 208

## Io[ventati?] — possible sacrifice to Juventas

- **Day**: 19 (tentative; depends on restoration of Ostian Fasti: Io[ventati?])
- **Fasti status**: not stated (Ostian Fasti, restoration uncertain)
- **Deity**: Juventas (more likely than Jupiter per Scullard)
- **Ritual**: Annual ceremony in which Roman youths (iuvenes) who had assumed the toga virilis made offerings at the temple of Juventas (near the Circus Maximus, dedicated 191 BC). Juventas had an old aedicula on the Capitol within Minerva's cella in the temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus. At assumption of manhood, each youth made a payment to the treasury of Juventas. Cicero attests that the anniversaria sacra Iuventatis were an annual event. The date of 19 December is speculative but would be suitable for preparing iuvenes for responsibilities in the new year.
- **Aition**: Temple vowed by M. Livius Salinator at the battle of Metaurus (207 BC); erection begun 204, dedicated 191 BC by C. Licinius Lucullus
- **Participants**: Roman young men (iuvenes) attaining manhood
- **Page**: pp. 208–209

## Divalia / Angeronalia

- **Day**: 21
- **Fasti status**: NP (DIVALIA ANGERONAE per Ostian Fasti); X (Julian XII) KAL. IAN.
- **Deity**: Diva Angerona (primary); Volupia (sacellum where the statue stood)
- **Ritual**: Statue of Diva Angerona with a sealed bandage over her mouth (a finger on lips, per Macrobius) stood on the altar of the sacellum of Volupia, where the Nova Via entered the Velabrum. Pliny connects Angerona's sealed mouth with the secret name of Rome (Roma's protecting deity's name was not to be uttered, to prevent enemies summoning her by evocatio). Whether held in the Curia Acculeia (Varro) or the sacellum of Volupia (Macrobius) is debated; possibly the same location under different names. Also on this day: a sacrifice of a pregnant sow, loaves/cakes (panes) and honey-wine drink (mulsum) to Hercules and Ceres — nothing else is known about this ceremony (Scullard fn. 292).
- **Aition**: Multiple contested explanations: (1) Angerona gives relief from angina pectoris / angor (Julius Modestus, Verrius Flaccus); (2) connected with the winter solstice — raising up the sun again (Mommsen; Scullard notes this is problematic pre-Julian calendar); (3) linked with angustissimo die anni. The sealed-mouth statue explained as guarding Rome's secret ritual name (possibly "Amor" = Roma reversed, per Lydus).
- **Participants**: Pontiffs and other priests (implied by location in sacellum)
- **Page**: pp. 209–210

## Herculi et Cereri (sacrifice on 21 December)

- **Day**: 21
- **Fasti status**: not stated separately
- **Deity**: Hercules; Ceres
- **Ritual**: A pregnant sow, loaves or cakes (panes), and a drink of honey and wine (mulsum) were offered to Hercules and Ceres. No other details are known.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 210

## Laribus Permarinis — dies natalis

- **Day**: 22
- **Fasti status**: C (IX [Julian XI] KAL. IAN.) — Praenestine Fasti
- **Deity**: Lares Permarini (Lares who guard sailors)
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of the temple of the Lares Permarini in the Campus Martius. The temple was vowed by L. Aemilius Regillus during a naval engagement against Antiochus the Great (190 BC) and dedicated in 179 BC by M. Aemilius Lepidus. A dedicatory inscription in Saturnian metre was placed on its walls. The temple was later enclosed in the Porticus Minucia built in 110 BC.
- **Aition**: Temple vowed during naval battle against Antiochus the Great (190 BC); L. Aemilius Regillus also made a dedication to the Cabiri of Samothrace after his victory
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 211

## Larentalia (Larentinalia)

- **Day**: 23
- **Fasti status**: NP (VIII [Julian X] KAL. IAN.)
- **Deity**: Acca Larentia (Di Manes)
- **Ritual**: Parentatio (funeral rites) performed at the supposed tomb of Acca Larentia in the Velabrum where it joins the Nova Via. Pontiffs and the flamen Quirinalis officiated. Varro says the priests made offerings to the Di Manes of slaves. The Praenestine Fasti note: Parentalia are held in honour of Acca Larentia, with variants about her identity. Libations were brought to her tomb. Puzzlingly, this is a December parentatio while the general Parentalia falls in February; Cicero (via Plutarch) explains that Decimus Brutus (consul 138 BC) used December as the "last month of the year" in the old reckoning. The festival was also linked (unclearly) to feriae of Jupiter per the Praenestine calendar.
- **Aition**: Explicitly stated in Praenestine Fasti: Acca Larentia received public funerary rites because she left to the Roman people a large sum of money bequeathed to her by her lover Tarutilus. Variants: (1) she was the nurse of Romulus and Remus; (2) a courtesan, mistress of Hercules; (3) wife of Faustulus (= Faunus); (4) Romulus' mother rather than foster-mother. Her name possibly connected to Lares (Mater Larum) or to Sabine goddess Larunda / Lara.
- **Participants**: Pontiffs; flamen Quirinalis
- **Page**: pp. 211–212

---

## Dianae et Iunoni Reginae in Campo; Tempestatibus — dies natales

- **Day**: 23
- **Fasti status**: not separately stated (noted alongside Larentalia)
- **Deity**: Diana; Juno Regina; Tempestates
- **Ritual**: Dies natales of two temples dedicated by M. Aemilius Lepidus as censor in 179 BC — both temples in the Circus Flaminius area (Diana's just west of the Circus; Juno's in Campo per Ant. mai. / in Circo Flaminio per Livy). Both had been vowed during Lepidus' campaign of 187 BC. The Senate granted 20,000 asses towards the cost of Games: scenic Games for 3 days after Juno's dedication, 2 days for Diana, and 1 day of circus games for each. Also on 23 December a celebration of the Tempestates (possibly the same temple Ovid mentions under 1 June, but this is obscure).
- **Aition**: Diana's temple vowed during campaign of 187 BC; Juno Regina's temple vowed during the final battle of that campaign
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 212

## Bruma (winter solstice) / Sol Invictus

- **Day**: 25
- **Fasti status**: C (VI [Julian VIII] KAL. IAN.) — noted but the day had no importance in the Republican civil calendar before the cult of Sol Invictus (AD 273/274)
- **Deity**: Sol (Unconquered Sun / Sol Invictus — Empire only; Aurelian's temple dedicated AD 274)
- **Ritual**: In the Julian calendar, 25 December was the astronomically significant day of the winter solstice (bruma). Varro (LL 6.8) reckoned a year as the sun's passage from one bruma to the next. Bruma was also used loosely for a period from 24 November to 25 December. In Byzantine times Brumalia became a festival lasting into at least the 10th century. Scullard notes the Christian Nativity was first attested on 25 December in the Calendar of Philocalus (AD 336), possibly chosen in opposition to the Sol Invictus festival — but these are post-Republican developments.
- **Aition**: not stated for the Republican period
- **Participants**: not stated (pre-Empire: astronomical observance only; post-AD 274: Sol Invictus cult)
- **Page**: p. 212

## Summary

Scullard covers fifteen distinct entries for December across pages 199–212. The six NP (large-lettered) festivals the calendars preserve for December are: **Agonalia** (11), **Telluri** (13), **Consualia** (15, probably NP), **Saturnalia** (17–23), **Divalia/Angeronalia** (21), and **Larentalia** (23). Key observations:
- The December cluster of Consualia–Saturnalia–Opalia (15th/17th/19th) reflects an agricultural sequence invoking storage, abundance, and the earth's produce.
- The Bona Dea night ceremony (c. 3 Dec) and Faunalia (5 Dec) are both attested but absent from the official urban calendars.
- Scullard is explicit that the Septimontium (11 Dec) was not a populi festival and only appears in late calendars.
- The Larentalia parentatio in December (23rd) is historically puzzling given the February Parentalia; Scullard reviews the scholarly debate without resolving it.
- Pages 213–215 are Part Three (Triumphs and Ovations), not December-specific festival material.
