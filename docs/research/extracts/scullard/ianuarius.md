## Vota Publica (New Year Inauguration)
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: F (Fasti Praenestini: *[Ann]us no[vus incipit] quia eo die ma[gistratus] ineunt*)
- **Deity**: Jupiter Optimus Maximus
- **Ritual**: The incoming consuls took auspices at dawn (either at home or on the Capitol), then donned purple-bordered togas (*praetexta*) and received formal salutations (*salutatio*) from senators, friends, and clients. A solemn procession wound up the Capitoline hill — senators behind, equites in front — accompanied by the sacrificial animals. At the temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus the consuls sat on ivory chairs (*sellae curules*), received public acclamation, then each sacrificed a white bull in payment of the vows made the previous year. New vows (*vota publica*) for the safety of the state were then proclaimed. The senior consul subsequently convened the Senate on the Capitol, announced the date of the Feriae Latinae, and addressed provincial assignments. The consuls were escorted home by the whole Senate.
- **Aition**: None stated.
- **Participants**: Both consuls, Senate, equites, Roman people (in festal dress).
- **Page**: pp. 52–55
- **Notes**: By at least 153 BC, 1 January was the fixed entry date for consuls. Scullard notes the auspices had become largely a formality by the late Republic. He also notes that within five days of entry the consuls had to swear to obey the laws before the quaestors in the temple of Saturn, and receive formal *imperium* from the Comitia Curiata (itself by then a formality of thirty lictors).

## Dedicatio Aesculapii et Vediovis in Insula
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: F (same day as consular ceremonies; calendars record *Aesculapio, Co[.]o, Vediove* in Ant. mai.; *Aesculapio, Vediovi in Insula* in Praen.)
- **Deity**: Aesculapius (primary), Vediovis, Co[r]onis (or Consus — identification uncertain)
- **Ritual**: Anniversary (*dies natalis*) of two temples on the Tiber Island. Aesculapius' temple (at the south end of the island, near modern S. Bartolomeo) was dedicated 1 January 291 BC; Vediovis' island temple was dedicated 1 January 194 BC. Cult of Aesculapius included incubation (sleeping in the temple for divine dream-cures). Sacrifice offered; the Tiber Island cult was modelled on that of Epidaurus.
- **Aition**: A plague prompted consultation of the Sibylline Books, which sent envoys to Epidaurus; a sacred snake crawled ashore on the Tiber Island and a temple was erected there. Ovid narrates the founding myth at length in the *Metamorphoses* (over 100 lines) and briefly in *Fasti* 1.289ff.
- **Participants**: Worshippers seeking healing; general Roman public; the crowd from the consular procession might extend to the island.
- **Page**: pp. 54–58
- **Notes**: Co[.]o in Ant. mai. is uncertain: Degrassi prefers Co[r]o(nidi) (Coronis, mother of Aesculapius), since Consus requires two letters (ns) that do not fit the stone. Vediovis also had festivals on 7 March and 21 May. His nature is disputed (chthonic, anti-Jupiter, or diminutive Jupiter); connection with Jupiter considered fairly secure by Scullard. Sacrifice to Vediovis was a she-goat killed *humano ritu* (Aulus Gellius) — possibly a surrogate for human sacrifice or an offering for a dead man, though Scullard is sceptical.

## Compitalia (Ludi Compitales)
- **Day**: Moveable; traditionally c. 3–5 January in the later Empire (Calendars of Philocalus and Silvius); Macrobius still treats it as *conceptivae*; in Cicero's day it fluctuated around the beginning of January on a single day. Held between the Saturnalia (17 December) and 5 January.
- **Fasti status**: C (feriae conceptivae — *Feriae Conceptivae*)
- **Deity**: Lares Compitales (Lares of the crossroads); Mania (mother of the Lares, mentioned in connection with earlier rites)
- **Ritual**: At crossroads shrines (*compita*) — open in all four directions to admit the Lares — woollen dolls were hung for each free person in the household and woollen balls for each slave the night before. Fattened pigs sacrificed (Propertius); each family contributed a honey-cake (Dionysius of Halicarnassus). Men preparing the sacrifices were slaves temporarily stripped of marks of servitude; the bailiff (*vilicus*) officiated — the only occasion he was permitted to do so. A broken plough (*fracta iuga*) was hung as a sign of completed agricultural work. Feasting, dancing, and games followed in the streets of each neighbourhood (*vicus*). Slaves were given an extra ration of wine (Cato). The praetor proclaimed the date in a formula cited by Aulus Gellius: *'Die noni populo Romano Quiritibus Compitalia erunt; quando concepta fuerint, nefas.'*
- **Aition**: Origin assigned by Romans to Servius Tullius; urban development organized by the Etruscan kings. Macrobius records that boys were formerly sacrificed to Mania for the household's well-being, until Brutus (first consul) substituted heads of garlic and poppy.
- **Participants**: Country and city *pagani* (parishioners/neighbours), slaves (given unusual freedom), the *vilicus*, each household. In late Republic the *collegia compitalicia* took on a political character and were suppressed in 64 BC, revived by Clodius, suppressed again by Caesar, and ultimately restored by Augustus with the *Lares Compitales* linked to the *Genius Augusti*.
- **Page**: pp. 59–60
- **Notes**: Scullard treats Compitalia and Sementivae as the two primitive moveable festivals of January. The Compitalia marked the end of one agricultural period and beginning of the next.

## Vicae Potae
- **Day**: 5
- **Fasti status**: F (NON. IAN.; recorded only in Fasti Antiates Maiores)
- **Deity**: Vica Pota
- **Ritual**: *Dies natalis* (birthday) of the shrine of Vica Pota on the Velia, near the early house of the Valerii.
- **Aition**: None stated by Scullard.
- **Participants**: Not specified.
- **Page**: p. 61
- **Notes**: Vica Pota was an old Roman goddess whose name was etymologized from *vincere* and *potiri*; she was later identified with Victoria. The Palatine temple of Victoria was dedicated in 294 BC (see Scullard under 1 August).

## Agonalia
- **Day**: 9
- **Fasti status**: NP (V. ID. IAN.)
- **Deity**: Janus (Ovid: *Ianus Agonali luce piandus erat*); also attested by the Arval Brethren's rite of sacrificing a ram to Janus
- **Ritual**: The Rex Sacrorum sacrificed a ram in the Regia: *'dies agonales, per quos rex in Regia arietem immolabat'* (cited by Scullard from Paulus). Ovid records the ram: *'ita rex placare sacrorum / numina lanigerae coniuge debet ovis.'* The same rite recurred on the other three *dies agonales* (17 March, 21 May, 11 December).
- **Aition**: None given. Scullard reviews multiple ancient derivations of the name (from *agone* = 'Shall I proceed?'; *agantur* = animals driven not led; *Agnalia* = festival of lambs; the victim's agony; Greek *agones* = games; *agonia* = old word for *hostia*) and finds none convincing, concluding that *agonium* may simply be a ritual word for a festival or sacrifice.
- **Participants**: Rex Sacrorum (officiated in the Regia).
- **Page**: pp. 61–62
- **Notes**: Scullard does not explicitly state the deity is Janus for all four *dies agonales*, only implies it for 9 January via Ovid. Festus' statement that the Quirinal was originally *Collis Agonus* and Varro's mention of a College of *Salii Agonenses* are noted but not treated as conclusive.

## Carmentalia (First Day)
- **Day**: 11
- **Fasti status**: NP (III ID. IAN.)
- **Deity**: Carmentis / Carmenta (both forms found)
- **Ritual**: Festival of Carmentis, a goddess of childbirth and prophecy. The *flamen Carmentalis* officiated; sacrifice was performed, but all skins of animals (*scortea*) were excluded from the shrine. Pontiffs may also have been involved (*sacrum pontificale*, Ovid). Women worshipped her at her shrine near the Porta Carmentalis at the foot of the Capitol (probably within the *Forum Holitorium*).
- **Aition**: Mythology made Carmentis the mother of Evander, who came from Arcadia to Italy and settled on the Palatine (connecting her to the site of Rome). Ovid and Virgil developed this literary tradition. The Fasti Praenestini record that the festival on 15 January was established by a victorious Roman general (Romulus?) to celebrate the capture of Fidenae. Ovid's explanation of the second celebration (matrons refused to bear children after losing the right to ride in carriages, provoking a senate decree) is dismissed by Scullard as most improbable.
- **Participants**: Women (primarily); the *flamen Carmentalis*; possibly pontiffs.
- **Page**: pp. 62–64
- **Notes**: The two-day festival (11 and 15 January) is unusual: nearly all festivals fell on days of uneven number, and 13 January was skipped. Scullard suggests the two days may represent originally separate celebrations by two communities (Palatine and Quirinal = Romans and Sabines?). Name derives from *carmen* (spell/prophecy/poem). Varro records that two Carmentes were set up altars in Rome — Postverta (for feet-first births) and Prorsa (for normal births). An archaic altar in the Forum Boarium may be hers. Leather taboo: the *flaminica Dialis* could not wear shoes made from the skin of an animal that had died naturally.

## Iuturnae (Juturnalia)
- **Day**: 11
- **Fasti status**: NP (recorded in Fasti Antiates Maiores alongside Carmentalia; Ovid says the cult day of the Campus Martius temple was 11 January)
- **Deity**: Juturna (water nymph, spirit of a spring in the Forum and of the river Numicus)
- **Ritual**: The *Juturnalia* was celebrated by 'those whose business was connected with water' (*qui artificum aqua exercent*). Juturna had two cult sites: (1) the *Lacus Iuturnae* in the south-west corner of the Forum at the foot of the Capitol (water from this pool used in official Roman sacrifices); (2) a temple in the Campus Martius vowed by a Lutatius Catulus (probably consul of 241 BC, victor at the Aegates Insulae, or possibly victor over the Cimbri in 101 BC). The Campus Martius temple's cult day was 11 January.
- **Aition**: Castor and Pollux were said to have watered their horses at Juturna's pool after bringing news of the Roman victory at Lake Regillus (494 BC), and again after the victory over Macedon (168 BC). This linked her site to the temple of Castor and Pollux beside it.
- **Participants**: Workers connected with water (*qui artificum aqua exercent*); the *statio aquarum* (water-department) had its headquarters in an adjacent precinct in the fourth century AD.
- **Page**: pp. 64–65
- **Notes**: Scullard notes Varro recorded Juturna as a goddess of healing (a statue of Aesculapius was found in the adjacent *statio aquarum* precinct). The site was excavated early in the twentieth century; Juturna's shrine (*aedicula*) was restored in the 1950s. Broken statues of Castor and Pollux were found in the pool's basin. Virgil gave Juturna a prominent role in the last book of the *Aeneid* as the sister of Turnus.

## Carmentalia (Second Day)
- **Day**: 15
- **Fasti status**: NP (XVI [Julian XVIII] KAL. FEB.)
- **Deity**: Carmentis / Carmenta
- **Ritual**: Second celebration of the same cult as 11 January (see above). Same shrine, same exclusion of leather. The Fasti Praenestini record this second day was established by a victorious general (possibly Romulus) to celebrate the capture of Fidenae.
- **Aition**: See entry for 11 January. Ovid's carriages-and-abortion story for why a second day was added is dismissed by Scullard.
- **Participants**: Women; *flamen Carmentalis*; possibly pontiffs.
- **Page**: pp. 62–64
- **Notes**: The skip of 13 January remains unexplained. See full discussion under Carmentalia (11 January).

## Sementivae (Paganalia)
- **Day**: Moveable; Ovid describes it under 24–26 January. Held on two non-consecutive days with an interval of seven days between them.
- **Fasti status**: Not in the fixed Fasti (Feriae Conceptivae)
- **Deity**: Tellus (first day), Ceres (second day)
- **Ritual**: On the first day a sacrifice of a spelt cake (*mola salsa*) and a pregnant sow was offered to Tellus (Mother Earth); on the second day to Ceres (goddess of growth). The oxen used in ploughing were adorned with garlands. Prayers were said for the protection of the seed already sown against birds, beasts, and diseases. Possibly *oscilla* (hanging figures) were hung from trees, as at the Latin Festival. Varro describes friends gathering in the temple of Tellus to celebrate the Sowing Festival 'in the manner of their fathers and grandfathers.'
- **Aition**: None stated.
- **Participants**: Farmers (*pagani*); the community at large. Varro and friends at the temple of Tellus.
- **Page**: pp. 68–69
- **Notes**: Scullard notes Ovid may treat Sementivae and Paganalia as identical, while Varro's briefer notice possibly implies two distinct festivals. The first temple of Ceres was dedicated on the Aventine in 493 BC after a famine; Tellus' temple was vowed in 268 and built on the Esquiline (see also 13 December). The main sowing season was autumn to early December; spring sowing (spring wheat *trimestre*, some millet and legumes) explains why the festival fell in late January.

## Castori et Polluci (Dies Natalis Templi)
- **Day**: 27
- **Fasti status**: C (IV [Julian VI] KAL. FEB.; Fasti Verul.: *Castori, Polluci ad Forum*)
- **Deity**: Castor and Pollux (Dioscuri)
- **Ritual**: Anniversary of the dedication of the temple of Castor and Pollux in the Forum. According to the Fasti Praenestini and Ovid, the temple was dedicated on 27 January 484 BC (vowed after the battle of Lake Regillus, where the Dioscuri helped Rome). *Ludi Castores* were celebrated at Ostia during the Roman Empire on this date.
- **Aition**: The Dioscuri were said to have fought for Rome at Lake Regillus and then ridden to Rome to announce the victory, watering their horses at the Lacus Iuturnae. The temple was vowed in consequence of this aid.
- **Participants**: Cavalry (Castor and Pollux were patrons of the Roman cavalry); general Roman public. The temple also served as a political platform and meeting place for the Senate.
- **Page**: pp. 65–68
- **Notes**: Livy gives 15 July as the dedication date — Scullard follows Mommsen in thinking Livy confused the date of the battle with that of the temple's restoration (Tiberius rebuilt and rededicated it in AD 6). The temple was also identified with the *Dei Penates Publici* (public penates), since the Dioscuri were merged with the two Penates deities when the cult arrived in Rome, possibly via Tusculum. An archaic inscription of c. 500 BC found at Lavinium (1959) attests the cult in Latium: *Castorei Podlouque qurois*. The three surviving columns belong to the Augustan reconstruction. The temple's high podium was used as a rostra and played a major role in late Republican political life.
