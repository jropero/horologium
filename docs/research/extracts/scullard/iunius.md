## Kalends — Iunoni Monetae / Iunoni in Arce
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Juno Moneta
- **Ritual**: Temple vowed by M. Furius Camillus in 345 BC, dedicated 1 June 344 BC on the Capitol (later site of S. Maria in Aracoeli). The title *Moneta* = "Warner" — either because her sacred geese warned of the Gallic attack in 390 BC, or because a voice from her temple warned of an earthquake and ordered an expiatory sacrifice of a pregnant sow. Later, a mint was established in or adjacent to the temple, giving rise to Latin *moneta* ("mint") and English "money".
- **Aition**: Two competing aitia: (1) the geese of Juno hissed and awakened Manlius to the Gallic night attack (390 BC); (2) Cicero's version: a voice from the temple ordered *procuratio* with a pregnant sow during an earthquake. Both are explicitly discussed by Scullard.
- **Participants**: Not specified for the cult act itself.
- **Page**: p. 127

## Kalends — Marti in Clivo
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: N (same calendar day)
- **Deity**: Mars
- **Ritual**: Temple on the Slope of Mars (*Clivus*) outside the Capena Gate, between the first and second milestones on the Appian Way, dedicated 1 June probably in 388 BC. Contained a statue of Mars and wolves. Soldiers assembled here before setting out for war. Starting point of the *transvectio equitum* (cavalry victory march, held annually on 15 July).
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Soldiers; cavalry for the *transvectio* later in the year.
- **Page**: p. 127

## Kalends — Tempestatibus
- **Day**: 1 (date doubtful: Ovid assigns 1 June; calendar Ant. mai. gives 23 December)
- **Fasti status**: not stated
- **Deity**: Tempestates (divine Storms)
- **Ritual**: Temple dedicated by L. Cornelius Scipio (consul 259 BC) after surviving a great storm off Corsica in the First Punic War. Located outside the Capena Gate near the Tomb of the Scipios. Cicero treats the Tempestates as consecrated according to Roman rites.
- **Aition**: Scipio's survival from the storm off Corsica — explicitly stated. (Two temples or a restoration may explain the dual date.)
- **Participants**: Not specified.
- **Page**: p. 127

## Kalends — Carnae (Carnaria)
- **Day**: 1 (= *Kalendae Fabariae*)
- **Fasti status**: not stated
- **Deity**: Carna (Caelian hill)
- **Ritual**: Very old goddess with a temple on the Caelian hill. Offerings of bean-meal and lard (*puls fabacia et larido*) made both publicly and privately (Varro). Carna was said to preside over the vital organs (liver, heart, etc.); prayers offered for preservation of these organs. The day was also called *Kalendae Fabariae* because mature beans are offered in the sacrifice. The ritual Ovid records (touching doorposts with arbutus, sprinkling special water, offering intestines of a two-month-old sow, prayer to ward off vampires/striges) belongs to Cardea, but Scullard notes Ovid conflates the two figures and the bean ritual may properly belong to Carna. The custom long survived; a late inscription calls it *Carneria*.
- **Aition**: Ovid's conflation with Cardea introduces the story of Janus seducing Cardea and giving her white thorn to ward off striges — but Scullard notes this aition belongs to Cardea, not Carna proper.
- **Participants**: Public and private; both sexes implied by Varro.
- **Page**: p. 128

## Bellonae in Circo Flaminio
- **Day**: 3 (in NON. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Bellona (war-goddess)
- **Ritual**: Temple vowed by Appius Claudius Caecus in 296 BC during war against Etruscans and Samnites, dedicated a few years later in the Campus Martius near the later Circus Flaminius (outside the city walls). Used by the Senate for receiving foreign ambassadors not admitted to Rome and for welcoming returning generals requesting triumphs (imperium-holders could not enter the city). A small pillar (*columella*) in front of the temple served as the symbolic "enemy territory" over which the fetial priest cast a spear to declare war on overseas enemies.
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Senate; fetial priests; returning generals.
- **Page**: p. 146

## Herculi Magno Custodi / Ludi in Minicia
- **Day**: 4 (PR. NON. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Hercules Magnus Custos ("Hercules the Great Guardian")
- **Ritual**: Temple near the Circus Flaminius. Ovid (F.6.209ff.) notes an inscription of Sulla, who may have restored rather than first dedicated the temple. Probably the temple built on the Sibylline Books' orders after Hannibal's early victories (218 BC). The *Porticus Minucia* was built in the Circus Flaminius in 110 BC; games (*Ludi in Minicia*) held there may be linked to the neighbouring Hercules cult.
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Not specified.
- **Page**: p. 146

## Dio Fidio in Colle
- **Day**: 5 (NON. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Dius Fidius (= Semo Sancus Dius Fidius)
- **Ritual**: Temple of Semo Sancus Dius Fidius on the Quirinal (between Via Nazionale and Via Quirinale). Founded allegedly by Tarquinius Superbus; dedicated 466 BC. Deity of "Divine Good Faith (*fides*)" and oaths; everyday oaths *medius fidius* / *me hercule* invoked him. Oaths had to be taken in the open air (not under a roof) so the sky-god could witness them; the temple roof was accordingly open. The temple contained a statue of Gaia Caecilia/Tanaquil, the early Rome–Gabii treaty inscribed on ox-hide, and a deposit of bronze rings (*aenei orbes*) from confiscated goods of the traitor Vitruvius (329 BC). A company of *sacerdotes bidentales* dedicated to him; a *bidental* = a lightning-struck spot requiring sacrifice of two-year-old sheep.
- **Aition**: Not stated explicitly (legendary foundation by Tarquinius unverified).
- **Participants**: Oath-takers; *sacerdotes bidentales*.
- **Page**: pp. 147–148

## Vesta Aperitur (opening of the penus)
- **Day**: 7 (VII ID. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: N, Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Vesta
- **Ritual**: The inner sanctum (*penus*) of the temple of Vesta was opened — for women only (men were forbidden to enter the temple at all) — and remained open until 15 June. The days from 7 to 15 were *religiosi*: no public or private business, no journeys. This formed the preliminary period leading to the Vestalia on 9 June.
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Women worshippers.
- **Page**: p. 148

## Piscatorii Ludi
- **Day**: 7 (VII ID. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: N, Dies religiosus (same day as Vesta aperitur)
- **Deity**: Vulcan (Tiberinus receives honour but the fish are dedicated to Vulcan)
- **Ritual**: Games of the Fishermen, celebrated by the *praetor urbanus* on behalf of the Tiber fishermen. Location disputed: Ovid places them on the Campus Martius by the Tiber; Festus on the far bank. The fishermen's catch was not taken to the market but to the *Area Volcani* (Volcanal in the Roman Forum, no temple, at the foot of the Capitol) — live fish given to Vulcan as substitutes for human souls. At the later Volcanalia (23 August) the same logic applied: animals offered to the fire-god as substitutes for people, so the god would spare things easily caught by fire.
- **Aition**: Not stated (Scullard explains the logic as substitution sacrifice for Vulcan).
- **Participants**: Praetor urbanus; Tiber fishermen.
- **Page**: p. 148

## Menti in Capitolio
- **Day**: 8 (VI ID. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: N, Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Mens (Mens Bona, "Right Thinking" / "Mind")
- **Ritual**: Temple vowed by praetor T. Otacilius in 217 BC following the disaster at Lake Trasimene, dedicated two years later on the Capitol. Also attributed to M. Aemilius Scaurus (115 BC, restoration). The deification of an abstract concept — perhaps to remind Romans of their religious duties at a moment of crisis, or to acknowledge that Flaminius had "lost his mind" at Trasimene.
- **Aition**: Vowed after Trasimene (217 BC) — explicitly stated.
- **Participants**: Not specified.
- **Page**: pp. 148–149

## VESTALIA
- **Day**: 9 (V ID. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: N, Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Vesta
- **Ritual**: Major festival of Vesta, guardian of the communal hearth. The round *aedes* (technically a *penus*, not a templum; contained no statue, only the perpetual fire) was open to women worshippers from 7–15 June. Women entered barefoot and brought simple food offerings ("a clean platter contains the food offered to Vesta"). The Vestals prepared the *mola salsa* (holy salted cake): water fetched from a sacred spring (originally spring of Egeria outside the Capena Gate, later spring of Juturna) carried in narrow-based vessels that could not be set down. Salt specially prepared: brine pounded and baked, the lump cut with an iron saw. Spelt ears gathered on 7, 9, 11 May threshed, ground, salted, and made into *mola salsa* for offering to Vesta. The day was a holiday for bakers and millers; millstones and the asses turning them were garlanded with violets and hung with small loaves (depicted in a Pompeii wall-painting).
- **Aition**: Not stated (origin traced to the communal hearth of the shepherd villages coalescing into Rome — Scullard's historical reconstruction, not an explicit ancient aition).
- **Participants**: Vestal Virgins (preparing *mola salsa*); matron women (allowed to visit temple barefoot); bakers and millers (holiday).
- **Page**: pp. 149–150

## MATRALIA — Matri Matutae
- **Day**: 11 (III ID. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: N? (uncertain), Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Mater Matuta
- **Ritual**: Festival of Mothers in honor of Mater Matuta, old Italian goddess. Temple in the Forum Boarium (excavated near S. Omobono), established by King Servius Tullius, dedicated 11 June (same day as adjacent Fortuna temple); destroyed by fire 213 BC, rebuilt 212 BC. Mater Matuta also had a famous temple at Satricum. Cult practices: (1) Her statue could be adorned only by a *univira* (wife of one marriage). (2) Female slaves were excluded; one slave girl was brought in and then slapped on the head and beaten (warning to others or fertility survival?). (3) Sacred cakes (*testuacia*) cooked in old-fashioned earthenware pots (*testu*; Ovid: *liba tosta*) were offered. (4) Women prayed to the goddess not primarily for their own children but for their nephews and nieces (*puerisororii*).
- **Aition**: Ovid erroneously identified Mater Matuta with the Greek sea-goddess Leucothea/Ino — Scullard explicitly rejects this as mythological, not genuine aition.
- **Participants**: Free Roman women; *univirae* (to dress the statue); slave girls (subject of the beating ritual).
- **Page**: pp. 150–152

## Fortunae (Virgo) — Forum Boarium
- **Day**: 11 (same day as Matralia; both dedicated 11 June by King Servius Tullius)
- **Fasti status**: N? Dies religiosus (as for Matralia)
- **Deity**: Fortuna (Virgo)
- **Ritual**: Temple of Fortuna in the Forum Boarium, adjacent to the Mater Matuta temple. Both founded by Servius Tullius and destroyed/rebuilt together. Called *Fortuna Virgo*; girls dedicated their robes to her at marriage. Contained a mysterious cult statue covered with *undulatae togae* (wavy robes such as the kings of Rome wore); variously said to depict Servius Tullius (Ovid, Dionysius) or Fortuna (Pliny, Varro) — or possibly *Pudicitia* (Chastity). The wooden statue reportedly survived the fire of 213 BC and was still venerated in Dionysius' day.
- **Aition**: Foundation by King Servius Tullius (explicitly stated). The humble/slave origin of Servius is given as the aition for the cult's popularity among slaves and common people — but that element is given for Fors Fortuna (24 June), not this Fortuna Virgo.
- **Participants**: Girls (dedicating robes at marriage).
- **Page**: pp. 151–152

## Feriae Iovi / (Quinquatrus minusculae)
- **Day**: 13 (ID. IUN.)
- **Fasti status**: NP, Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Jupiter (Ides sacred to Jupiter); Minerva (patron of flute-players); Jupiter Invictus
- **Ritual**: All Ides were sacred to Jupiter. Ovid (alone) mentions the dedication of a temple of *Jupiter Invictus* on this date (possibly the same temple as *Jupiter Victor* on 13 April). **Quinquatrus minusculae** (13–15 June): the *Lesser Quinquatrus*, festival of the guild of flute-players (*tibicines*). Flute-players wandered the city for three days in festive robes, wearing masks and women's long robes (*stolae longae*), making music with public licence. They met at the temple of Minerva on the Aventine and dined in that of Jupiter. Historical background: in 311 BC, after censors banned their traditional banquet in the Jupiter temple, the flute-players withdrew to Tibur; the Senate arranged their return through a ruse, and they were then granted three days of roaming the city annually and the right to dine in the temple.
- **Aition**: The 311 BC expulsion and restoration — explicitly stated by Livy, retold by Scullard.
- **Participants**: *Tibicines* (guild of flute-players); general public (spectators).
- **Page**: pp. 152–153

## Vesta clauditur — Q.ST.D.F.
- **Day**: 15 (XVI KAL. QUINCT.)
- **Fasti status**: Q.ST.D.F. (*Quando stercum delatum fas*), Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Vesta
- **Ritual**: The temple of Vesta was swept clean (*stercus* = the accumulated dirt/rubbish), the dirt carried to the *porta stercoria* — an alley halfway up the Capitoline Slope — and then (per Ovid) carried down to the Tiber. The temple was then closed. Once done, public business became lawful again (*fas*) and the *religiosi* days around the Vestalia ended. The accumulated rubbish from the Vesta temple being swept into the Tiber also marked the earliest auspicious date for marriage in the second half of June (Ovid F.6.219ff.).
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Vestal Virgins.
- **Page**: p. 153

## Summano ad Circum Maximum
- **Day**: 20 (XI KAL. QUINCT.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Summanus (god of nocturnal lightning)
- **Ritual**: Temple near the Circus Maximus (probably west side towards the Aventine), founded c. 278 BC. Received black wethers (*verveces atri*; Arval Brethren records *vertices atri*) — uniquely, Jupiter never receives black animals. Also received wheel-shaped cakes. Summanus was responsible for *nocturnae fulmina* (nocturnal lightning), counterpart to Jupiter who wielded thunderbolts by day. Pliny: among nine Etruscan lightning-gods, only Jupiter (daytime) and Summanus (nocturnal) were retained by the Romans. The cult appears in Plautus (*Bacchides*, *Curculio*, c.200 BC) and Lucretius; it gradually declined in the later Republic.
- **Aition**: Explicitly stated: a terracotta statue of Summanus on the roof of the Capitoline temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus was struck by lightning at night; when the head could not be found, haruspices said it had been thrown into the Tiber; it was recovered at the exact spot predicted; a temple was then built for Summanus. The temple itself was later struck by lightning in 197 BC. Varro also records altars dedicated to Summanus by King Tatius.
- **Participants**: Not specified. Common people may still have offered cakes as "insurance" against nocturnal storms (Scullard's suggestion).
- **Page**: pp. 153–155

## Forti Fortunae (trans Tiberim)
- **Day**: 24 (VII KAL. QUINCT.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Fors Fortuna
- **Ritual**: Two temples of Fors Fortuna across the Tiber (at the first and sixth milestone from the city), both traditionally founded by King Servius Tullius and dedicated on 24 June. A third temple built by Sp. Carvilius in 293 BC from war booty "near the temple of the goddess dedicated by King Servius Tullius." Dedications found near the Arval Brethren's grove from sellers of violets, roses, garlands, wool-dealers, and bronze-smiths. Festival was rowdy: flower-bedecked boats on the Tiber carrying revellers and tipplers. Especially popular with common people and slaves. Day coincided with Midsummer's Day (summer solstice) — Scullard notes attempts to find solstitial ritual connections are "not convincing."
- **Aition**: Foundation by Servius Tullius (explicitly stated); his allegedly humble/slave origin explains the cult's popularity with slaves and the common people — explicitly stated by Scullard citing Ovid.
- **Participants**: General populace, especially common people, slaves; sellers of garlands and produce; revellers on the Tiber.
- **Page**: pp. 155–156

## Ludi Taurei quinquennales
- **Day**: 25–26 (VI–V KAL. QUINCT.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Di inferi (gods of the underworld)
- **Ritual**: Quinquennial (every five years) Games, the only Circus Games held in the Circus Flaminius (rather than the Circus Maximus). Included horse-racing around the turning-post (*circum metam*). Established in the reign of Tarquinius Superbus according to the Books of Fate because pregnant women had been stricken with a plague caused by piecemeal sale of beef (*ex carne taurorum*) — possibly sacrificial meat. Probably of Etruscan origin. Only recorded Republican celebration: 186 BC, *religionis causa*, followed by ten days of Games by M. Fulvius Nobilior (actors, athletes, lion and panther hunts).
- **Aition**: Plague of pregnant women caused by eating beef sold piecemeal — explicitly stated (from Festus citing the Books of Fate).
- **Participants**: Circus audience; presiding magistrates.
- **Page**: p. 156

## Laribus V[...] (Laribus Vialibus?)
- **Day**: 27 (IV KAL. QUINCT.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Lares (uncertain: possibly Lares Viales)
- **Ritual**: Calendar reads *Laribus V[...]* (Ant. mai.). K. Latte proposed *Laribus Vialibus* (guardians of roads and travellers), but S. Scullard suggests this refers to the temple of the Lares at the top of the Via Sacra near the later Arch of Titus (mentioned in 106 BC; restored by Augustus). To be distinguished from the Lares Praestites (festival 1 May).
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Not specified.

## Iovi Statori in Palatio
- **Day**: 27 (IV KAL. QUINCT.; same calendar day as Lares V[...])
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Jupiter Stator ("the Stayer")
- **Ritual**: Temple near the Arch of Titus, probably identifiable with foundations uncovered in 1829. Temple erected by M. Atilius Regulus soon after 294 BC on the *fanum* Romulus had set aside. The Senate met here on notable occasions (most famously 8 November 63 BC when Cicero denounced Catiline). In 207 BC the twenty-seven maidens learned Livius Andronicus' hymn (to Juno, not Jupiter) here before processing through the city.
- **Aition**: Romulus vowed a temple to Jupiter Stator to halt the Romans fleeing the Sabines in the Forum valley after the rape of the Sabine women — explicitly stated. Temple not built until Atilius Regulus made a similar vow after 294 BC.
- **Participants**: Senate (meetings); religious processions.
- **Page**: p. 157

## Herculi Musarum in Circo Flaminio
- **Day**: 29 (= 30 Julian; PR. KAL. QUINCT.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Hercules Musagetes; the nine Muses
- **Ritual**: Temple of "Hercules of the Muses" in the Circus Flaminius area, built by M. Fulvius Nobilior after his triumph over the Ambracians (or paid from fines as censor in 179 BC). Contained statues of the nine Muses and a statue of Hercules playing a lyre (both brought from Ambracia), and an old bronze shrine of the Muses transferred from the temple of Honos and Virtus. Nobilior also set up a copy of his *Fasti* (a calendrical work) in the temple. Statues of the Muses depicted on denarii of Q. Pomponius Musa (66 BC). The temple was surrounded by a *Porticus Philippi* after restoration by L. Marcius Philippus in 29 BC. Well known to Cicero as an example of a man of war honouring the arts of peace.
- **Aition**: Nobilior's interest in Hercules Musagetes arose because while in Greece he heard that Hercules was leader of the Muses (*Musagetes*) — explicitly stated (via Eumenius). His friendship with Ennius is also cited as motivation.
- **Participants**: Literary men; descendants of Fulvius Nobilior (Scullard's imaginative inference for the festival day).
- **Page**: pp. 157–158

## Summary

Scullard covers **17 named cult events** in June across **15 calendar dates**, plus the general note that days 7–15 were *religiosi* and the first half of June was inauspicious for marriage. The chapter is structured date by date with a brief introductory section on the month's name (debated: Juno, *Iuventas*, Concordia, or even M. Iunius Brutus) and the month's character under Mercury / Hercules / Fons Fortuna in the rustic calendars.

**Key festivals by significance:**
- **Vestalia (9 June)** — most extensively treated; the *mola salsa* preparation, the penus ritual, and the holiday for bakers/millers are all described in detail.
- **Matralia (11 June)** — second most detailed; the slave-girl beating, the *univira* restriction, the *testuacia* cakes, and the prayer for nephews/nieces are all documented.
- **Fors Fortuna (24 June)** — rowdy festival with boats on the Tiber; popular with slaves and common people.
- **Summanus (20 June)** — extensively discussed as the god of nocturnal lightning; unusually full aition.
- **Quinquatrus minusculae (13–15 June)** — flute-players' festival, well documented via Livy.

**Dating note**: Scullard notes that the Julian calendar shift means some June dates differ by one day from the pre-Julian Republican calendar (e.g. 15 June = XVII KAL. QUINCT. in Julian; XVI KAL. QUINCT. Republican).

**Fasti statuses attested**: N (1, 5, 7, 8, 9 June); NP (13 June); C (3, 4, 19, 20, 24, 25–26, 27, 29 June); Q.ST.D.F. (15 June). The status of 11 June (Matralia) is marked N? with a question mark by Scullard.
