## Feriae Marti / Salii / Matronalia
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: NP; Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Mars (primary); Juno Lucina; Vesta
- **Ritual**: The old New Year's Day. The Salii (two colleges of twelve patrician priests each, Palatini and Agonenses/Collini) set out on the first of their processions through the city (arma ancilia moventur), performing a ritual war-dance (Carmen Saliare), beating their figure-of-eight shields (ancile) with swords, halting at certain points for elaborate dances to flute accompaniment. In the evening they rested at a mansio Saliorum and feasted. The sacred fire of Vesta's temple was tended and fresh laurels were affixed to the Regia, to the houses of the flamines, and to the Curiae Veteres. A celebration was held, possibly at the ancient altar of Mars in the Campus Martius.
- **Aition**: According to legend, one original shield (ancile) fell from heaven as a gift from Jupiter to Numa; to prevent theft, the smith Mamurius made identical copies. The laurels in the Regia courtyard were sacred to Mars, who had a shrine (sacrarium) there. A late legend told that Juno became mother of Mars and that the child was born on 1 March.
- **Participants**: Salii (patrician priests, patrimi et matrimi); Vestal Virgins (tended fire); flamines (fresh laurels at their houses)
- **Page**: p. 85–87

## Matronalia
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: NP (unofficial designation; observed on the Kalends of March)
- **Deity**: Juno Lucina
- **Ritual**: The Kalends of March was also the dies natalis of the temple of Juno Lucina on the Esquiline (near S. Prassede on the Cispius), which lay in a grove long consecrated to the goddess. Women participated in her cult; those who worshipped had to unbind their hair and untie any knot in dress (nothing could symbolically hamper safe delivery). Husbands prayed for the health of their wives and gave them presents; wives entertained their slaves and served their food. Gift-giving was widespread and mentioned frequently in Latin literature (Plautus, Tibullus, Tertullian).
- **Aition**: Not explicitly stated by Scullard beyond the temple dedication.
- **Participants**: Matrons; husbands; slaves entertained by their mistresses
- **Page**: p. 87

## Vediovis
- **Day**: 7
- **Fasti status**: F
- **Deity**: Vediovis (Vediovis Artis; Vediovis inter duos lucos on the Capitol)
- **Ritual**: Festival at Vediovis' temple on the Capitoline, which lay between the two summits (inter duos lucos). Ovid associates the site with the ancient asylum Romulus is said to have set aside inter duos lucos as a place of refuge. The letters ARTIS in calendar notation remain obscure (possibly = AEDIS). Substantial remains of the temple found in 1939 behind the Tabularium, with a marble statue.
- **Aition**: Ovid associates the temple with Romulus' asylum, though Scullard notes this may be merely antiquarianism on the poet's part.
- **Participants**: Not stated
- **Page**: p. 87–88

## Arma Ancilia Moventur (Second Salian Procession)
- **Day**: 9
- **Fasti status**: C; Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Mars
- **Ritual**: The sacred shields (ancilia) were carried round the city by the Salii for the second time (cf. 1 March). Scullard notes processions were held perhaps only on the 1st, 9th and 23rd of March, though the festival lasted until the 24th or possibly until end of month.
- **Aition**: Not stated separately from 1 March entry
- **Participants**: Salii
- **Page**: p. 89

## Equirria (March)
- **Day**: 14
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Mars
- **Ritual**: Horse-racing festival, quickly following that of 27 February; again linked with preparations for the campaigning season. It has been suggested that the March Equirria originally fell on the Ides (15th) and was later put back a day to avoid clashing with the festivals of Jupiter and Anna Perenna on the 15th, but Scullard regards this as improbable.
- **Aition**: Not stated
- **Participants**: Not stated specifically
- **Page**: p. 89–90

## Mamuralia / Sacrum Mamurio
- **Day**: 14
- **Fasti status**: NP (same day as Equirria; listed in Philocalus and Menologia but not earlier calendars)
- **Deity**: Mamurius Veturius (legendary smith; possibly equated with Mars or of Etruscan origin)
- **Ritual**: References in late calendar of Philocalus and the Menologia. Lydus (6th c. AD) states that on the Ides of March (possibly in error for the 14th) a man dressed in skins was beaten with rods and driven out of the city with shouts of "Mamurius". Servius records that on a day consecrated to Mamurius the Salii "pellem virgis caedunt ad artis similitudinem" (beat a skin as a smith beats metal); Minucius Felix says they carried the shields and "pelles caedunt". Rustic calendars list "A sacred rite to Mamurius" in March. Whether there was a separate ceremony or Mamuralia is merely a late name for the Equirria remains uncertain.
- **Aition**: Story that Mamurius was driven from the city because misfortune befell the Romans when they changed the use of the shields (Lydus, somewhat confused). Possible interpretation: Mamurius Veturius as personification of the old year (or old Mars) in whom community sins were concentrated — a scapegoat — but Scullard notes this is speculation and "Mamurius remains a mystery."
- **Participants**: A man dressed in skins (beaten and driven out); Salii (beating a skin)
- **Page**: p. 89–90

## Feriae Annae Perennae
- **Day**: 15
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Anna Perenna
- **Ritual**: Festival held on the first full moon of the new year, in a grove near the Tiber at the first milestone on the Flaminian Way (near modern Porta del Popolo). Macrobius says people went to sacrifice to her both publicly and privately; offerings were made "ut annare perennareque commode liceat." Ovid describes what he saw: people scattered on the grass in couples, some in tents or huts, revelling and drinking as many cups of wine as the number of years they prayed to live; they sang popular songs learned in the theatres and staggered about dancing. On his way home Ovid saw a drunken old woman lugging a drunken old man. Lydus mentions public prayers to secure a healthy year. Anna was represented as an old woman.
- **Aition**: Anna is a female personification of the year (annus); Perenna signifies her care for the continual succession of years. Macrobius says offerings were made "ut annare perennareque commode liceat." Mythology (possibly invented by Ovid) identified her with Dido's sister Anna who came to Italy and fell victim to Lavinia's plotting; the story that she perished in the river Numicus led to the suggestion she was a water-nymph, which Scullard treats as "wild." Scullard notes her dim origins may have been linked with fertility rites, but the festival had become an uninhibited "day-out in the country" for urban populations.
- **Participants**: People generally (public and private), men and women; formal sacrifice by priests; Lydus mentions public prayers
- **Page**: p. 90–91

## Itur ad Argeos
- **Day**: 16–17
- **Fasti status**: Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Argei (twenty-seven sacraria at various points within the four "Servian" regions of Rome)
- **Ritual**: A procession "went to (the shrines of) the Argei," twenty-seven sacraria at various points within Rome's four Servian regions, going back to a very early stage of Rome's growth before the Capitol or Aventine were included. Scullard defers full discussion to the May entry (14 May), when puppets called Argei were thrown into the Tiber.
- **Aition**: Not stated for this March entry; discussion deferred to 14 May
- **Participants**: Not specified
- **Page**: p. 91

## Liberalia
- **Day**: 17
- **Fasti status**: NP (calendars vary: some give Liberalia only, others add Agonalia; Caer. gives both and adds "Libero Liberae")
- **Deity**: Liber Pater; Libera
- **Ritual**: Old women acting as priestesses of Liber Pater, crowned with ivy, sat at various points in the city displaying cakes (liba) made of oil and honey and offering sacrifice on behalf of any customer. Liber was an old Italian fertility god, especially of the vine, later equated with Dionysus. The Calendar of Caere records the Liberalia honoured both Liber and his female counterpart Libera. Augustine (following Varro) records that a phallos was carried around the countryside on a cart and then escorted into town (at Lavinium, where Liber was worshipped for a whole month, a virtuous matron had to lay a wreath on the image once it reached the market-place) — done for the sake of crops and to repel witchcraft (fascinatio). Liber and Libera were also worshipped alongside Ceres in her temple on the Aventine (founded 493 BC), corresponding to the Eleusinian triad Demeter/Kore/Iacchos. By Ovid's day Liber shared Ceres' Games (Cerialia) on 19 April. The late calendars of Philocalus and Silvius and Ausonius record Games in the Campus Martius on 17 March.
- **Aition**: Not explicitly stated as an aition by Scullard; the agricultural/fertility basis is described as functional context.
- **Participants**: Old women acting as priestesses of Liber; general public (customers at sacrifice); boys coming of age (see below)
- **Page**: p. 91–93

## Agonalia (March)
- **Day**: 17
- **Fasti status**: NP (combined with Liberalia entry; Macrobius calls the day agonium Martiale; Varro says in the books of the Salii Agonenses the day was called Agonia)
- **Deity**: Mars (Agonium Martiale per Macrobius); possibly also Liber
- **Ritual**: The Rex Sacrorum sacrificed a ram in the Regia. Macrobius says the day of the Liberalia was named by the priests agonium Martiale; Varro says in the books of the Salii Agonenses the day was called Agonia. Whether Mars had a share in the festival of Liber is uncertain; a separate sacrifice (agonium) may have been made to Mars independently.
- **Aition**: Not stated
- **Participants**: Rex Sacrorum
- **Page**: p. 92–93

## Toga Virilis Ceremony
- **Day**: 17
- **Fasti status**: not stated (domestic ceremony on the day of Liberalia)
- **Deity**: Lares (domestic); Liber or Iuventus (Capitol sacrifice)
- **Ritual**: The normal day for boys to come of age (though in the late Republic and Empire some other days were also used). The boy put aside the purple-edged toga praetexta and golden bulla in front of the images of the Lares, to whom he sacrificed; he then put on the toga virilis (also called pura or libera). He was escorted by family and friends to the Tabularium in the Forum (deductio in forum) for registration as a full citizen and enrollment in a tribe; he then made a further sacrifice on the Capitol to Liber or Iuventus or both. Wealthy families entertained clients and held a grand family dinner. The boy was then ready for tirocinium fori (preparation for public life) and tirocinium militiae (preliminary military service).
- **Aition**: Not stated
- **Participants**: The adolescent boy; family and friends; Lares (household gods); Liber or Iuventus (Capitol)
- **Page**: p. 92–93

## Quinquatrus
- **Day**: 19
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Mars (primary; Fasti Vaticani state Feriae Marti); Minerva (secondary, popular association)
- **Ritual**: Called Quinquatrus because it was (by Roman inclusive reckoning) the fifth day after the Ides; came popularly to be regarded as a period of five days (19–23 March, later Quinquatria), giving schoolboys a five-day holiday. The Salii danced in the comitium in the presence of pontiffs and tribuni celerum (Fasti Praenestini note; confirmed by Varro). Charisius states the arma ancilia were purified on this day. The tribuni celerum were officers of the ancient Celeres (earliest Roman cavalry or royal bodyguard); their presence suggests the ceremony was designed to purify the weapons not only of the Salii but of the whole army — a ritual preparation for the campaigning season, like the Equirria. The popular association with Minerva arose because her temple on the Aventine was dedicated on that day (date of dedication not known). From 19 March onward (though the 19th itself was bloodless), gladiatorial contests were held on the next four days at least by Ovid's time. Ovid urges a wide variety of workers to pray to Minerva: boys and girls seeking learning, girls seeking skill in spinning and weaving, fullers, dyers, cobblers, doctors, schoolmasters, sculptors, painters. Schoolmasters dedicated their first fee (stips) from a new pupil at the Quinquatrus (Tertullian).
- **Aition**: Not stated as a formal aition; the popular identification with Minerva arose from her Aventine temple's dedication on that day. The original meaning relates to Mars and the annual purification of the ancilia.
- **Participants**: Salii; tribuni celerum; pontiffs; craftsmen and artisans of all kinds; schoolmasters and schoolboys; doctors; artists; fullers; dyers; cobblers
- **Page**: p. 93–95

## Tubilustrium
- **Day**: 23
- **Fasti status**: NP; Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Mars (primary; Praenestine calendar calls it a festival of Mars); Minerva (Ovid attributes it to "forti deae," a strong goddess = Minerva); Nerio/Nerine (Lydus; alleged wife of Mars, Sabine name)
- **Ritual**: The Purification of the Trumpets (tubi). Repeated also on 23 May (followed on both dates by Q.R.C.F. the next day). The Praenestine calendar records that on this day "the trumpets used in the sacred rites are purified in the Hall of the Shoemakers" (in atrio sutorio tubi lustrantur, quibus in sacris utuntur); a ewe lamb was sacrificed. Site of the Hall of the Shoemakers is not known. Lydus mentions a "moving of the arms," i.e. a dance of the Salian priests. The ceremony involved a symbolic purification of the trumpets of the army, making it fit for war. The Salii danced through the streets. The Tubilustrium developed in the same way as the Quinquatrus (Mars + Minerva association).
- **Aition**: Not stated
- **Participants**: Salii; priests performing purification; army (symbolically represented)
- **Page**: p. 94–95

## Q.R.C.F. (Quando Rex Comitiavit Fas)
- **Day**: 24
- **Fasti status**: F
- **Deity**: None specified (procedural/legal day)
- **Ritual**: One of two days (also 24 May) on which in early times the Comitia Calata met to sanction wills; court proceedings could start only after the rex had dismissed the comitia. The Praenestine Fasti explains Q.R.C.F. as the day on which the rex, having presided over the Comitia, declared it fas (lawful) to transact business. The early form of will (witnessed before the Comitia Curiata) had become obsolete before the first century BC, succeeded by the private mancipatory will requiring only five citizens as witnesses. Scullard notes and rejects the alternative interpretation that Q.R.C.F. stood for the day the king (Tarquin) fled from the comitium (a confusion with the Regifugium of 24 February).
- **Aition**: Not stated
- **Participants**: Rex Sacrorum; Comitia Calata / Comitia Curiata
- **Page**: p. 95–96

## Luna in Aventino (dies natalis)
- **Day**: 31
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Luna (Moon)
- **Ritual**: Birthday (dies natalis) of the temple of the Moon on the Aventine, attributed to Servius Tullius; first mentioned as damaged by storm in 182 BC. Mummius dedicated some of the spoils from Corinth in it; Gaius Gracchus hurt himself jumping from it when fleeing enemies; lightning damage to it led to the postponement of consular elections in 84 BC. It probably lay at the northern end of the Aventine near the Porta Trigemina. Luna also had a shrine on the Palatine illuminated at night. Varro, when invoking twelve gods as "guides of husbandmen," lists the Sun and Moon (along with Jupiter and Tellus) as those "whose seasons are observed at sowing and harvesting," suggesting Luna was an old rural deity.
- **Aition**: The introduction of the worship of Moon and Sun into Rome is attributed by Varro and Dionysius of Halicarnassus to Titus Tatius; Scullard considers early introduction more likely than late importation from Greece.
- **Participants**: Not stated
- **Page**: p. 96

## Summary

Scullard's March chapter (pp. 84–96) covers eleven distinct festival days and twelve named observances. The month is dominated by the cult of Mars and military-preparatory rites: the Salii danced in procession on at least 1, 9, and 23 March; the Equirria (horse-racing) was held on 14 March; the Quinquatrus (19 March) purified the ancilia; the Tubilustrium (23 March) purified the trumpets. The ambiguous Mamuralia/Sacrum Mamurio on 14 March may represent a scapegoat expulsion ritual but remains contested.

Other notable observances: the Matronalia (1 March, women's festival at Juno Lucina's temple); the festival of Anna Perenna (15 March, popular outdoor celebration at the first milestone on the Flaminian Way); the Liberalia (17 March, Liber Pater / Libera, with the toga virilis ceremony for boys coming of age); and the Argei procession (16–17 March, deferred to the 14 May throwing of puppets into the Tiber). The month closes with the dies natalis of the temple of Luna on the Aventine (31 March).
