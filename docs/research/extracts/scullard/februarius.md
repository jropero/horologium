## Iuno Sospita (lunoni Sospitae Matri Reginae)
- **Day**: 1 (Kalends of February)
- **Fasti status**: N (Ant. mai.)
- **Deity**: Juno Sospita Mater Regina
- **Ritual**: Ovid records that 'at the beginning of the month Sospita, the neighbour of the Phrygian Mother Goddess, is said to have been honoured with new shrines'. The consuls made an annual sacrifice at her main cult-centre at Lanuvium. When Rome granted Lanuvium citizenship in 338 BC it officially adopted the cult; the pontiffs administered it. The goddess wore a goatskin drawn over her head as a helmet and carried a spear and shield. Blindfolded girls brought barley cakes annually to a snake in her sacred grove at Lanuvium; acceptance of the gift proved the girls' virginity and assured the year's fertility.
- **Aition**: Livy records that in 197 BC C. Cornelius Cethegus vowed a temple to Juno Sospita during battle against the Insubrian Gauls, dedicated in 194. Ovid's temple, said to be near the Palatine Mater Magna, had already fallen by his day.
- **Participants**: Consuls (annual sacrifice at Lanuvium); pontiffs (oversight of cult).
- **Page**: p.71
- **Notes**: Confusion in sources (Livy, Ovid) about whether the Forum Holitorium or Palatine site is meant. Temple restored by L. Julius, consul 90 BC, after a dream-report by Caecilia, daughter of Metellus Balearicus.

## Helernus (Helerna)
- **Day**: 1 (Kalends of February)
- **Fasti status**: not stated (embedded in 1 Feb. entry)
- **Deity**: Helernus (primitive deity, perhaps of the underworld; name uncertain — MSS vary between Avernus, Elernus, Elemo)
- **Ritual**: Ovid says that on 1 February the grove of Helernus was thronged with worshippers: 'the pontiffs still bring sacrifices thither' (sacra ferunt). A black ox was sacrificed, according to Festus.
- **Aition**: none given by Scullard
- **Participants**: Pontiffs; general worshippers.
- **Page**: p.72
- **Notes**: Helernus's grove was possibly near the Tiber below the Palatine. The name and deity remain poorly understood.

## Concordia (Concordiae in Capitolio)
- **Day**: 5 (Nones of February)
- **Fasti status**: N (Ant. mai.; Praen. records it as "Concordiae in Arce")
- **Deity**: Concordia
- **Ritual**: Dies natalis of temple vowed by praetor L. Manlius after he crushed a mutiny of troops in Cisalpine Gaul in 218 BC, dedicated two years later. Situated probably on the east side of the Capitol, overlooking the older temple of Camillus in the Forum below. No specific annual ritual described by Scullard beyond the anniversary.
- **Aition**: none given
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p.73
- **Notes**: Scullard's transition paragraph notes that after 5 February a busy two-week period begins: Fornacalia (ending 17th), Parentalia (13th–21st/22nd), Lupercalia (15th), Quirinalia (17th), Terminalia (23rd), Regifugium (24th), Equirria (27th).

## Faunus (Fauno in Insula)
- **Day**: 13 (Ides of February)
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Faunus
- **Ritual**: Urban festival at the temple of Faunus on the Tiber Island, built in 196 BC with money from fines levied on defaulting public-land farmers, dedicated 194 BC. The attempt to urbanize Faunus's rural cult was largely unsuccessful; in the countryside his winter festival continued to be celebrated in the pagi with dancing and merry-making.
- **Aition**: none given
- **Participants**: not stated; originally farmers and rural communities in the pagi.
- **Page**: p.73
- **Notes**: No trace of the Tiber Island temple survives. Faunus's main festival was in December (p.201). The February date is linked to the island temple dedication.

## Fornacalia (Feriae Conceptivae)
- **Day**: moveable; proclaimed by Curio Maximus on or before the Nones (5 February); ended 17 February (Quirinalia = Stultorum Feriae, the fallback day for those who missed their curia's meeting)
- **Fasti status**: Feriae Conceptivae (moveable public feast)
- **Deity**: No major deity; Fornax (Goddess of the Oven) was a later invention. Dionysius of Halicarnassus (2.23) records that the curiae tables were dedicated to Iuno Curitis. Ovid (F.2.525) says that happy farmers (coloni) prayed to Fornax.
- **Ritual**: 'Feast of Ovens'. Each of the thirty curiae held its own meeting at its assembly hall (curia building) to toast spelt (far) and share a communal meal. The Curio Maximus proclaimed the date in a set formula and posted notices for each curia in the Forum. Members who did not know their curia or missed its meeting could participate in the general assembly on the Quirinalia (Stultorum Feriae). Meals were of archaic simplicity: barley-bread, cakes and spelt, first-offerings of fruits, libation wine in earthen cups, no silver or gold (Dionysius, 2.23). Festus (82L) says the Feast was established to toast the spelt (far) because sacrifices were made at the oven (fornax) in bakehouses.
- **Aition**: none given; origins described as very primitive neighbourly custom.
- **Participants**: All thirty curiae and their members; Curio Maximus; curia flamines.
- **Page**: pp.73–74
- **Notes**: Stultorum Feriae ("Feast of Fools") on 17 February was the deadline for those who had not attended their curia's proper meeting.

## Parentalia
- **Day**: 13–21 February (begins at the sixth hour of 13 February; Vestal parentat on first day per Phil.)
- **Fasti status**: Dies religiosus (NP on 13th per Farn./Silv.; see also entry for Feralia on 21st). Calendars note: Parentatio incipit (Farn.), Parentatio tumulorum incipit (Silv.), Virgo Vestalis parentat (Phil.).
- **Deity**: Di Manes (ancestral dead)
- **Ritual**: Period for appeasing the dead (placandis Manibus). All temples closed; no fires burned on altars; marriages forbidden; magistrates laid aside insignia. Groups of mourners visited family tombs outside the city and performed private rites (sacra privata). Offerings were simple: a tile wreathed with votive garlands, sprinkled corn, salt, bread soaked in wine, loose violets — left on a sherd in the middle of the road (near tomb-lined roads). Commemoration focused on dead kin, especially parents. On the first day a Vestal Virgin (presumably the Senior Vestal) performed ceremonies for the dead (parentat). Tarpeia's restless ghost may have been propitiated at this time, but Scullard treats this as uncertain.
- **Aition**: none given; described as possibly less ancient than the Lemuria (9–13 May).
- **Participants**: All Roman families visiting tombs; Vestal Virgin on first day; private.
- **Page**: pp.74–75
- **Notes**: The Parentalia were one of two annual festivals for the dead (the other being the Lemuria in May, when each day was marked N in the calendars, whereas the dies parentales were not).

## Feralia
- **Day**: 21 February (ix KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: F (Ant. mai.; Maff.) / FP (Caer.; Vend.). Dies religiosus.
- **Deity**: Di Manes (official); Tacita / Muta (Silent/Mute Goddess, Mother of the Lares) in private rite.
- **Ritual**: Culmination of the Parentalia; the one public festival of the Parentalia period. Offerings carried to tombs (Varro: from inferi and ferre, "to carry"; or ferire, "to strike" — sheep sacrificed to the dead per Festus). Ovid also describes a private witch-rite performed in honour of Tacita: an old hag, seated among girls, placed three lumps of incense under a threshold, bound enchanted threads with dark lead, mumbled seven black beans, roasted the sewn-up head of a small fish pierced with a bronze needle, dropped wine on it; she and companions drank the remaining wine. The purpose was to silence enemies and prevent their curses.
- **Aition**: none given for the official rite. Tacita's rite is described as private sympathetic magic, possibly unconnected with the official Feralia.
- **Participants**: Families at tombs (official); old hag and girls (private magic).
- **Page**: pp.74–75
- **Notes**: The private rite of Tacita (later Muta, identified with Mother of the Lares) is presented by Ovid as set on the day of the Feralia, but Scullard doubts it had much real connection with the official festival.

## Caristia / Cara Cognatio
- **Day**: 22 February (vm KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Lares (family gods)
- **Ritual**: Family reunion and love-feast. After propitiating the dead at the Parentalia/Feralia, living kin renewed mutual ties and resolved quarrels. The day was named from cari (dear kinsfolk). A shared family meal was held, each member contributing food; unkind or guilty relatives were excluded. Worship was paid to the Lares: incense offered to the family gods; food set before them on a platter. Lares were depicted as youthful dancing male figures pouring wine from a drinking-horn. Offerings included grapes, corn, honeycombs, cakes, wine, incense, flowers, and sometimes pigs.
- **Aition**: none given; Scullard notes the antiquity is uncertain but the custom seems a natural development of the Parentalia.
- **Participants**: All living members of a family; all kin except those deemed guilty or unkind.
- **Page**: pp.75–76
- **Notes**: The custom was continued in Christian times; the Catholic Church converted it into the feast of St Peter (Cathedra Petri) on 22 February, observed until at least the twelfth century AD.

## Lupercalia
- **Day**: 15 February (xv KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: NP. Dies religiosus.
- **Deity**: Faunus (per Ovid); Inuus (per Livy, possibly "the Goer-in", god of sexual intercourse); both identified with Pan. "Lupercus" was an Augustan invention; the original deity is uncertain and probably pre-anthropomorphic.
- **Ritual**: Two colleges of Luperci — Quinctiales (or Quintilii, founded by Romulus) and Fabiani (or Fabii, founded by Remus) — met at the Lupercal, the sacred cave at the south-west foot of the Palatine where the she-wolf suckled Romulus and Remus under the Ruminal fig-tree. Ceremony: (1) Sacrifice of goats and a dog (unusual victims) and offering of sacred cakes (mola salsa) prepared by the Vestal Virgins from the first ears of the previous harvest. (2) Some Luperci smeared the foreheads of two young men of good family with the blood-stained sacrificial knife; others wiped the blood away with wool soaked in milk; at this the youths had to laugh. (3) Luperci cut goat skins into strips (thongs), girdled themselves; enjoyed an apparently rowdy feast. (4) Naked apart from their goat-skins, the Luperci in two groups ran wildly in a circuit from the Lupercal, striking with thongs all bystanders, especially women, who approached them. In Caesar's time the route may have extended up and down the Via Sacra.
- **Aition**: Livy and others alleged the cult was imported from Arcadia by Evander (citing comparison with Zeus Lycaeos in Arcadia); Scullard dismisses this as based on a false comparison. The two colleges were traditionally founded by Romulus and Remus respectively. A third college (Julii) was created in 45 BC in honour of Caesar, with Antony as head, but did not outlast his death.
- **Participants**: Luperci (two colleges: Quinctiales and Fabiani); Vestal Virgins (mola salsa); two young men of good family (possibly team-leaders) who received the blood-smearing; general crowd as bystanders/recipients of the thongs.
- **Page**: pp.76–78
- **Notes**: Last celebrated until suppressed in AD 494 by Pope Gelasius I, who converted it into the Feast of Purification of the Virgin Mary. Most scholars interpret the ceremony as fertility magic combined with purification and "beating the bounds" of the early Palatine settlement, but no single explanation commands agreement. Antony's offer of a crown to Caesar at the Lupercalia of 44 BC is the most famous historical incident associated with it.

## Quirinalia
- **Day**: 17 February (xm KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: NP. Dies religiosus.
- **Deity**: Quirinus
- **Ritual**: Festival on the Quirinal hill at the temple of Quirinus (vowed 325 BC, dedicated 293 BC). Also served as the last day of the Fornacalia, when all those who had not attended their curia's proper meeting could participate in a general assembly: this second function was known as the Stultorum Feriae (Feast of the Fools). Scullard notes little is known about the specific ritual of the Quirinalia itself; the festival may have been well attended in the late Republic given the importance of the Quirites identity.
- **Aition**: none for the festival per se. Quirinus himself was the god of the primitive Sabine settlement on the Quirinal, later identified with the deified Romulus.
- **Participants**: Roman citizens (as Quirites); those who missed their curia's Fornacalia meeting.
- **Page**: p.79
- **Notes**: Two myrtles stood in front of Quirinus's temple — one called patrician, one plebeian; after the Social War of 90–89 BC the patrician myrtle was said to have withered and the plebeian to have flourished. In 45 BC the Senate erected a statue to Caesar and "the unconquered god" (Quirinus) in the temple. The flamen Quirinalis ranked third among the flamines maiores; he also served at the festivals of Consus and Robigo.

## Terminalia
- **Day**: 23 February (vii KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Terminus (god of boundaries)
- **Ritual**: Private rite at boundary stones: each landowner garlanded his side of the shared stone and they built an altar together. The farmer's wife brought fire from the hearth; the old man chopped wood and prepared a bonfire. A young son cast corn from a basket three times into the fire; a daughter offered honeycomb; others offered wine. The assembled company (dressed in white, silently: linguis candida turba favet) watched. The stone was sprinkled with the blood of a lamb or sucking pig (Plutarch says the sacrifice was originally bloodless). A feast followed with songs in praise of holy Terminus. Public rite: an annual sheep sacrifice at the sixth milestone from Rome on the Via Laurentina, which had marked the boundary between early Romans and the Laurentes.
- **Aition**: Scullard states the festival was traditionally established by Numa. The stone on the Capitol: when Tarquin built Jupiter's new temple the other gods willingly vacated their shrines, but Terminus refused to move; his shrine was therefore preserved within the cella of Jupiter with an opening in the roof (Terminus required sacrifices under open sky). An early law ascribed to Numa forbade removal of boundary stones: the violator became accursed (sacer).
- **Participants**: Landowners and their families (private); pontiffs / public authorities for the Via Laurentina sacrifice. Trajan and Hadrian replaced boundary stones on the road to Ostia and in Campania.
- **Page**: pp.80–81
- **Notes**: Animistic origin: stones were once regarded as dwelling-places of vague spirits (numina); the rite helped promote neighbourliness. The termini sacrificiales go back to very primitive times.

## Regifugium
- **Day**: 24 February (vi KAL. MART.) — but if an intercalary month followed, the Regifugium fell on its proper date (sixth day before Kalends of March) within the intercalary month, not on 24 February itself.
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Unclear; Rex Sacrorum officiated. Salii participated in the sacrifice. Macrobius links it to Di Manes / Amburbium context. Silvius notes "Cum Tarquinius Superbus fertur ab urbe expulsus".
- **Ritual**: The Rex Sacrorum offered a sacrifice at the comitium and then fled from the Forum as fast as he could (per Plutarch). The Salii participated in the sacrifice. Romans of the Republic interpreted this as a commemoration of the expulsion of Tarquin and the founding of the Republic — a kind of Independence Day — but Scullard emphasizes this was not the original meaning. The calendars carry the notation Q.R.C.F. (Quando Rex Comitiavit Fas) on 24 March and 24 May, meaning legal business became permissible after the Rex Sacrorum performed a ceremony in the comitium; the Praenestine calendar notes that Q.R.C.F. was often wrongly read as "Quod Rex Comitio Fugerit" ("that the king fled from the comitium").
- **Aition**: Republican tradition: flight of Tarquin. Actual origin obscure. Frazer suggested the rex was a temporary mock-king for the intercalary period, whose power was then symbolically terminated. Comparison with the Athenian Bouphonia (where the officiating priest fled after sacrificing an ox to Zeus) and the Poplifugia on 5 July. The sacrifice may have been felt as a kind of murder, the victim being somehow sacred.
- **Participants**: Rex Sacrorum (officiated and fled); Salii (participated in sacrifice).
- **Page**: pp.81–82
- **Notes**: Its connection with the end of the old Roman year (23 February) and possible intercalation gives it calendrical significance. Scullard notes: "its real significance escapes us."

## Equirria
- **Day**: 27 February (in KAL. MART.)
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Mars
- **Ritual**: Festival of horse-racing held in the Campus Martius (or on the Caelian hill if the Campus was flooded). Whether chariots were involved is uncertain. The site in the Campus Martius may be the Trigarium on its western side. A second Equirria fell on 14 March; Scullard notes we do not know why two races were held so close together. The purpose was to prepare for the campaigning season and to exercise horses after winter rest.
- **Aition**: Traditionally instituted by Romulus, according to Scullard.
- **Participants**: not stated (racers, horses, spectators implied).
- **Page**: p.82
- **Notes**: Mars was thus honoured twice before his own month began. The 14 March Equirria is discussed in the March chapter.

## Amburbium (Feriae Conceptivae)
- **Day**: moveable; sometime in February. Macrobius (1.13.3) places it in February: 'King Numa ordered the city to be lustrated in February and sacrifice offered to the Di Manes.'
- **Fasti status**: Feriae Conceptivae (moveable)
- **Deity**: Di Manes (per Macrobius); purification rite involving the city's boundary (pomerium), so also implicitly related to Terminus and protective deities.
- **Ritual**: Probably a pig, sheep and ox were led in procession around the boundaries of the city (pomerium) and then sacrificed with prayers to purify all within the hallowed circle ('the victim went round the city': urbem circuit et ambit victima, Servius). The ceremony involved pontiffs, Vestal Virgins, College of Fifteen (Quindecimviri), augurs, Salii, and a flamen, all dressed in the Gabinian style (toga arranged to free the arms). An ox was sacrificed; its entrails were inspected. Scullard notes that recorded instances of its celebration tend to be crisis moments: 49 BC (Caesar marching on Rome) and AD 271 (time of national danger, when the Sibylline Books were consulted on 11 January).
- **Aition**: none given; Scullard connects it with Numa and compares it with the Ambarvalia (May) and the Umbrian Amburbium at Iguvium documented on the Iguvine Tablets.
- **Participants**: Pontiffs; Vestal Virgins; College of Fifteen; augurs; Salii; flamen; citizens arranged in priestly and military ranks.
- **Page**: pp.82–84
- **Notes**: Scullard devotes considerable space to the Iguvine Tablets as parallel evidence for the spirit of early Roman amburbial rites. The Roman ceremony may have become somewhat irregular. The three sacrificial animals (suovetaurilia: pig, sheep, ox) and the circuit of the city are the defining features.
