# September — Scullard (1981)
**PDF pages used**: 182–192
**Source**: H.H. Scullard, *Festivals and Ceremonies of the Roman Republic* (Thames & Hudson, 1981)

**Chapter note**: Scullard describes September as "a month of Games rather than of the commemoration of individual deities." None of the festivals are marked in large letters (i.e., no major feriae publicae in the old sense). About half the month was devoted to the Ludi Romani. The rustic Menologia place September under the protection of Vulcan and record an epulum in honour of Minerva. Scullard argues September was effectively Jupiter's month.

---

## Ludi Romani (Ludi Magni)
- **Day**: 5–19 (by the late Republic; originally anchored on the 13th)
- **Fasti status**: Not stated as a unit; the central day (13 Sep) is NP
- **Deity**: Jupiter Optimus Maximus
- **Ritual**: The oldest and most famous Roman Games. Originally votive Games linked to triumphal processions culminating at Jupiter's Capitoline temple (dedicated 13 September 509 BC). Annual by at least 366 BC. Organized by the curule aediles. Opened with a solemn procession (pompa circensis) from the Capitol through the Forum to the Circus Maximus: magistrates, cavalry, charioteers, athletes (naked save for loin-cloths), dancers in scarlet tunics with bronze helmets and swords, men impersonating satyrs and Sileni, flute and lyre players, incense-burners, men carrying gold and silver sacred vessels, and finally images (fercula) of the twelve Olympians plus Saturn, Ops, Themis, Latona, the Parcae, Mnemosyne, Muses, Graces, and heroized mortals (Hercules, Aesculapius, Castor, Pollux). Sacrificial animals closed the procession. The consuls presided; priests purified oxen with water and mola salsa before slaughter. Events included four-horse and two-horse chariot races, single-horse racing (desultores leaping between two horses attested from 169 BC), running, boxing, wrestling; theatrical performances (ludi scaenici) were also part of the programme. A sixteenth day was added in Caesar's honour before his death. After the Games (20–23 Sep), market days (Mercatus) were held.
- **Aition**: Originally votive Games tied to triumphal processions; they became annual by 366 BC. No specific founding myth given.
- **Participants**: Curule aediles (organizers); consuls (presiding); all citizens as spectators; upper-class young men participated in the Lusus Troiae equestrian tournament on occasion; professional chariot drivers (often slaves), organized in factions (Reds, Whites, and probably Greens and Blues).
- **Page**: pp. 183–186

## Dedicatio: Iovis Tonanti et Iovis Liberi / Iunoni Reginae in Aventino
- **Day**: 1 (Kalendae Septembres)
- **Fasti status**: F
- **Deity**: Jupiter Tonans; Jupiter Liber; Juno Regina
- **Ritual**: Temple anniversary dedications. Jupiter Tonans on the Capitol: dedicated by Augustus in 22 BC. Jupiter Liber: see 13 April; restored and rededicated by Augustus on 1 September. Juno Regina on the Aventine: originally built in 392 BC to house the wooden cult statue evoked from Veii by Camillus before the siege of 396 BC (ritual of evocatio); it lay near the later church of S. Sabina and was restored by Augustus.
- **Aition**: Camillus vowed a temple to Juno Regina of Veii by the rite of evocatio in order to persuade the goddess to abandon Veii before its capture; after his victory he built the Aventine temple in 392 BC and placed there the statue brought from Veii (p. 183).
- **Participants**: Not stated; presumably the pontifices and the relevant temple priests.
- **Page**: p. 183

---

## Dedicatio: Iovis Statoris
- **Day**: 5 (Nonae Septembres)
- **Fasti status**: F
- **Deity**: Jupiter Stator
- **Ritual**: Temple anniversary. Jupiter Stator had received a first temple in 294 BC (see 27 June). A second temple was dedicated in 146 BC by Q. Caecilius Metellus Macedonicus, located near the Circus Flaminius, beneath the later church of S. Maria in Campitelli. These two temples by Metellus (the other to Juno Regina) were probably the first all-marble temples in Rome. Lysippus's bronze equestrian statues of Alexander's generals were displayed in front of the temple.
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Not stated.
- **Page**: p. 183

## Epulum Iovis (Iovis, Iunoni, Minervae)
- **Day**: 13 (Idus Septembres)
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Jupiter Optimus Maximus; Juno; Minerva
- **Ritual**: On the traditional dedication-day of the Capitoline temple (509 BC), Jupiter was honoured on the Capitol with a public feast (epulum). At the feast, images of the three Capitoline deities presided dressed for a banquet: Jupiter's face was reddened with minium and he reclined on a lectulus; Juno and Minerva were seated on sellae. Tables of food were set before them; music was probably played. The Vestal Virgins' second batch of mola salsa (prepared earlier in the year) was used for the sacrifice; the first had been offered at the Vestalia, the third reserved for the Lupercalia. Magistrates and senators attended. A college of three epulones, created in 196 BC (grown to ten by the late Republic), organized the banquets. The epulum served as the religious climax of the Ludi Romani. Also on this day a nail was driven annually into a wall of the Capitoline temple by the praetor maximus — a practice apparently discontinued by the third century BC; its purpose is debated (recording years passed, or magical/apotropaic function).
- **Aition**: The feast was linked to the temple dedication of 509 BC. Its precise origin is uncertain: Scullard discusses whether it predated the Ludi Romani or was originally tied to the Ludi Plebeii of 13 November, concluding cautiously that "a feast of Jupiter was not necessarily a feature only of the Plebeian Games, but may have been held on 13 September from early times" (p. 186).
- **Participants**: Magistrates; senators; the college of epulones (organizers); Vestal Virgins (preparation of mola salsa); pontifices.
- **Page**: pp. 186–187

## Equorum Probatio
- **Day**: 14
- **Fasti status**: F Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: None named
- **Ritual**: A cavalry review (equorum probatio). Scullard notes this is analogous to the cavalry parade of 15 July (the transvectio equitum), but cautions that "since this entry for 14 September does not appear in the Republican Fasti Antiates maiores, the references in the imperial calendars may apply only to the Empire."
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Roman cavalry (equites).
- **Page**: p. 188

## Mercatus
- **Day**: 20–23
- **Fasti status**: Not stated
- **Deity**: None
- **Ritual**: Market days or trade fairs set aside after the Ludi Romani, analogous to the Mercatus following the Apolline Games of 13 July.
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: General public, merchants.
- **Page**: p. 188

## Dedicatio: Apollini et Latonae ad Theatrum Marcelli
- **Day**: 23
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Apollo; Latona
- **Ritual**: Temple anniversary. The original temple of Apollo was dedicated in 431 BC outside the pomerium in the Campus Martius (an extra-pomerial foreign cult). Its dedication day is uncertain (possibly 13 July; see p. 164). It was restored on 23 September (the birthday of Augustus) by C. Sosius (consul 32 BC, governor of Syria), who enriched it with famous works of art. It became known as the temple of Apollo Sosianus, adjacent to the temple of Jupiter Stator; three of its columns were re-erected in 1940. Its podium lies beneath the cloisters of S. Maria in Campitelli.
- **Aition**: Not stated.
- **Participants**: Not stated.
- **Page**: p. 188

---

## Dedicatio: Veneri Genetrici in Foro Caesaris
- **Day**: 26
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Venus Genetrix
- **Ritual**: Temple anniversary. Julius Caesar vowed a temple to Venus Genetrix — traditional ancestress of the Julian gens — at the battle of Pharsalus (48 BC). The temple, built of solid marble in his new forum, was dedicated on 26 September 46 BC, the last day of his triumph. It contained a statue of the goddess, two famous paintings, a gilded statue of Cleopatra, a collection of engraved gems, and a breastplate decorated with British pearls.
- **Aition**: Caesar's vow at Pharsalus is the explicit founding occasion (p. 188).
- **Participants**: Not stated; presumably the pontifices and Julian family.
- **Page**: p. 188

## Summary

Scullard's September chapter covers eight distinct entries:

| Day | Name | Fasti | Deity |
|-----|------|-------|-------|
| 1 | Dedicatio Iunoni Reginae / Iovis Tonanti / Iovis Liberi | F | Jupiter (×2), Juno Regina |
| 5 | Dedicatio Iovis Statoris | F | Jupiter Stator |
| 5–19 | Ludi Romani | (NP on 13th) | Jupiter Optimus Maximus |
| 13 | Epulum Iovis / Iunonis / Minervae | NP | Jupiter, Juno, Minerva |
| 14 | Equorum Probatio | F (dies religiosus) | — |
| 20–23 | Mercatus | — | — |
| 23 | Dedicatio Apollini et Latonae | C | Apollo, Latona |
| 26 | Dedicatio Veneri Genetrici | C | Venus Genetrix |

Key themes: September is dominated by the Ludi Romani (5–19 Sep), centred on Jupiter Optimus Maximus and reaching its religious peak in the Epulum Iovis on the 13th (Ides). The remaining entries are temple dedication anniversaries (three Augustan restorations). The Equorum Probatio on the 14th may be imperial-era only. No entry in September carries the large-letter designation of a major public festival in the older sense; the Ludi Romani themselves are the sole major observance.
