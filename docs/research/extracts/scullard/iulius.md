## Kalendae Quintiles — Iunoni / Felicitati
- **Day**: 1
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Juno (Kalends), Felicitas
- **Ritual**: The Kalends of each month were sacred to Juno. The Antiate maiores also record Felicitati; the Antiate minores specify "Felicitati in Capitolio". The cult of Juno on this Kalends is not recorded elsewhere. The temple of Felicitas may be that of Fausta Felicitas on the Capitol.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 158–159

## Poplifugia
- **Day**: 5
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Jupiter (Feriae Iovi)
- **Ritual**: The only festival written in large letters in the calendars that fell before the Nones of the month. The rite is wholly obscure; later Romans invented two explanations — either the people fled when Romulus disappeared during a tempest, or the flight referred to a rout by the people of Fidenae after the Gallic sack. Varro says traces (vestigia) of the rite appear in sacrifices he described in his (lost) Antiquities. By the late Republic the festival may have been merely formal maintenance of an old cult.
- **Aition**: Two competing ancient explanations: (1) flight when Romulus vanished in a storm (mythological); (2) flight from the people of Fidenae after the Gallic sack (historical). Neither is accepted as genuine by Scullard.
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 159

## Ludi Apollinares
- **Day**: 6–13 (festival day proper: 13 July; games extended backward to 6 July by end of Republic)
- **Fasti status**: 13 July = C (chief day); game days not individually stated
- **Deity**: Apollo; also Latona, Diana (sacrifice)
- **Ritual**: Instituted during the Hannibalic War on instruction of the Sibylline Books and the oracles of the seer Marcius; made permanent in 208 BC after a plague. Originally one day (13 July), extended to eight days (6–13) by the end of the Republic — two days for Circus races, the rest for stage plays. Sacrifices according to Greek rite: an ox for Apollo, a cow for Latona, two white she-goats probably for Diana (gilded horns). People wore garlands; matrons offered prayers; everyone feasted in the forecourt with open doors. Horse-racing in the Circus Maximus; plays in theatres. The praetor urbanus presided with a monetary grant and two full-grown victims; the decemviri sacris faciundis oversaw the sacrifices. Beast-hunts (venatio) also held; no gladiatorial combats.
- **Aition**: Prescribed by Sibylline Books and seer Marcius during the Hannibalic War; made annual and permanent after a plague in 208 BC (healing and military aid from Apollo).
- **Participants**: Praetor urbanus (presiding), decemviri sacris faciundis, people (garlands), matrons (prayers)
- **Page**: p. 159–160, 164

## Fortunae Muliebri (Via Latina)
- **Day**: 6
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Fortuna Muliebris
- **Ritual**: Temple dedicated on 6 July (vow made 1 December — recorded only in literary sources, not in the calendars). Located approximately four miles outside Rome on the Via Latina. The cult was confined to univirae (women married only once); widows and women who married twice were barred from touching or garlanding the statue. Only newly married women or univirae paid worship. A second statue, contributed by women at their own expense, was said to have spoken on two occasions.
- **Aition**: Linked to the story of Coriolanus — the Senate honoured the women who accompanied the wife and mother of Coriolanus and dissuaded him from attacking Rome, granting them this temple to Fortuna Muliebris (per Dionysius of Halicarnassus, following "the books of the pontiffs").
- **Participants**: Univirae (women married once only)
- **Page**: p. 160–161

## Nonae Caprotinae / Ancillarum Feriae
- **Day**: 7
- **Fasti status**: N
- **Deity**: Juno Caprotina
- **Ritual**: Feast of Serving Women (ancillarum feriae), recorded in Silvius' late calendar. Booths of fig-tree boughs erected outside the city. Romans ran out calling common names (Gaius, Marcius, Lucius). Ancillae dressed in matrons' clothing, feasted by the men, then held a mock battle among themselves. Women (both ancillae and free women — liberae) offered sacrifice to Juno Caprotina under a wild fig-tree; milky juice of the fig-tree was offered to Juno in place of milk. A branch (stick) from the tree was cut, possibly used in the ancillae's mock battle — perhaps a fertility rite (cf. Lupercalia). An agricultural dimension is possible: the caprification of cultivated fig-trees (placing wild figs among cultivated ones to encourage pollination) was best done in July.
- **Aition**: After the Gallic sack of Rome, the Latins demanded Roman women. A serving maid named Philotis (or Tulola) advised sending ancillae dressed as free women; at night the ancillae disarmed the sleeping Latins and Philotis gave the signal to attack by lighting a fire from a wild fig-tree (caprificus). The Romans attacked successfully and later established this commemorative festival. (Plutarch also records an alternative tradition linking the day to the death / disappearance of Romulus at the Caprae Palus, but the calendars show Poplifugia and Nonae Caprotinae fell on different days.)
- **Participants**: Ancillae (serving women) and free women (liberae); Romans generally
- **Page**: p. 161–163

## Consus in Circo
- **Day**: 7
- **Fasti status**: not stated
- **Deity**: Consus
- **Ritual**: Sacrifice at the underground altar of Consus at the first turning-point (meta) in the Circus Maximus, performed by the State-priests (sacerdotibus publicis). The altar was kept covered and exposed only at the festival. (A second sacrifice on 20 August was performed by the flamen Quirinalis and Vestal Virgins.) Consus was a god of the store-bin of harvested grain kept underground; his name most probably derives from condere ("to store/hide") rather than consilium.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: State-priests (sacerdotibus publicis)
- **Page**: p. 163

## Vitulatio
- **Day**: 8
- **Fasti status**: N Dies religiosus
- **Deity**: Vitula
- **Ritual**: Sacrifices for the goddess Vitula, apparently an official ceremony attended by the pontiffs. The occasion was linked to a tradition of a Roman victory on 8 July (the day after the Poplifugia flight). The name Vitula was variously explained as deriving from victoria (victory), from vitulari ("to sing a paean" / voce laetari), as the goddess of joy, or from vita ("life"), since she received first fruits of the earth. The possibility that vitulus ("heifer") was the original meaning — as used as a scapegoat at Iguvium — was apparently not considered by ancient scholars.
- **Aition**: After the Romans had fled before the Etruscans on 5 July (Poplifugia), they won a victory on the following day (8 July) and established sacrifices for Vitula (per Macrobius 3.2.11ff.).
- **Participants**: Pontiffs
- **Page**: p. 163–164

## Apollo — dies natalis templi (chief day of Ludi Apollinares)
- **Day**: 13
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Apollo
- **Ritual**: Sacrifice to Apollo recorded by the Antiates maiores alone. This was the oldest temple of Apollo, dedicated in 431 BC on account of a plague; it lay in the Campus Martius between the Circus Flaminius and the Forum Holitorium (outside the pomerium, as a foreign cult). It was later identified as the temple restored by Sosius (consul 32 BC), known as Apollo Medicus; it received a porticus in 179 BC and contained famous works of art. Used by the Senate for extra-pomerial meetings.
- **Aition**: Temple dedicated in 431 BC on account of a plague (healing function of Apollo).
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 164

## Mercatus
- **Day**: 14–19
- **Fasti status**: not stated
- **Deity**: none
- **Ritual**: Six days of markets or fairs set aside after the Apolline Games. Business could be combined with pleasure; crowds from the countryside who had come for the festival could buy and sell. Similar mercatus are marked in the calendars after the Ludi Romani and Plebeii.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: General public, country visitors
- **Page**: p. 164

## Equitum Romanorum Transvectio
- **Day**: 15
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Castor and Pollux
- **Ritual**: Cavalry parade through Rome. Equites equo publico rode in tribes and centuries, crowned with olive branches, wearing purple robes with scarlet stripes (trabeae). Procession began from a temple of Mars outside the walls, passed through parts of the city and the Forum, and went by the temple of Castor and Pollux, sometimes numbering up to 5,000 men wearing military decorations. Established by censor Q. Fabius Rullianus in 304 BC; lapsed in mid-Republic; revived by Augustus as a probatio / recognitio of the knights. Separate from (though sometimes combined with) the censorial review of equites (probatio).
- **Aition**: Established to celebrate the help received at the battle of Lake Regillus from Castor and Pollux, who were believed to have watered their horses in the Roman Forum after the victory.
- **Participants**: Roman equites (equo publico); originally organised by the censor
- **Page**: p. 164–165

## Honori (et Virtuti)
- **Day**: 17
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Honos (and Virtus)
- **Ritual**: Temple dedication day for the temple of Honos et Virtus just outside the Porta Capena. The first part was built by Q. Fabius Cunctator in 233 BC after his Ligurian campaign, dedicated to Honos. M. Claudius Marcellus later vowed a temple to Honos and Virtus (222 BC), renewed after the capture of Syracuse (211 BC), but the pontiffs forbade a joint rededication; the old temple of Honos was restored and a new section for Virtus dedicated by his son in 205 BC. Honos and Virtus were regarded essentially as military qualities. The dedication of 17 July most probably refers to Fabius Cunctator's original temple of Honos. There was also a temple of Honos outside the Colline Gate and one to Honos et Virtus built by Marius.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 165–166

## Victoriae in Capitolio
- **Day**: 17
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Victoria
- **Ritual**: Sacrifice (unknown which statue/temple). A gold statue of Victory, sent by Hiero of Syracuse to encourage the Romans after Trasimene, was placed in the temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitol. A Victory driving a biga was in the vestibule. It is not known whether the sacrifice on 17 July was to one or the other.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated

## Alliensis Dies
- **Day**: 18
- **Fasti status**: C Dies religiosus (dies ater / black day)
- **Deity**: none
- **Ritual**: A dies ater (black day) commemorating two Roman military disasters: the defeat of the Fabii on the Cremera (479 BC) and the Roman defeat at the Allia by the Gauls (390 BC), which led to the capture of the city. Recorded in the Antiate maiores as "Alliensis dies"; in Antiate minores as "Dies Alliae et Fabiorum". Cicero (ad Att. 9.5.2) confirms it was an ill-omened day (dies religiosus). No ritual action is described; it was a day of ill omen marked in the calendar.
- **Aition**: Defeat of the Fabii on the Cremera (479 BC) and especially the Roman defeat at the river Allia by the Gauls (390 BC) — the latter led to the sack of Rome.
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 166

## Lucaria
- **Day**: 19 and 21 (two days)
- **Fasti status**: NP (both days)
- **Deity**: not stated (grove spirits / vague forest powers implied)
- **Ritual**: Festival held in a large grove (lucus) between the Via Salaria and the Tiber (probably in the area of the Pincian Gardens). Only the site and the dates are securely known. Probably goes back to primitive Rome when land was reclaimed from the forest: the grove spirits (lurking in the trees) had to be propitiated before trees were felled. Cato preserves a suitable prayer with pig sacrifice when a grove was thinned. Revenue for public festivals (lucar) may have derived from public groves (luci — Plutarch). The legendary explanation — that the Romans hid in the grove fleeing the Gauls after the battle of Allia (18 July) — is a secondary invention.
- **Aition**: Legendary explanation: Romans fleeing after the Allia fled into this grove (Festus). Scullard's preferred interpretation: ancient rite of propitiation when forest land was cleared, with no specific historical aition.
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 166–167

## Ludi Victoriae Caesaris
- **Day**: 20–30
- **Fasti status**: not stated
- **Deity**: Venus Genetrix (also Victoria)
- **Ritual**: Games vowed by Julius Caesar at Pharsalus; a temple to Venus Genetrix was dedicated in his Forum on 26 September 46 BC. From 45 BC onwards the Games were held 20–30 July. Comprised seven days of scenic events followed by four days of Circus celebrations. After 46 BC, more than half of July was consumed by Games (eight days for Apollo, eleven for Caesar).
- **Aition**: Vow made by Julius Caesar at the battle of Pharsalus (48 BC) to Venus Genetrix.
- **Participants**: General public
- **Page**: p. 167–168

## Concordiae
- **Day**: 22
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Concordia
- **Ritual**: Temple dedication day (dies natalis) for a temple of Concordia in the Forum at the foot of the Capitol. Surviving remains belong to a restoration by Tiberius (7 BC). The early history is uncertain: Camillus is said to have vowed a temple to Concordia in 367 BC to celebrate the end of the patrician–plebeian struggle; in 121 BC L. Opimius was ordered by the Senate to build (or restore) it after the death of Gaius Gracchus. Almost certainly the temple that had its birthday on 22 July. Concordia was first attested as a goddess in Rome when Cn. Flavius dedicated a bronze shrine to her in 304 BC.
- **Aition**: Camillus vowed a temple to Concordia in 367 BC to celebrate the concordia ordinum (ending of the struggle between patricians and plebeians), per Ovid and Plutarch.
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 168

## Neptunalia
- **Day**: 23
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Neptune
- **Ritual**: Arbours (umbrae) of leaves were made, presumably to protect worshippers from the summer heat. Purpose may have been to secure the water-supply at this dry time of year. An altar of Neptune in the Circus Flaminius (first mentioned 206 BC) was probably dedicated on 23 July. Neptune was one of only three gods (with Apollo and Mars) to whom a bull might be sacrificed. Neptune was probably originally an Italian god of water whose domain was extended to the sea when equated with Poseidon; first mentioned in Rome at the lectisternium of 399 BC, paired with Mercury.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 168–169

---

## Furrinalia
- **Day**: 25
- **Fasti status**: NP
- **Deity**: Furrina
- **Ritual**: A state holiday (feriae publicae) for the goddess Furrina, who was given an annual sacrifice and her own special priest (flamen Furrinalis). By Varro's day her nature was almost entirely forgotten: "honour was paid to her among the ancients… but her name is barely known, and even that to only a few." She may have presided over a spring or wells. Later (probably incorrectly) confused with the Furiae (Furies). Her sacred grove (lucus Furinae) on the Janiculum was where Gaius Gracchus met his death.
- **Aition**: not stated
- **Participants**: Flamen Furrinalis; State
- **Page**: p. 169

---

## Fortunae Huiusque Diei in Campo
- **Day**: 30
- **Fasti status**: C
- **Deity**: Fortuna Huiusque Diei ("Fortune of This Day")
- **Ritual**: A temple to "The Fortune of this Day" was vowed by Q. Lutatius Catulus on the day of the battle of Vercellae (30 July 101 BC) and dedicated by him on the anniversary of the battle. Located in the Campus Martius; possibly to be identified with Temple B in the Largo Argentina. L. Aemilius Paullus also built a shrine to this goddess on account of his victory at Pydna, probably on the Palatine (where there was a vicus Huiusque Diei). Both generals dedicated statues by Pheidias in their respective temples.
- **Aition**: Vowed by Q. Lutatius Catulus on the day of the battle of Vercellae (30 July 101 BC) and dedicated on its anniversary.
- **Participants**: not stated
- **Page**: p. 169

## Summary

Scullard covers eighteen distinct entries for July (some days carry multiple entries). The month's dominant religious feature is the **Ludi Apollinares** (originally 13 July, extended to 6–13 July by the end of the Republic), which Scullard treats as the most significant festival for ordinary Romans. From 46 BC onwards, the **Ludi Victoriae Caesaris** (20–30 July) consumed more than a third of the month in games, making July heavily a month of public entertainment.

The older festivals — **Poplifugia** (5 July), **Nonae Caprotinae** (7 July), **Vitulatio** (8 July), **Lucaria** (19 and 21 July), **Neptunalia** (23 July), **Furrinalia** (25 July) — are treated by Scullard as genuinely archaic rites whose original meaning was unclear even to ancient scholars. Most had lost popular significance by the late Republic.

Notable calendar features for July:
- **18 July** is a dies ater (Alliensis dies) — no ritual, purely ill-omened.
- **15 July** (Equitum transvectio) was a spectacular military parade revived by Augustus.
- The month had no major festivals in NP status in the second half beyond the Lucaria (19, 21) and Neptunalia (23) and Furrinalia (25).
- Scullard notes the absence of Ovid's Fasti for July and beyond significantly limits surviving poetic commentary on these festivals.
